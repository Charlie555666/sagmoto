SAGMOTO i5 in the Sri Lanka Market: Electric Light Truck Feasibility for Colombo Logistics

2026-10-14Market Analysis10 min read

Sri Lanka's distribution geography is unusually kind to electric light trucks

Sri Lanka is a small island with a concentrated demand pattern, and that combination matters more for electric truck feasibility than any single specification number. Roughly 40 percent of national GDP is generated within the Western Province, and the Colombo metropolitan area plus its port handles the overwhelming majority of containerised and break-bulk import volume. A fleet operating out of Colombo rarely needs to stray beyond a 150 km radius for its core delivery work, and the two dominant inter-city corridors it does serve, Colombo to Kandy and Colombo to Galle, are both well within the practical range of a battery-electric light truck.

This is the reason the SAGMOTO new energy electric trucks line deserves serious attention from Ceylon logistics operators rather than being dismissed as an experiment. The SAGMOTO i5 is a light-duty battery-electric truck built around a roughly 98 kWh lithium iron phosphate, or LFP, battery pack. LFP chemistry is the correct choice for the Sri Lankan context for three reasons: it tolerates high ambient temperature better than nickel-based cells, it is resistant to thermal runaway, and it offers a long cycle life that supports the high daily kilometre counts typical of urban delivery. For a light truck doing multiple short trips per day from a Colombo depot, the battery does not need to be enormous; it needs to be durable and cheap to replace.

The commercial question for a Sri Lankan fleet manager is therefore not whether an electric light truck can physically do the work. The i5 clearly can, on the routes most operators actually run. The question is whether the total cost of ownership, after the island's specific electricity tariffs, import duties and tropical climate are accounted for, beats a comparable diesel light truck over a five-year holding period. This analysis works through that calculation.

Electricity cost and charging infrastructure in and around Colombo

The decisive variable for any electric vehicle business case is the price of the kilowatt-hour it consumes, and in Sri Lanka that price is set by the Ceylon Electricity Board, or CEA, under a tiered tariff structure. For commercial and industrial consumers, the relevant rates are the general-purpose and industrial tariffs, which in recent years have sat in an indicative band of LKR 35 to LKR 55 per kWh including the fixed and demand charges that apply to three-phase commercial connections, with the precise figure depending on sanctioned load, time of use and consumption band. For a fleet with its own depot and a three-phase supply, off-peak and night charging can be arranged to sit at the lower end of that band.

What the tariff means in fuel-cost terms

At an indicative delivered electricity cost of LKR 40 per kWh, the i5's roughly 98 kWh usable pack costs about LKR 3,920 for a full charge. A realistic urban delivery consumption figure for a 4.5 to 6 tonne gross vehicle weight, or GVW, electric light truck in stop-start Colombo traffic is in the region of 35 to 45 kWh per 100 km, which places a full charge's energy cost at roughly LKR 0.88 to LKR 1.13 per kilometre. Against a diesel light truck returning 9 to 11 km per litre on the same duty and diesel priced in the LKR 290 to LKR 320 per litre range at the pump, the diesel energy cost is roughly LKR 2.6 to LKR 3.5 per kilometre. The energy cost gap in favour of electricity is therefore in the order of 60 to 70 percent per kilometre before any maintenance advantage is counted.

Depot versus public charging

Colombo and its suburbs have seen a steady build-out of public AC and DC charging points, but for fleet economics the right model is depot charging overnight on a dedicated three-phase connection. A 22 kW AC wallbox will replenish the i5 pack from empty to full in roughly five to six hours, which fits a single overnight shift comfortably, while a 60 kW DC charger can recover 80 percent in well under two hours for fleets that need a midday top-up on a double-shift operation. The capital cost of a depot charger, cabling and a three-phase upgrade is modest relative to the fuel saving it unlocks, and it converts a recurring diesel expense into a fixed, predictable electricity line that can be hedged through time-of-use scheduling.

Key point: On indicative Colombo commercial tariffs, the i5's energy cost per kilometre is roughly 60 to 70 percent below that of a diesel light truck on the same stop-start duty, before any maintenance saving is counted.

Duty, tax and the import environment for EV light trucks

Sri Lanka's import regime has historically been heavily weighted toward revenue collection through taxes on vehicles, and the treatment of electric commercial vehicles is a moving target that any serious buyer must confirm at the point of order. Commercial battery-electric goods vehicles have, in recent policy windows, benefited from reduced or waived import duties and luxury taxes relative to their internal-combustion equivalents, as part of a broader national push toward electrification and reduced fuel imports. A useful way to think about the import environment is to separate the acquisition premium from the operating saving. A diesel light truck of comparable GVW may land at a lower upfront cost because its drivetrain is cheaper to build, but the electric unit's lower duty treatment in supportive policy periods narrows that gap, and on some GVW bands the electric vehicle lands at parity or below. The buyer should obtain a binding landed-cost quotation that includes CIF Colombo, applicable duty and VAT, port handling, and registration, and should treat any policy announcement as valid only for the fiscal year in which it is gazetted.

One further consideration is the availability of after-sales support. Battery-electric vehicles have far fewer wearing parts than diesel units, but they still require a service capability for brakes, tyres, suspension, high-voltage isolation testing and battery management system diagnostics. Importers should confirm that the supply channel provides the high-voltage service training, the diagnostic tooling and the spare-cell or pack warranty pathway before committing a fleet.

Tropical climate: humidity, monsoon and battery management

Sri Lanka's climate is classed as tropical monsoon, with high year-round humidity, a south-west monsoon from May to September and a north-east monsoon from December to February. For battery-electric vehicles the two climate risks are cabin and battery thermal management during the hot, humid months, and the effect of sustained high temperature on long-term cell degradation. LFP chemistry, as fitted to the i5, is comparatively forgiving on both fronts, but the operating plan still matters.

Battery warranty and degradation expectations

A realistic warranty and degradation assumption for a commercial LFP pack in tropical service is a capacity retention guarantee of 70 to 80 percent over a defined cycle or mileage window, with the pack expected to retain the majority of its capacity across 2,000 to 3,000 equivalent full cycles in moderate climate operation. In Sri Lanka's heat and humidity, degradation will sit at the higher end of that range unless the battery thermal management is functioning correctly, which argues for specifying the truck with active cooling rather than passive air cooling, and for avoiding routine 100 percent fast-charging in the hottest part of the day. A fleet that charges primarily overnight on AC power, keeps the state of charge between 20 and 90 percent for daily duty, and reserves DC fast charging for exceptions will see the best calendar-life result.

Operators should also account for the monsoon. Heavy rain and flooded low-lying roads in Colombo during the south-west monsoon are not a battery hazard provided the pack and connectors meet the relevant ingress-protection rating, which production electric trucks do, but they are a traction and braking hazard, and they argue for tyres with good wet grip and for driver training on reduced visibility and aquaplaning. The electric drivetrain itself, with its low centre of gravity from a floor-mounted pack, actually improves stability in crosswinds on the coastal Colombo-Galle corridor.

Key point: Keep the i5 battery between 20 and 90 percent state of charge for daily duty and charge overnight on AC power; in tropical service this protects LFP calendar life far more than any marginal saving from 100 percent fast charging.

Route profiles: Colombo to Kandy and Colombo to Galle

Two corridors define most inter-city light-truck work out of Colombo, and both are well within i5 capability, though they differ in character. The Colombo to Kandy run is approximately 115 km and climbs from sea level to around 500 m, with sustained grades on the final approach through the central hills. A loaded i5 will consume more on the uphill leg to Kandy than on the flat return, and a fleet should plan the Kandy trip as a single out-and-back within one charge rather than chaining multiple hill trips, because repeated hill climbing draws the pack down faster and recovers little on descent in urban conditions.

The Colombo to Galle corridor is roughly 115 to 130 km along the coastal expressway and is essentially flat, making it the easiest electric duty on the island. A round trip of 240 to 260 km sits comfortably inside the i5's usable range even with the air conditioning load of a tropical day and a partial payload, and many operators will complete a Galle round trip with range to spare for a Colombo distribution loop afterward. For purely urban Colombo delivery, where daily distances are 80 to 140 km with heavy stop-start, the i5 can operate a full day on a single overnight charge with no public charging required.

Route planning should therefore be organised around depot-charged daily radius rather than range anxiety. The i5 is not a long-haul vehicle and should not be specified as one; it is a metro-and-short-corridor workhorse, and on that definition of duty it is a strong commercial proposition for the Sri Lankan fleet that understands its own kilometre profile.

Conclusion

Sri Lanka's geography, grid and policy environment combine to make the SAGMOTO i5 one of the more defensible electric light-truck deployments in the emerging Asian market. The island's compact, Colombo-centric demand pattern keeps most light-truck work inside the i5's 98 kWh LFP range; the CEA commercial tariff makes the per-kilometre energy cost roughly 60 to 70 percent below diesel; and supportive EV import-duty windows close much of the acquisition gap that would otherwise slow adoption.

The honest caveats are climate and policy. Tropical heat and humidity push LFP degradation toward the higher end of its range, which is manageable with overnight AC charging, sensible state-of-charge discipline and active pack cooling, but it is not negligible. And the import duty treatment is a moving target that must be confirmed against the current gazette, not assumed from a prior year. Within those two constraints, the five-year total cost of ownership and the sub-eighteen-month payback on typical Colombo duty make the i5 a credible, bankable choice for fleet buyers who run metro and short-corridor routes.

For Sri Lankan operators, the practical next step is to meter an existing diesel light truck for thirty days, capture its true energy spend and annual kilometres, and model the i5 against that measured baseline with a confirmed landed cost. A business case built on real meter data beats a generic comparison every time, and it is the only one that should reach a finance committee.

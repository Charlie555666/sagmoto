E3 vs X9s Tractor Comparison: Value Long Haul or Premium Regional

2026-11-10Comparison Guide8 min read

Within the SAGMOTO tractor line the E3 and the X9s sit next to each other but answer two different briefs. The E3 is a 340 to 400 horsepower value oriented long haul tractor built around the Yuchai YC6MK engine, while the X9s is a 400 to 460 horsepower medium heavy regional unit on Weichai WP10 or WP12 power. Both pull trailers, both wear the same badge, yet putting the wrong one in the wrong role quietly bleeds margin from a fleet. This comparison is an internal positioning exercise: not which truck is better, but which truck belongs on which lane, and what the numbers say when you run them side by side across a five year life.

Two Tractors, Two Briefs

The E3 is the workhorse. Its lower power band and lean specification keep purchase price and running cost down, which is exactly what a high mileage, low margin line haul operation wants. The X9s is the premium regional player: more horsepower, a heavier chassis and the refinement that matters when a tractor is in and out of yards, climbing shorter steeper grades and covering varied duty in a single shift. Read the two as a deliberate pair, not as a cheaper and a dearer version of the same idea. Fleet operators comparing the wider range should also review the SAGMOTO tractor trucks prime mover line up before fixing the split between the two models.

The positioning question is rarely about ability and almost always about duty profile. A fleet that runs steady 40 tonne loads down a single corridor for a thousand kilometres a day should weight toward the E3. A fleet that mixes 30 tonne regional drops, yard transfers and the occasional heavy pull should weight toward the X9s, because the extra horsepower and the stronger chassis earn their keep across the varied day even if they sit idle on the easy legs.

A simple way to split a fleet is to score each lane by length and weight, then assign the E3 to every lane that clears both thresholds and the X9s to the rest. A fleet running eighty percent long light lanes and twenty percent short heavy lanes might still buy only enough X9s to cover the twenty percent, and that focused deployment is where the pairing saves the most money. Buying X9s for the long lanes just because they are newer wastes the horsepower, while buying E3s for the short heavy lanes just because they are cheaper wrecks the uptime both models were meant to protect.

Power Band and Where Each Earns Its Keep

The E3 Yuchai YC6MK produces usable power across a calm, economical rev range that suits long steady cruising, where the engine spends its life near torque peak and rarely asks for a downshift. The X9s Weichai gives a broader, higher ceiling that matters when the road turns up or the load turns heavy, and when the duty is stop start rather than endless highway. Neither engine is wrong; they are tuned to different rhythms. Specifying the E3 on a steep, varied regional route leaves the driver downshifting where the X9s would simply hold gear, and specifying the X9s on flat line haul spends horsepower and fuel the route never uses.

Practical rule: if your dominant lane is above six hundred kilometres and below 42 tonnes, the E3 power band is the efficient choice. If your dominant lane is below four hundred kilometres, above 42 tonnes, or varies hour to hour, the X9s power band pays for itself in driver confidence and fewer gear changes, which on a regional route is a real productivity number, not a comfort one.

Positioning Rule

Send the E3 to steady long haul below 42 tonnes and the X9s to varied regional work above that line, because duty profile not raw ability decides which model earns its keep.

Driveline and Component Differences

Both tractors use the Fast twelve speed transmission family, but the X9s backs it with a heavier rear axle and stronger driveline components sized for the higher torque and the more abusive regional cycle. The E3 driveline is lighter and cheaper, which is correct for its gentler life, but it is not the unit to specify for constant heavy start stop work. Suspension follows the same logic: the E3 favours the simpler leaf or lighter air set up for line haul, while the X9s offers the air suspended tractor spec that protects fragile regional cargo and the driver who lives in the cab all day.

Braking and safety are shared across both, with dual circuit air brakes, ABS and ESC standard, and the retarder optional on either. The difference is that the X9s more often needs the retarder because regional corridors hide short steep descents between towns, whereas long haul routes are planned to avoid them. When you position the two models, treat the retarder as a default on the X9s and an option on the E3, and cost it accordingly in the comparison below.

The service interval story also differs. The E3, working steady miles, reaches long service intervals and low consumable use, which is a quiet contributor to its low cost per kilometre. The X9s, working harder and stopping often, needs more frequent brake and clutch attention, yet its stronger components mean each intervention is less likely to become a roadside failure. Position the X9s where its durability prevents a breakdown that would cost more than the extra maintenance, and the E3 where the gentle duty makes its lean spec the efficient one.

Fuel per 100 km: Real Numbers

Fuel is where positioning shows up in the monthly account. On a representative 40 tonne load, the E3 returns roughly 28 to 31 litres per 100 kilometres on steady highway running, because its lower power band and lighter driveline are tuned for exactly this. The X9s on the same 40 tonne load returns roughly 30 to 33 litres per 100 kilometres, a small penalty for the extra horsepower and mass. But shift the X9s to its natural varied regional duty at 45 tonnes with more stops, and its figure widens to 32 to 35 litres, while the E3 asked to do that same varied duty would fall to worse than 34 litres because it is working outside its design envelope.

The lesson is that the on paper fuel gap reverses once you put each truck on the lane it was built for. The E3 is the frugal choice only on the lane that suits it; on the wrong lane it drinks more than the X9s. Position by duty and the fleet fuel number looks after itself.

Quantify it for your own fleet: at 120,000 kilometres a year, the two litre per hundred kilometre gap between the E3 on its lane and the X9s on its lane is roughly two thousand four hundred litres of diesel annually, which at a representative fuel price is a five figure sum per truck. Spread that across ten tractors and the positioning decision is worth a six figure annual fuel bill, which is why the comparison deserves a spreadsheet and not a guess.

Purchase Price and Residual Value

The E3 enters at a lower purchase price, which is its headline advantage and the reason it dominates pure cost per kilometre calculations on steady work. The X9s costs more up front, reflecting the stronger driveline, the heavier chassis and the higher power ceiling. Residual value tells the second half of the story: the X9s tends to hold a higher percentage of its value at five years because its power band stays relevant as fleets shift toward heavier regional loads, while the E3 depreciates faster in absolute terms but from a lower base, so the gap in resale dollars is narrower than the gap in list price suggests.

Driver Preference and Retention

One variable the spec table never shows is the driver. Regional drivers who live in the cab through a varied day notice the X9s air suspension, the stronger cab and the easier gear changes, and they stay. Long haul drivers on a steady lane are happier with the E3's simplicity and fuel bonus. Specifying the right truck for the right driver reduces turnover, and in a tight driver market that retention is a hard financial edge that sits beside the fuel and residual numbers rather than beneath them.

Which One Belongs in Your Fleet

The answer is rarely one or the other across a whole fleet. Most mixed operations should run both, with the E3 taking the predictable long lanes where its fuel and price advantages compound over high mileage, and the X9s taking the variable regional lanes where its power and chassis protect uptime. Buy on the lane, not on the brochure, and the two models stop being competitors and start being a complete tractor strategy. For operations whose duty escapes both envelopes, the heavier Z3 tractor truck 520HP Cummins M13 covers the top of the range, but for the vast middle the E3 and X9s pair is the positioning that pays.

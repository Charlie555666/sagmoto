SAGMOTO X6s 6x4 for Sawn Timber and Log Transport Operations

2026-11-16Industry Application9 min read

Study the load diagram of a typical X6s 6x4 turning out of a forest road and the numbers tell you immediately where this truck earns its keep. A bolster trailer carrying 18 tonnes of sawn timber puts roughly 6.2 tonnes on the front axle, 5.9 tonnes on each drive axle and nothing on a third axle that is not there, for a gross vehicle weight near 24 tonnes against a 25 tonne design limit. Shift that load two metres back and the front axle drops below 5 tonnes while the tandem climbs past its rating, the steering goes light and the legal axle limit is breached. Timber transport is an exercise in managing a long, dense load on a medium duty chassis, and the X6s succeeds when the body, the securing and the axle distribution are specified together rather than in isolation.

Reading the load before specifying the truck

Timber is an awkward cargo because its density varies and its length dictates the body. Green sawn timber can weigh 700 to 900 kg per cubic metre, while dry boards may be nearer 450 to 600 kg, so the same stacked volume can differ in mass by half. A 6x4 X6s rated around 25 tonnes gross vehicle weight and powered by the Cummins ISD in the 180 to 240 horsepower band will typically carry 12 to 18 tonnes of timber depending on body and route. The buyer who specifies purely on volume will be overweight before the stack looks full.

The second variable is length. Logs and sawn boards of 4 to 6 metres overhang the cab and require a bolster trailer or a long stake body with adequate rear support. That overhang moves the centre of gravity aft and unloads the front axle, which is why the load diagram matters more than the headline payload. Every timber operation should begin with a weighed sample load and a measured axle weighbridge ticket, not with an assumed payload from a brochure. Those two numbers drive every decision that follows.

Bolster trailer versus stake body

Two body styles dominate. A bolster trailer, also called a logging bogie or pole trailer, uses upright bunks on a low frame to carry long logs, with the load strapped down between the bunks. It suits whole-tree or long-log work where the timber is delivered to a sawmill or a loading bay, and it keeps the weight low and central. The X6s 6x4 pulls such a trailer well on formed forest roads, and the articulation helps the combination follow uneven ground without twisting the frame.

A stake body, by contrast, is a flat deck with vertical stakes fitted to the truck itself, used mainly for sawn timber, palletised boards and short logs. It gives a tighter turning circle than a trailer and is easier to reverse into a mill yard, which makes it the better choice for operations with short, repetitive routes and tight delivery points. The trade-off is that a stake body carries the load high and forward, raising the centre of gravity and putting more mass on the drive axles. Fleets that move both products often run a mixed fleet rather than asking one body to do both jobs badly.

Key point: a bolster trailer carries long timber low and central, while a stake body suits sawn boards and short logs on tight routes. Choose the body from the timber length and the mill yard geometry, not from purchase price alone.

Load securing and why it fails

Timber loads shift because the stack settles as the journey vibrates, slackening straps that were tight at the mill. On an X6s the standard practice is a chain or webbing strap over each bolster or stake pair, tensioned with a ratchet and checked again after the first 20 km. For sawn timber, friction between boards is low, so both ends of every stack should be restrained against forward and rearward movement. Loose timber is not merely a cargo damage issue; a log that slides forward into the cab or sideways off the deck is a fatal hazard.

Securing equipment is cheap relative to the load it protects. A set of four rated straps and ratchets costs 200 to 350 USD and should be inspected every month for frayed webbing and bent ratchets, then replaced at the first sign of damage. Chains last longer on sharp-edged logs but require tensioners and edge protectors to avoid crushing the timber. The time cost is real, around twenty minutes to secure and check a full load, and crews who skip it are betting a 15,000 USD truck and a 12,000 USD cargo against those twenty minutes.

Axle load distribution and the weighbridge

Axle distribution is the single most responsible variable in timber work. The ideal 6x4 setup carries the load so the front axle stays above roughly 5 tonnes for adequate steering traction and the drive tandem stays under its rating, commonly around 18 tonnes combined on a medium duty chassis. Because timber is loaded from the rear of a bolster trailer, it is easy to finish with too little weight on the steering axle, producing a truck that wanders on wet forest roads and cannot hold a line on a descent.

The fix is disciplined loading: place the heaviest logs forward over the kingpin or the front bunks, distribute the rest evenly, and weigh the loaded combination before it leaves the forest on the first few runs of each season. Once the pattern is known, loaders can reproduce it by eye, but the weighbridge check should be repeated whenever the timber species, moisture or log length changes. An X6s that is consistently overloaded on the drive tandem will fail springs, wheel bearings and tyres early, and no amount of engine power compensates for a chassis working beyond its rating.

Forest road traction and the 4x4 option

Formed forest roads with a compacted gravel surface suit the 6x4 X6s well, especially when the drive axles are carrying their correct load. The Cummins ISD engine has usable low-speed torque and the 10-speed gearbox lets the driver keep the engine in its pull range on steep, slippery climbs. Differential locks on the drive axles are valuable on mud and loose gravel, and should be engaged before a wheel spins rather than after the truck has stopped. Matching tyres across the drive tandem is essential, because mismatched rolling radii make the differential work continuously and overheat.

For operations that must leave the formed road, the X6s is also offered in a 4x4 configuration. The front drive axle transforms traction on wet clay, steep access tracks and unbridged creek crossings, and it gives a loaded timber truck the ability to climb out of a soft verge that would defeat a 6x4. The penalties are higher purchase price, roughly 8 to 12 percent more fuel, added driveline weight that reduces payload, and more maintenance on the front axle and transfer case. Specify 4x4 only when the route genuinely demands it; for highway and formed-road haulage the 6x4 is the more economical tool.

Specifying for the duty cycle

A profitable timber specification starts with the route. Highway and formed-road work with 6 metre logs favours the 6x4 with a bolster trailer, which keeps weight low and fuel burn reasonable. Short mill-yard movements of sawn timber favour the stake body for its manoeuvrability. Steep, wet, unformed tracks justify the 4x4 despite its running costs. In each case the engine should be chosen with the loaded grade in mind rather than the empty truck, because a 180 horsepower unit that feels adequate unladen will struggle at 24 tonnes on a wet incline.

Service intervals also shorten under timber duty. Dust from forest roads contaminates the intake and oil, brake linings wear quickly on long descents, and load securing points and stake pockets take constant hammering. Plan engine oil changes at 15,000 km rather than the general duty figure, inspect spring packs and U-bolts monthly, and check the frame and body mounts for cracks at every service. Fleets that treat forest work as severe duty keep their X6s timber trucks earning for a decade; those that treat it as normal haulage rebuild the chassis early.

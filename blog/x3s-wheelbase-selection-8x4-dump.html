SAGMOTO X3s 8x4 Wheelbase Options: Choosing the Right Frame Length

2026-11-19Product Deep Dive9 min read

On an 8x4 dump truck the wheelbase is not a detail; it is the decision that fixes the frame length, the body length, the turning circle and the way weight is shared between the axles. The SAGMOTO X3s 8x4 is a serious machine, rated at 31 tonnes GVW on a frame of 300 x 80 x (8+5) mm, with a Cummins ISME or Weichai WP10/WP12 engine in the 280 to 460 horsepower range and a Fast 12JSD200T twelve-speed gearbox. Choose the wheelbase badly and a truck that should be productive becomes slow to turn, unstable on a ramp or unable to carry the body it was sold with.

This guide is about making that choice deliberately. The X3s tipper is used on quarry, construction and municipal contracts where the truck spends its life at high gross weight on broken ground, and the wheelbase must balance three competing demands: a short frame for tight site access, a long enough bed to carry a full load at practical density, and an axle spread that keeps the front axle light enough to steer when the body is heaped. There is no single correct answer, but there is a correct answer for your site, and it can be found with a handful of measurements and some simple arithmetic.

Key point: Select the wheelbase from the site, not the brochure. Measure the tightest turn and the steepest loaded ramp the truck must use, then choose the shortest frame that still carries your body and keeps the front axle within its rating.

What wheelbase actually controls

Wheelbase is the distance between the front axle and the centre of the rear bogie. On an 8x4 it governs the lever arm that lets a heavy body tip the load rearward, the space available for the bed and the radius the truck needs to turn. A longer wheelbase gives a longer, more stable platform and better rearward weight distribution when the body is level, but it demands more road space and increases the turning circle. A shorter wheelbase improves access and manoeuvrability but concentrates weight on the front axle and limits body length.

The frame itself matters as much as the dimension. The X3s uses a 300 x 80 x (8+5) mm section, a layered beam designed to carry high bending loads without excessive flex. A longer frame carries a longer body but also carries more of its own weight, and it flexes more between the axles under a heaped load. That is why buyers should think of wheelbase as part of a system with the frame, the suspension and the body, not as an isolated number on a specification sheet. Change one and the others must be rechecked.

Notice the direction of the front-axle share. As the frame lengthens, the body sits further behind the front axle and the front wheels carry less of the load. That helps steering at full gross weight but can make the truck light at the front when it runs empty, reducing traction on a wet or sandy approach. Fleets that run loaded one way and empty back often prefer the medium wheelbase because it behaves predictably in both states. A short-wheelbase truck that is fine when heaped can feel nervous and skittish on the return leg.

Matching wheelbase to the tipper body

Body length and material density decide how much of the 31 tonne GVW can be used productively. Dense material such as crushed rock reaches the weight limit in a short body; light material such as sand or spoil needs a longer bed to reach a useful payload. Because the body cannot be longer than the frame will sensibly support, wheelbase and material must be chosen together. Weight distribution and stability

A tipper spends its working life close to gross weight, so axle loads deserve careful attention. The front axle on an 8x4 X3s has a finite rating, and a heaped body on a short frame can push it over the limit even when the total weight is legal. That overload shows up as heavy steering, rapid tyre wear on the front pair and poor braking balance. The remedy is either a longer wheelbase, a shorter body or a different load distribution, and the decision should be made before the body is built.

Stability is the other side of the same coin. A long, high-sided body on a short wheelbase has a high centre of gravity and a short wheelbase, a combination that is uncomfortable on a cross slope or a sharp turn at a tip. A longer frame lowers the effective tip-over risk by spreading the load, but it demands a wider, flatter site to manoeuvre. The 940 mm sleeper cab on the X3s also adds mass behind the front axle on crew-spec trucks, so a fleet that specifies the sleeper for long shifts should recheck front-axle loading at full body. These interactions are why a paper specification is never enough.

Key point: Check axle loads in two conditions, fully heaped and empty. A wheelbase that balances the truck when loaded can leave the front axle too light on the return, costing traction and braking stability.

Reading the rating plate and body approval

Before a body is ordered, the chassis rating plate should be read and understood. It states the gross vehicle weight, the front and rear axle ratings and the permitted towing capacity, and those figures, not the engine output, set the legal payload. A tipper body supplier will design to a target volume, but the final tare weight must be checked against the plate once the body is mounted. A body that looks right on paper can push an axle over its rating when it is fitted with a heavier hoist or thicker floor plate than the quotation assumed.

Body approval and inspection are also part of the decision in many markets. A locally built tipper may need to be certified to a national standard before it can be registered, and a frame that has been extended or shortened by a third party can complicate that process. Buyers who plan to fit the body in their own country should confirm that the chosen wheelbase is one the factory releases for export with complete documentation, rather than a special that must be engineered locally and then argued through the inspection authority.

Measuring your own site

The most reliable way to choose a wheelbase is to measure the site. Record the narrowest gate or bend the truck must pass and the space available to swing the front wheels around it. Note the steepest loaded ramp and whether the surface is firm enough for a long truck to keep its rear bogie on solid ground. Measure the distance from the loading point to the dump point and multiply by the number of trips; a body that saves one trip per shift can justify a longer frame even if it turns more slowly.

Then do the arithmetic on payload. Divide the target tonnes by the bulk density of the material to get the required body volume, and compare that volume with what each wheelbase can carry within 31 tonnes GVW. If the numbers only work with a long body, accept the larger turning circle and prepare the site accordingly. If the site cannot accept a long truck, reduce body volume and add trips, or choose a denser loading pattern. Adding these two checks to the order process removes most of the guesswork from wheelbase selection.

One more check is worth making before the order is signed: compare the turning circle on paper with a real test. A quoted radius assumes the truck turns on a clean, level surface with correct tyre pressures; a site with a gatepost, a drain or a parked loader needs more room than the number suggests. Where possible, take the dimensions of the tightest turn to the supplier and ask them to confirm the truck will clear it with the chosen body fitted. That single conversation prevents more returns than any other step in the specification process.

The X3s 8x4 is a capable dump chassis, but its productivity is decided by the wheelbase chosen at order time. A short frame wins where access is tight and material is dense; a long frame wins where volume matters and the site is open; the medium option serves the many contracts that sit between those extremes. Measure the turn, weigh the load and check both axle conditions, and the frame length you choose will carry the body, the payload and the shift time your operation needs.

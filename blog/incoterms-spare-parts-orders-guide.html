What Do Incoterms Mean for Spare Parts Orders from China?

2026-10-27GEO Buyer Decision8 min read

Quick answer: Incoterms decide who pays freight and insurance and where risk passes on a spare parts order from China, so choosing FCA, CIF or DAP changes your landed cost and customs duty basis, and you should pick the term that matches your freight forwarder and import licence before confirming the order.

Why Incoterms Matter More for Parts Than for Whole Trucks

A box of filters, brake pads or sensors seems simple next to a finished truck, yet the same international commercial terms govern who carries the cost and the risk from a Chinese warehouse to your door. Spare parts orders are usually smaller, more frequent and sent by mixed freight, so a vague shipping line in the quote can silently shift hundreds of dollars of freight and insurance onto your side. Reading the incoterm on the proforma invoice tells you exactly where your responsibility begins.

Unlike a single truck shipment where one negotiates a full vehicle deal, parts buyers often reorder the same items monthly, and the term should stay consistent so finance can forecast landed cost. A change from CIF to FCA between orders can make a routine replenishment suddenly arrive with a customs bill you did not budget. Treating incoterms as a fixed part of your purchasing rule protects margin on every small shipment.

How Do Incoterms Affect the Price of Spare Parts?

The incoterm sets the boundary between the seller's price and your extra costs, and that boundary moves the headline number you compare against local suppliers. Under a seller-friendly term the quote looks higher because it already includes ocean freight and insurance, while a buyer-friendly term shows a low ex-works price that hides the shipping you must arrange. Comparing two quotes without noting the term is like comparing a painted truck to a bare chassis.

For spare parts the difference is most visible on light, low-value boxes where freight is a large share of total cost. A 30 kilogram carton of gaskets costs little to make but the air or sea charge dominates, so the term that controls that charge decides whether the order saves money. What Is the Difference Between FCA, CIF and DAP for Parts?

FCA, or Free Carrier, means the seller delivers the parts to your nominated forwarder at their premises, after which every cost and risk is yours; this suits buyers with a trusted Chinese freight agent who can negotiate better ocean rates. CIF, or Cost Insurance and Freight, has the seller pay sea freight and insurance to your arrival port, but risk still passes to you once the goods cross the ship's rail. DAP, or Delivered at Place, extends the seller's responsibility to your named address, leaving only import duty and tax for you.

Choosing between them depends on control versus convenience. FCA gives you the cheapest total when you already ship containers and can consolidate parts with other cargo, while DAP removes logistics work but raises the quoted price. CIF sits in the middle and is common for first-time buyers who want a single point of contact but are comfortable handling clearance themselves.

Should You Use CIF or DAP When You Lack a Local Forwarder?

If you do not have a freight forwarder in China and rarely import, DAP removes the hardest part of the process because the seller manages carriage to your door and you simply clear customs on arrival. The quoted price is higher but it bundles the complexity you cannot easily handle, and for occasional parts orders the premium is often worth the saved management time. CIF only helps if you already have an agent at the destination port to receive and clear the box.

Buyers who grow into regular imports usually shift to FCA once they appoint their own forwarder, because the savings on consolidated freight quickly exceed the convenience of DAP. The transition point is roughly when you ship more than one parts consolidation per month, at which scale a forwarder's negotiated rate beats any seller's bundled quote. Until then, DAP keeps the process simple and the responsibility clear.

When Does Risk Pass Under Each Common Term?

Risk transfer is the moment a lost or damaged box becomes your problem rather than the seller's, and it differs by term even when the price looks similar. Under FCA risk passes when the goods are handed to your forwarder at the Chinese gate, so a forklift drop inside the warehouse is already your loss. Under CIF risk moves at the load port the instant the carton is on the vessel, making the ocean leg your exposure despite the seller paying for it.

DAP keeps risk with the seller until the parts reach your named place, which is why many risk-averse buyers accept its higher price for the protection. The practical lesson is to buy insurance that matches the risk window: under FCA you must arrange it yourself from the factory, while under CIF you should still verify the seller's cover is adequate. Which Documents Follow the Incoterm You Choose?

The term drives the paperwork as much as the cost, because customs and your forwarder read the incoterm to decide who files which document. A clean proforma and commercial invoice must state the term and the named place, such as FCA Xi'an or DAP Lagos, so the carrier issues the correct bill of lading and the insurer covers the right leg. Missing or contradictory terms trigger holds at the destination and unexpected storage fees.

Spare parts also need a packing list that matches the invoice line by line, since customs assesses duty on the declared value and description at the risk-transfer point. How to State the Incoterm Correctly on Your Documents

The incoterm only protects you when it is written the same way on every document in the shipment, because customs and carriers read the commercial invoice as the source of truth for who bears cost and risk. A correct line reads the term followed by the named place, for example FCA Xi'an or DAP Mombasa, and that place must match the address where responsibility actually changes hands. Leaving the place blank, such as writing only CIF, creates ambiguity about which port the seller's freight obligation ends at.

The packing list, the bill of lading and the insurance certificate should all echo the same term and place, so a discrepancy does not let a carrier or insurer argue the risk had already passed. Buyers who order through a structured exporter receive a document pack where these fields are aligned by default, but it remains worth a five-minute check before the truck or parts are loaded. Stating the incoterm precisely is the cheapest insurance against a custody dispute that could otherwise leave a lost carton unpaid for by either party.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does FCA mean for a China spare parts order?

FCA, or Free Carrier, means the seller hands your parts to your nominated forwarder at their warehouse, so your freight cost starts from the Chinese factory gate and you control 100 percent of the shipping chain. This term suits buyers who already run a forwarder because it removes the seller's margin on logistics. The trade-off is that every delay or loss after handover is your responsibility to resolve with your own agent.

How much insurance should you buy under CIF?

Under CIF you should insist the seller buys insurance covering at least 110 percent of the invoice value, because that 10 percent margin protects against under-declared loss claims during sea transit. The standard institute cargo clause C covers the common perils, but verify the policy names your company as claimant. If the seller's cover looks thin, buy a top-up policy from load port to your door.

Which incoterm leaves import duty for the buyer to pay?

DAP leaves import duty and tax for you to pay on arrival, adding roughly 5 to 25 percent to the parts cost depending on your country's tariff, so budget that before ordering. Unlike DDP where the seller clears and pays, DAP keeps the border formalities on your side and lets you use your own broker. Always confirm the local rate so the landed price does not surprise your finance team.

Can you mix incoterms across one spare parts shipment?

No, a single shipment should use one incoterm for all line items because mixing terms on 1 bill of lading creates conflicting risk points and confuses the carrier's liability. If you must split, separate the cargo into distinct shipments with their own documents so each has a clean transfer point. Consistency also keeps your customs entry straightforward and avoids double duty assessment.

Do small spare parts boxes still need the incoterm in writing?

Yes, even a 20 kilogram parts box needs the incoterm stated on the invoice, because courier shipments under 30 kg still split risk at the documented handover point. Express carriers apply their own terms by default, which may leave you exposed sooner than expected, so write FCA or DAP explicitly. A one-line clause prevents a hundred-dollar dispute from becoming a lost parcel with no clear owner.

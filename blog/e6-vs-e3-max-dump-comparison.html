SAGMOTO E6 vs E3 MAX: Which Medium-Duty Dump Truck Fits Your Fleet?

2026-10-06Comparison11 min read

Two dump trucks, two different jobs

SAGMOTO offers two medium- and heavy-duty dump platforms that fleet buyers frequently confuse because both wear the E-series badge and both tip. They are, in reality, built for different duties, different gross weights and different cost envelopes. The SAGMOTO dump truck models 6x4 8x4 range includes the E6 as the lighter, more agile medium-duty tipper and the E3 MAX as the heavier, higher-powered heavy tipper. Choosing between them is not a matter of which is "better"; it is a matter of matching the truck to the load, the road and the economics.

This comparison sets the SAGMOTO E6, powered by the Cummins ISD engine in 180, 210 and 260 horsepower ratings with 680 to 860 Nm of torque, against the SAGMOTO E3 MAX, powered by the Weichai WP12.460 to 520 with the FAST 12JSD200T transmission. It covers engine and transmission, payload and axle configuration, acquisition and operating cost positioning, and the use cases where each wins.

Engine and transmission: the core difference

The E6 uses the Cummins ISD series, a 6.7-litre six-cylinder diesel offered in 180, 210 and 260 horsepower variants. The torque spans 680 to 860 Nm depending on rating, which is ample for medium-duty tipping where the truck is legal at 18 to 31 tonnes gross and spends its life on shorter, lower-speed cycles. The ISD is a globally supported, mechanically conventional engine with deep parts availability through Cummins networks, which keeps both acquisition and lifecycle cost predictable.

The E3 MAX uses the Weichai WP12, a 12-litre six-cylinder diesel in the 460 to 520 horsepower band, paired with the FAST 12JSD200T twelve-speed manual. Its torque reaches roughly 2,400 Nm in the upper ratings. That is more than double the E6's maximum torque, and it places the E3 MAX in the heavy tipper class for 33 to 43 tonne gross work, long hauls to remote quarries, and duties where the truck must pull away under full load on a grade repeatedly through a shift.

Payload and axle configuration

Payload follows gross weight and body volume. The E6 in 6x4 configuration with a tipper body typically carries 10 to 16 tonnes of payload within legal limits, depending on the market's axle-weight law. It suits municipal waste and aggregate, building-site supply, and short construction-feed runs where manoeuvrability and lower tare weight matter more than raw capacity. The 4x2 E6 variant drops tare further for lighter urban duty.

The E3 MAX in 6x4 carries roughly 18 to 24 tonnes of payload, and the 8x4 variant extends to 26 to 32 tonnes where local law permits. That capacity is what makes it the quarry and mining-feed workhorse. The 8x4 configuration spreads the load across four axles for higher legal gross and better off-road stability on unmade haul roads. For fleets moving rock, ore, overburden or bulk aggregate over longer distances, the E3 MAX's capacity per trip is the economic argument.

Price positioning and total cost of ownership

The E6 sits in the lower acquisition band because its smaller engine, simpler transmission and lighter chassis cost less to build. The E3 MAX sits higher because the WP12, the twelve-speed box and the heavier axles cost more. But acquisition price is only half the story. The E6 returns lower fuel and maintenance cost per kilometre on the light duty it is built for, while the E3 MAX earns its higher price through higher payload per trip, which lowers cost per tonne moved on heavy duty.

The decision rule is cost per tonne delivered, not cost per truck. On a 12 km aggregate loop where the E6 carries 12 tonnes per trip and the E3 MAX carries 22 tonnes, the E3 MAX moves nearly double the material per cycle. Even at higher fuel burn per kilometre, its cost per tonne is lower because fixed and labour costs are spread over more payload. On a 4 km municipal loop where both trucks are legal at lower weights, the E6 wins because its lower tare and acquisition cost are not offset by any capacity advantage.

Key point: Decide by cost per tonne moved, not by sticker price. The E6 wins short light loops; the E3 MAX wins long heavy loops because its extra payload more than offsets its higher fuel and acquisition cost per trip.

Driver, maintenance and parts

Both trucks are mechanically conventional and diagnosable. The E6's Cummins ISD is supported through Cummins networks worldwide, which is a strong argument for fleets already running Cummins-powered equipment, because the parts overlap reduces inventory. The E3 MAX's Weichai WP12 and FAST 12JSD200T are the most widely supported heavy powertrain in the Chinese export market, with deep parts availability and workshop familiarity from Africa to Southeast Asia.

Maintenance labour differs mainly in scale: the E6 is lighter to service, with easier access and lower fluid volumes, while the E3 MAX's larger engine and driveline are heavier to work on but similarly conventional. Neither requires proprietary electronic tooling, which matters for fleets that maintain trucks in their own workshops rather than through franchised dealers. The shared chassis and brake components across the SAGMOTO dump range also simplify spares holding for mixed fleets running both models.

Which should your fleet buy

The answer splits cleanly by operation. Buy the E6 if your work is municipal, urban, suburban or short-haul aggregate where gross weights are in the 18 to 31 tonne range, manoeuvrability and lower tare matter, and you want the lowest acquisition and per-kilometre cost. Buy the E3 MAX if your work is quarry, mining feed, long aggregate haul or remote unmade-road duty where gross weights reach 33 to 43 tonnes and payload per trip drives the economics.

Many fleets reasonably run both: E6 units for the urban and short loops, E3 MAX units for the heavy and long loops. That split is the most common mature configuration we see among SAGMOTO dump customers who have grown past a single-type fleet. The trap to avoid is buying the E3 MAX for light duty and paying for capacity you never use, or buying the E6 for heavy duty and wearing it out prematurely under loads it was not built for.

Specification tips for each

For the E6, specify the 210 or 260 hp ISD for hilly or fully loaded duty and the 180 hp for light urban work; choose the 6x4 for capacity and the 4x2 for tare-sensitive urban runs; add the tropical and corrosion package for coastal and humid markets. For the E3 MAX, specify the WP12 rating to the gross weight (460 to 480 hp covers most 6x4 work, 520 hp suits 8x4 mining), pair it with the 12JSD200T, and choose hub-reduction rear axles for unmade or soft roads. Both should be ordered with the dust-pre-cleaner intake and up-rated cooling for dusty quarry and tropical environments.

Safety, braking and compliance

Dump trucks carry high centres of gravity and tip loads, which makes braking and stability specification a safety and insurance matter rather than an option list. Both the E6 and E3 MAX run full air braking with ABS as standard, and both should be ordered with engine or exhaust braking for the long descent work that quarry and remote haul roads demand. For the E3 MAX on unmade or steep haul roads, specify the hub-reduction axle and consider a retarder to protect the foundation brakes on sustained downgrades. Tipper body stability is also a bodywork question: specify the correct sub-frame, the correct tipping hinge geometry and, for loose or sticky loads, the correct body length and headboard height to avoid overload and roll-over.

Compliance varies by market, but the principle is constant: confirm the tare and gross plates, the axle-weight legality for the routes you actually run, and any local requirement for reversing alarms, blind-spot cameras and flashing beacons. A dump truck that is legal on paper but overweight on the routes it actually serves is a liability, not an asset, and the specification should be checked against the real duty rather than the brochure limit.

Financing, warranty and fleet standardisation

The E6 and E3 MAX are typically acquired against a letter of credit or a distributor finance plan, with powertrain warranty of 12 to 24 months or 100,000 to 150,000 km. For fleets scaling past a first handful of trucks, standardising on one model for one duty simplifies the parts inventory, the driver training and the workshop procedures, and it improves resale because dealers price predictable, identical fleets higher. Many mature SAGMOTO dump customers run a standardised E6 fleet for light duty and a standardised E3 MAX fleet for heavy duty, and the discipline of keeping those two fleets internally uniform is what keeps their total cost of ownership low.

Conclusion

The SAGMOTO E6 and E3 MAX are not competitors so much as complements. The E6, with its Cummins ISD 180 to 260 hp and 680 to 860 Nm, is the economical medium-duty tipper for municipal, urban and short-haul aggregate work. The E3 MAX, with its Weichai WP12.460 to 520 and FAST 12JSD200T, is the heavy tipper for quarry, mining and long-haul bulk duty where payload per trip decides the result. Match the truck to the gross weight and the duty cycle, decide by cost per tonne moved, and most fleets will end up running both in the right proportion.

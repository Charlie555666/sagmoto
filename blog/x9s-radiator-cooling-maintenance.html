SAGMOTO X9s Radiator Cooling Maintenance for Heavy Haul Fleets

2026-11-16Maintenance9 min read

A contractor in northern Chile lost an X9s tractor on a single afternoon climb. The unit was hauling 52 tonnes up a sustained grade in 38 degree ambient heat when coolant temperature climbed past 105 C, the derate map cut power, and the driver coasted to a stop with a steam cloud over the bonnet. The workshop found a radiator core blocked solid with mining dust and a fan clutch that had failed to lock, a combination no driver skill could offset. The tow, a new core at 780 USD and two days of lost haulage cost over 4,000 USD. None of it was sudden. The dust had built over 40,000 km and the clutch had weakened for months, both invisible until the gauge moved. Overheating in an X9s is rarely one dramatic failure; it is a chain of small, cheap faults that a disciplined cooling routine interrupts early.

Why the X9s runs a hot cooling load

The X9s is a heavy tractor in the 400 to 460 horsepower class, built around the Weichai WP10 and WP12 and rated for a gross combination weight of 40 to 60 tonnes. At full torque a WP12 rejects well over 200 kW to the coolant and charge air, and the radiator is sized to shed that heat at a 40 to 45 degree approach to ambient on a steady grade. Anything that reduces airflow or heat transfer removes margin, and the first symptom is temperature rising on the grade rather than on the flat.

Operators new to the model often misread this. A temperature that sits at 88 C on level ground and climbs to 103 C on a long pull is not a marginal radiator; it is a system with roughly ten percent of its capacity missing. A healthy unit holds temperature on the same grade in the same heat, with headroom to spare. The maintenance task is to preserve that headroom, not merely to stop the engine boiling. Every check below targets a specific source of lost capacity.

Diagnose in the right order, cheapest test first

When an X9s presents as hot, the temptation is to condemn the radiator because it is the most visible component. That is usually wrong and always expensive. Begin with a visual check of the grille and core face for dust, chaff and mud, because a blocked face is the cause in most heavy haul overheating cases and costs nothing to confirm. Shine a torch through the grille from the front: if daylight is not clearly visible through the core, airflow is the problem and cleaning comes before any parts are ordered.

Next, verify coolant level in the expansion tank when cold and check for the sweet smell of vapour at the cap, which points to a pressure fault rather than circulation. Then watch the fan: on a warm engine the clutch should engage with a distinct rise in fan noise and a drop in temperature within two minutes. If the fan free-wheels regardless of temperature, the clutch is the fault. Only after airflow, level and fan behaviour are confirmed should a mechanic suspect the thermostat, water pump or a scaled core.

Key point: a blocked radiator face is the most common cause of X9s overheating in dusty haulage and the cheapest to confirm. Hold a torch behind the grille; if you cannot see clear daylight through the core, clean it before ordering any parts.

Radiator core cleaning without damaging the fins

Dust cleaning is skilled work on a heavy truck because the aluminium fins are thin and collapse easily under a careless pressure washer. Work from the engine side outward, blowing compressed air at no more than 6 bar through the core so debris is pushed back the way it came. On the X9s remove the fan shroud first to reach the inner face, where oil and dust combine into a sticky film that air alone will not shift. That film is cleaned with a low-pressure spray and a soft brush, always moving from the inside out.

Never aim a high pressure lance at the fins from the front: it bends the aluminium flat against the tubes and permanently reduces airflow, turning a serviceable core into a failed one. A core bent across a wide area should be professionally rodded or replaced. In severe mining or quarry duty, plan a compressed air blow-through every 10,000 km and a full inner-face wash every 30,000 km. The labour is around 1.5 hours and the cost is a fraction of a replacement core.

Coolant chemistry, thermostat and fan clutch

The WP10 and WP12 need a heavy duty organic acid technology coolant meeting ASTM D3306 or the equivalent, mixed 50 percent with demineralised water. That mix protects to about minus 35 C and raises the boiling point under the 1.0 to 1.2 bar system pressure to near 120 C. Fleets in hot climates are tempted to run a weaker mix for better heat transfer, but the inhibitors and boiling point both fall, and water pump cavitation follows within a season. Check concentration with a refractometer at every service; it takes seconds and costs almost nothing.

Change coolant at 3 years or 240,000 km, and never top up with plain water except in an emergency. Drain, flush until the water runs clear, then refill slowly with the heater on to purge air pockets, which cause local hot spots no gauge will show. A thermostat stuck partly closed is a classic load-only overheat. Feel the upper and lower hoses when fully warm: if both stay cold as the gauge rises, the thermostat is not opening. Replacement costs 45 to 90 USD and about two hours, and should include a new gasket.

The fan clutch is the other frequent failure. A viscous unit should lock when temperature behind the radiator reaches roughly 88 to 92 C; a failed one either stays locked, costing fuel and power, or never locks, causing overheat on grades. Check for oil staining at the bearing and confirm engagement by noise and temperature drop. A replacement runs 260 to 520 USD with about 2.5 hours of labour. Ignoring a slipping clutch cooks the charge air cooler mounted ahead of it.

Hoses, the pressure cap and slow leaks

Rubber hoses work in a hot, vibrating environment and soften with age. Squeeze each one cold: a hose that feels spongy or bulges near a clamp is degraded internally and should be replaced. The lower radiator hose often collapses under suction at high rpm if its internal spring has weakened, giving an intermittent overheat that is hard to trace. A full set costs 180 to 320 USD and is worth fitting at 400,000 km or at the first sign of swelling.

The pressure cap is the most overlooked part in the system. A cap that no longer holds its rated pressure lowers the boiling point and allows slow loss that appears as a mysterious drop in the expansion tank. Caps cost 15 to 30 USD and should be pressure tested at every major service. A system that loses coolant without an external leak is usually a cap or a small head gasket weep, and a pressure test separates the two in under an hour. Left alone, that weep becomes a 2,500 USD cylinder head job.

Key point: a hot-running X9s announces itself early. Treat a temperature rise on a grade as the warning, log when it happens, and act the first time. For a fleet earning 600 to 900 USD a day, one avoided overheat pays for a year of coolant and inspection work.

The lesson from the Chilean contractor is not that the X9s cooling system is weak. It is that a tractor at the edge of its gross combination weight in hot, dusty country has very little thermal margin, and margin is what dust, weak coolant and a tired clutch remove. Keep the core face clean, the coolant at specification and the fan engaging on time, and the system holds temperature through a full contract. Ignore those three and the bill arrives on the worst possible day, far from the workshop and with a load that someone else is waiting for.

X5 Diesel vs i5 Electric Truck Operating Cost Comparison

2026-11-16Model Comparison10 min read

Numbers settle arguments that opinions cannot. A fleet manager looking at a SAGMOTO X5 diesel, a 200 to 380 hp medium truck with a Cummins ISD or Yuchai YC6J engine, and the battery-electric i5, a 4.5 to 5.5 tonne truck with a 90 to 120 kW motor and a 66 to 100 kWh LFP pack, is not really comparing two versions of the same vehicle. They are comparing two different cost structures. Diesel is cheap to buy and expensive to run; electric is expensive to buy and cheap to run. This article builds the bridge between them with hard numbers: cost per kilometre, the annual distance where electric breaks even, the payload you lose to the battery, the time cost of charging, and the duty cycles where each truck is the right answer.

Two machines, two cost philosophies

The X5 is a conventional 4x2 or 6x4 medium truck built for tipper and cargo work. Its strength is range, fast refuelling and a low purchase price, with a fuel burn in the region of 18 to 26 litres per 100 km depending on load and terrain. The i5 is a light electric truck sized for city distribution, with a 66 to 100 kWh LFP battery, roughly 200 km of real range and DC fast-charge capability. It has no engine oil, no gearbox oil, no exhaust aftertreatment and far fewer moving parts. Those structural differences drive everything below. Neither truck is universally better; the X5 wins where distance and payload dominate, and the i5 wins where stop-start mileage, low emissions and energy price dominate.

It is important to set expectations on class. The i5 is a 4.5 to 5.5 tonne light truck, while the X5 spans a heavier 4x2 and 6x4 band. They overlap only in the lighter end of the X5 range. A buyer comparing them should be comparing a specific light-duty job, such as urban delivery or municipal work, where either chassis could plausibly do the task. On heavy 6x4 construction work the comparison does not apply, and the X5 wins by default because the i5 is not built for that load.

Cost per kilometre, line by line

Assume diesel at USD 1.10 per litre and electricity at USD 0.12 per kWh, both realistic commercial rates. The X5 at 22 litres per 100 km costs about USD 0.24 per km in fuel. The i5 at 40 kWh per 100 km costs about USD 0.05 per km in energy. That is a gap of roughly USD 0.19 per km before maintenance. Add service: the X5 needs oil, filters and aftertreatment attention at roughly USD 0.07 per km, while the i5's scheduled maintenance is closer to USD 0.03 per km, mostly tyres, brakes and cabin filters. The combined running-cost advantage for the electric truck is about USD 0.23 per km.

At 60,000 km a year that is USD 13,800 saved in a single year, enough to recover the purchase premium in under twelve months of high utilisation. At 20,000 km a year it is only USD 4,600, and the premium takes more than two years to repay. This is the central truth of the comparison: the i5's economics are driven almost entirely by how many kilometres you cover, and a low-mileage fleet will not see the return. Be honest about annual distance before you electrify.

Break-even annual distance

Break-even is the annual mileage at which the i5's lower running cost repays its higher purchase price within a chosen payback period. Using the USD 9,000 average price premium and the USD 0.23 per km running saving, a one-year payback needs about 39,000 km per year. A two-year payback needs roughly 20,000 km per year. If you include a simple depot charger at USD 4,000 to 8,000 per vehicle, add around 25 percent to those thresholds. The table below shows the sensitivity so you can read your own situation off it.

Break-even rule: above 40,000 km per year the i5 normally pays for its premium inside twelve months on energy and service alone. Below 20,000 km per year, stay with the X5 unless low-emission access or a corporate mandate overrides pure cost.

The LFP payload penalty and charging downtime

A 66 to 100 kWh LFP pack is heavy, and on a 4.5 to 5.5 tonne chassis that mass is subtractive. Expect roughly 500 to 900 kg of battery weight, which comes straight off legal payload compared with a diesel truck of the same gross vehicle weight. If your operation carries dense loads such as sand, bricks or bottled goods up to the legal limit, the i5 may need an extra trip, and each extra trip erodes the per-kilometre saving. If you carry light, bulky cargo such as parcels, textiles or food packaging, the penalty is academic because you never reach the weight limit. Measure your typical load factor before you decide.

Charging downtime is the second operational cost. A diesel X5 refuels in ten minutes almost anywhere; an i5 needs one to two hours on a DC fast charger, and a full overnight charge on a slower point. For a truck doing three short urban routes a day and returning to depot at midday, that dwell time fits naturally into the schedule. For a truck that must cover 400 km a day with little slack, the i5's 200 km range and charging pause are disqualifying. Regenerative braking partly offsets urban energy use, recovering perhaps 10 to 15 percent of consumption in heavy stop-start traffic, which is another reason the i5 shines in cities and struggles on highways.

Battery health, warranty and resale value

The cost model above assumes the i5's pack holds its capacity, so battery longevity deserves its own scrutiny. LFP chemistry is among the most durable choices available, typically tolerating 2,000 to 3,500 full charge cycles before capacity falls to 80 percent of new. For a fleet covering 50,000 km a year at roughly 40 kWh per 100 km, that is about 20,000 kWh of throughput a year, well inside the pack's rated life over a normal five to eight year ownership period. Two habits protect it: avoid leaving the truck at 100 percent state of charge in hot weather, and prefer partial top-ups to deep discharges where the route allows. Ask your supplier for the written battery warranty in years and kilometres, and confirm whether it covers capacity retention or only outright failure, because the two are very different promises.

Resale is the other number buyers forget. A diesel X5 has a deep, liquid used market and a predictable depreciation curve. The i5's resale value is less certain and depends heavily on a verifiable battery health report and a local buyer base for electric trucks. Some markets offer strong incentives and ready buyers; others have almost none. When you build the five-year total cost, use a conservative residual for the i5 until your own market proves otherwise, and treat any incentive or low-emission-zone access as upside rather than a guaranteed saving.

Matching the truck to the duty cycle

The decision resolves into a clear pattern. Choose the i5 when your routes are under 200 km, return to a depot with charging, run mostly in urban or low-emission zones, and exceed 40,000 km a year. Typical winners are city distribution, municipal services, campus and port logistics, and refrigerated last-mile delivery where night access and quiet operation are valued. Choose the X5 when routes are long, payload is dense, refuelling must be fast, or annual distance is low. Construction supply, intercity cargo and any duty beyond 250 km from base belong to diesel for now. Buyers can compare the broader electric options on the SAGMOTO new-energy range and the diesel configurations on the X5 medium-duty truck page before fixing the specification.

To close, run the model with your own fuel contract, electricity tariff, route profile and payload data, and the answer usually becomes obvious. The i5 is not a cheaper X5; it is a different tool that wins on energy cost, maintenance and urban access but gives up range, payload and refuelling speed. The X5 remains the rational default for heavy, long or low-mileage work, while the i5 is the forward-looking choice for high-mileage urban fleets with charging at base. The buyer who matches the truck to the duty cycle, rather than to a slogan about electrification or diesel, is the one whose cost per kilometre will hold up over a five-year plan.

SAGMOTO X6 Gearbox Overhaul and Service Guide

2026-11-04Maintenance10 min read

The SAGMOTO X6 medium duty truck spans 210 to 245 hp from the Cummins ISD engine and carries the Fast 10JSD-family gearbox that faces its hardest life in stop-start distribution and on construction sites where the clutch and synchronisers work hardest. Protecting that transmission against premature overhaul protects the truck's second-largest replacement cost after the engine.

Oil grade and change intervals

The 10JSD transmission in the X6 runs on 85W-90 GL-4 gear oil rather than the GL-5 grade used in the rear axles, and mixing the two through a shared drum on the workshop floor is the most common cause of synchroniser hub scoring seen at 120,000 km. Drain and refill at 60,000 km under normal distribution duty, but cut that to 40,000 km for tippers working the clay pits around Bekasi where fine dust finds the breather and turns the oil to lapping compound. Check the level through the side fill plug with the truck level and the oil warm, because an overfill that climbs the main shaft froths and pushes past the input seal onto the clutch. The magnetic drain plug tells the story: a light steel smear is normal, but aluminium flakes mean the synchroniser rings are letting go and the next drain will show more. Carry a dedicated GL-4 drum coloured differently from the axle oil so a rushed night-shift fitter cannot confuse them.

Never share the GL-4 transmission drum with the GL-5 axle oil on the workshop floor, because the sulphur in GL-5 attacks the synchroniser hub coating and scores the cones by 120,000 km.

Synchroniser wear and shift quality

The 10JSD uses multi-cone synchronisers on the lower gears that wear the blocker-ring friction coating after roughly 250,000 km of urban delivery, and the first sign is a crunch going into second when the box is cold. Double-declutch the offending gear for a week and listen for whether the crunch fades as the oil warms, because a cold-only crunch is worn cones while an all-temperature crunch is a damaged selector fork. Pull the top cover at 200,000 km on high-cycle trucks and measure the ring-to-gear clearance with a feeler; anything past 0.6 mm means the ring is spent even if the shift still works. The X6 that spends its life on the SAGMOTO dump truck models 6x4 8x4 tipper circuit wears synchronisers faster than highway units because every load-and-dump cycle is a full downshift under load, so budget the ring set as a scheduled item rather than a breakdown repair.

Shift-linkage adjustment

The remote shift tower on the X6 uses a pair of linkage rods with spherical joints that stretch the rubber boots and develop play that the driver feels as a vague gate and a missed third-to-fourth change. Adjust the linkage with the cab tilted and the gearbox in neutral by setting the rod length to the stamped dimension and then confirming each gate engages without force, because a half-turn out of spec pushes the selector out of plane and bends the fork. Lubricate the ball joints with a molybdenum grease every 30,000 km and replace the boots at the first split, since grit in the joint is what rounds the selector fingers. Where the X6 runs a long-door cab and the linkage passes through the cab floor, check the rubber grommet for oil wick-up, because gearbox oil on the carpet is a blocked breather pushing past the selector shaft seal.

Main-shaft and counter-shaft bearing inspection

The counter-shaft in the 10JSD spins on a pair of taper-roller bearings that grow a low whine at 80 km/h as the preload relaxes, and that whine in neutral with the clutch out, disappearing when you press the pedal, is the textbook bearing symptom. Lift the driveline and rock the output flange to feel for radial play that exceeds 0.15 mm, then confirm with a dial gauge through the PTO opening because hand-feel alone misses the early stage. The main-shaft pilot bearing in the flywheel wears if the clutch is allowed to slip past 200,000 km, and a poor pilot lets the shaft nod and destroys the rear seal. Pull the extension housing at 300,000 km on severe-duty X6 units and measure the bearing tracks for pitting, because a pitted track is a guaranteed roadside failure within the next 40,000 km once it starts singing.

Air-assisted shift system checks

Higher-spec X6 builds use an air-assisted shift cylinder on the top cover that reduces lever effort for drivers doing 200 shifts a day on the Durban container circuit, and that system needs a clean air supply or the exhaust silencer clogs with compressor oil. Test the cylinder stroke by working through the gates with the engine off and the air reservoir charged, and watch for a lag where the lever returns slowly, which means the silencer is blocking the vent. Drain the shift-air filter bowl weekly in humid ports because water in the line freezes on the Highveld winter mornings and leaves the truck stuck in gear. The air line shares the cab supply, so a soft pedal and a lazy shift together point to a system-pressure drop rather than a gearbox fault, and charging the reservoir restores both.

Treat the shift-air silencer as a consumable on any X6 running an air-assisted box in humid or dusty ports, because a blocked vent leaves the driver fighting the lever and mashes the synchronisers within a season.

Clutch release bearing interaction

The clutch release bearing on the X6 rides on the input-shaft guide and fails early when drivers rest a foot on the pedal at traffic lights on the Lagos beltway, because that constant load overheats the carbon thrust face. A rattling bearing that quiets when the pedal is pressed is worn; replace it together with the clutch kit so the gearbox is not dropped twice. The hydraulic concentric slave on later X6 builds bleeds at the top cover bleed nipple with the cab raised, and air in that line gives a soft pedal that the driver compensates by stamping, which beats the synchronisers. Set the pedal free-play to the 30 mm spec and confirm the fork return spring seats, because a hanging fork drags the clutch and the box crunches every upshift until the bearing is changed.

PTO gearbox variants for tippers and cranes

The X6 offers a rear-mounted PTO on the 10JSD for tipper hoists and a front engine PTO for crane hydraulics, and the gearbox variant you specify decides whether the bodybuilder can mount a pump without a relator. The tipper PTO draws through a constant-mesh gear that handles a 60 L/min hoist pump on a six-cubic-metre body, while the crane variant needs the higher-torque front take-off because a knuckle-boom crane holds pressure while stationary and heats the pump. Confirm the PTO engagement is air or hydraulic and that the bodybuilder wires the interlock so the box cannot be in gear while the hoist rises, because a mismatch strands the truck at the body shop rather than the job site. Operators comparing the X6 with the heavier SAGMOTO X6s tipper variant should note the X6s carries the same 10JSD but with a reinforced PTO housing for continuous crane duty.

Failure symptoms and overhaul decision

A box that jumps out of fourth on overrun has a worn selector fork or a stretched detent spring, and that fault degrades fast once it starts, so schedule the teardown rather than nursing it. Noisy neutral with the clutch out is a counter-shaft bearing, while a hard shift across all gears with a good clutch is usually air in the slave or a bent linkage. Overhaul the gearbox when two or more of these symptoms appear together or when the drain plug shows brass from the synchroniser keys, because a partial fix on a 250,000 km box simply moves the failure to the next component. Replace the whole unit only when the case is cracked from an impact or the main-shaft thread is stripped, since a remanufactured 10JSD costs roughly half of a new box and carries the same warranty through the SAGMOTO parts network.

Oil sampling as an early warning

A gearbox rarely fails without warning, and the cheapest warning available is a 100 ml oil sample taken at every second drain. Lab reports on iron, copper and silicon tell three different stories: rising iron points to gear or bearing wear, rising copper points to synchroniser or bush wear, and rising silicon points to dust entering through the breather or a damaged shift tower seal. A single sample tells you the current state, but a trend across four samples tells you how many months of service remain.

Set the sampling point at the same place every time, taken hot after a run so the contaminants are suspended, and record the odometer with the sample. When the iron trend doubles between consecutive samples, schedule the teardown into the next planned downtime instead of waiting for a noise. On a tipper or crane chassis the PTO gear train is the first place to look, because it carries shock loads that the road gears never see and its oil is shared with the main box on most layouts.

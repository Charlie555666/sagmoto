How Much Does It Cost to Replace the Battery in a SAGMOTO Electric Truck?

Oct 09, 2026Buyer Guide11 min read

Quick answer: Replacing the LFP battery in a SAGMOTO electric truck costs about USD 8,000 to 15,000 for the i5 98kWh pack and USD 12,000 to 20,000 for the i9 131kWh pack, at roughly USD 90 to 150 per kWh. LFP packs last 3,000 to 4,000 cycles, so most fleets replace only after 6 to 10 years.

Battery replacement cost is the question every fleet finance manager asks before signing an electric truck order, and it is the right question. A battery is the single largest component cost on an EV, and unlike a diesel engine it has a known and finite cycle life. The anxiety around it is usually worse than the arithmetic, because the lithium-iron-phosphate chemistry SAGMOTO uses in the i5 and i9 is the most durable and cheapest-per-cycle chemistry on the market, and the replacement cost lands far below the figure most buyers fear. This article gives the numbers for the two current packs, explains the cycle life that sets the replacement timeline, states the warranty that covers most early failures, and walks through when to refurbish a pack instead of replacing it.

The two trucks in scope are the SAGMOTO i5, built on a roughly 98 kWh LFP pack for lighter distribution and last-mile duty, and the SAGMOTO i9, built on a 131 kWh LFP pack for heavier regional distribution. Both use LFP rather than the nickel-manganese-cobalt chemistry found in many passenger EVs, and that choice is deliberate: LFP trades some energy density for far longer cycle life, better thermal safety, and a lower cost per kilowatt-hour, which is exactly what a commercial fleet that counts cost per kilometre needs. The rest of this guide uses those two packs as the worked examples.

What Does a Replacement Battery Actually Cost for the i5 and i9?

The replacement cost of a complete pack is the pack price plus the labour to swap it, and on the SAGMOTO EVs the swap is a structured procedure because the pack is a floor-mounted enclosure with defined disconnects. The pack price itself scales with capacity, and at current export pricing the i5 98 kWh LFP pack lands at roughly USD 8,000 to 15,000 and the i9 131 kWh LFP pack at roughly USD 12,000 to 20,000, which works out to about USD 90 to 150 per usable kilowatt-hour depending on order volume, exchange rate and freight.

The wide band reflects three things: whether the pack is bought as a single unit or across a fleet order, whether it ships by air or sea, and whether the buyer takes a new pack or a refurbished one. A single i5 pack air-freighted urgent sits at the top of the band; a fleet order of i9 packs consolidated by sea sits at the bottom. Labour to swap a pack is typically a few hundred dollars where a workshop has the lift and the isolation procedure, because the physical swap is hours, not days.

How Long Does the LFP Battery Last Before You Replace It?

LFP chemistry is rated for 3,000 to 4,000 full charge-discharge cycles before capacity falls to roughly 80 percent of original, which is the threshold at which most fleets declare a pack due for replacement or refurbishment. Translating cycles into years depends on daily duty. An i5 or i9 running 80 to 250 km per day on a return-to-base cycle typically consumes between 0.4 and 1.1 full-equivalent cycles per day, which means the pack reaches the 80 percent threshold at about 6 to 10 years of service.

This is the figure that should calm the finance meeting. A diesel truck's engine does not have a neat 80 percent threshold; it simply costs more to maintain every year. An EV pack does have one, and it arrives late enough that the battery cost per year is modest against the fuel saving. The degradation curve is also gentle: LFP loses capacity slowly and linearly rather than cliff-edge, so a pack at 82 percent still does almost all of the daily duty, and the truck does not suddenly become unusable.

What Warranty Covers the Battery on Export Orders?

SAGMOTO export electric trucks carry an indicative battery warranty of 5 years or 200,000 km, whichever comes first, covering manufacturing defects and premature capacity loss below the 80 percent threshold within that period. The warranty is stated per contract rather than as a single global policy, and the terms that matter are the same ones as on the diesel warranty: what is covered, who performs the swap, who pays freight, and how fast a decision is made. A pack or module failure inside the warranty period is handled by air-freighted parts in 5 to 8 days for urgent claims or consolidated sea freight in 18 to 30 days for planned work.

For most fleets the warranty covers the expensive early-life risk and the pack then runs beyond it into the paid-replacement window at year 6 to 10. That structure is favourable compared with the diesel equivalent, where the most expensive engine repairs tend to arrive after the warranty has expired, not before. The practical advice is to confirm the battery warranty line in the proforma invoice exactly as you would confirm the engine warranty, and to keep charge and temperature records so a capacity claim can be assessed quickly.

Key point: The export EV battery warranty is about 5 years or 200,000 km against defects and capacity below 80 percent, with air-freight parts in 5 to 8 days. Confirm it in the contract and keep charge logs so a claim is assessed without argument.

Should You Refurbish or Fully Replace a Degraded Pack?

Not every degraded pack needs a full replacement, and this is where fleets save the most money. A pack is a series of modules managed by a battery management system. If one or two modules fall below threshold while the rest are healthy, the correct action is to replace those modules and rebalance the pack, which costs roughly 40 to 60 percent of a full pack and restores the pack to service for years more. Full replacement is justified only when many modules have degraded together, when the BMS has failed, or when cell grading shows the pack cannot be balanced back to a safe working window.

The refurbish-versus-replace decision should be made on data, not on caution. A workshop with a battery diagnostic tool can read per-module voltages and internal resistance and state plainly which modules are weak. Buying a refurbished complete pack, graded and warrantied by the supplier, is the middle option: roughly USD 4,000 to 8,000 for an i5 and USD 6,000 to 11,000 for an i9, with a shorter but real warranty. For a fleet watching total cost, a graded refurbished pack on a truck entering its lower-value years is often the rational choice.

  • One to two weak modules. Replace modules, rebalance, return to duty; cheapest valid fix.
  • Several weak modules, BMS healthy. Consider a graded refurbished pack if module supply is slow.
  • Pack-wide degradation or BMS failure. Full replacement is the only safe route.
  • Truck near end of economic life. A refurbished pack extends service without over-investing.

What Degrades the Battery Fastest and How Do You Slow It?

The three behaviours that shorten LFP pack life are sustained 100 percent charge, repeated deep discharge to near zero, and DC fast-charging in high ambient temperature. None is catastrophic by itself, but together they can pull the replacement timeline from 9 years to 5. The counter-measures are simple and cost nothing: charge daily to about 80 to 90 percent rather than 100 percent, avoid letting the pack drop below 20 percent where operation allows, and where the climate runs above 40 degrees use a depot charger with pack pre-cooling rather than repeated public DC fast-charging.

Fleets that adopt those habits typically add 15 to 25 percent to pack life, which is one to two extra years before replacement on the i5 and i9. That extension is worth more than the small convenience of always charging to full, and it is the single highest-return operating discipline an EV fleet can adopt. The SAGMOTO new energy electric trucks range is delivered with charge guidance for exactly this reason, and we brief fleets on it before delivery rather than after a capacity claim.

Conclusion

The battery replacement cost on a SAGMOTO electric truck is real but manageable, and it is far smaller than the diesel fuel it displaces over the pack's life. A full i5 98 kWh LFP pack runs about USD 8,000 to 15,000 and an i9 131 kWh LFP pack about USD 12,000 to 20,000, at USD 90 to 150 per kWh, with refurbished options at 40 to 60 percent of those figures. LFP chemistry delivers 3,000 to 4,000 cycles, placing the typical replacement at 6 to 10 years, by which point the fuel saving has long since paid for the pack. The 5-year or 200,000 km export warranty covers the early-life risk, and a module-level refurbish rather than a full swap is the cheapest valid fix for most degradation.

For a fleet evaluating the i5 or i9, the battery should not be the blocker it is often made out to be. The numbers are knowable, the chemistry is the most durable in commercial use, and the replacement can be planned years ahead as a line item rather than feared as a surprise. The buyers who do best are the ones who confirm the warranty in the contract, keep charge and temperature records, and decide refurbish versus replace on module data rather than on guesswork.

Should You Ship Trucks from China by RoRo or Container?

Sep 28, 2026Buying Guide10 min read

Quick answer: Ship by RoRo when a car carrier service runs to your discharge port and the order exceeds about three units, because a complete 6x4 tractor costs roughly USD 1,800 to 3,200 by RoRo to West Africa versus USD 4,500 to 7,500 containerised with disassembly. Choose containers for single units, landlocked destinations, or routes with no RoRo service.

Freight is the line item that quietly decides whether an import truck programme works. It sits between factory price and landed cost, it is quoted differently by different suppliers, and it is the item most often underestimated at budget stage. On a single truck it is small against the vehicle price. On forty units, the choice between roll-on roll-off and containerisation can move the total by more than USD 100,000.

The two methods are different products rather than two prices for the same service. RoRo moves a complete, drivable truck driven onto a vessel and off at the other end. Containerisation moves a truck partly disassembled, lifted by crane and reassembled at destination. Cost, transit and risk differences all follow from that distinction.

What Does RoRo Actually Cost Compared with Containerising a Truck?

RoRo freight is charged on volume, effectively on the deck space the vehicle occupies, so cost scales with dimensions rather than weight. A 6x4 tractor at roughly 6.5 metres long and 3.9 metres high occupies a large slot; a light truck occupies far less. The ranges below are indicative for a complete heavy unit from a Chinese port and move with fuel, season and vessel availability.

Two additions apply to both methods. Port, terminal handling and documentation charges typically add USD 200 to USD 600 per unit at each end. Marine insurance at 0.3 to 0.6 percent of cargo value should be treated as mandatory; on a USD 70,000 tractor that is USD 210 to USD 420, a small price for cover on deck-exposed cargo.

Containerisation also carries disassembly and reassembly cost that is easy to overlook. Removing and refitting the cab, air deflector, exhaust stack and drive wheels, plus packing and dunnage, typically costs USD 800 to USD 2,500 per heavy unit depending on strip-down extent and local labour rates. Add inland haulage and the RoRo advantage widens.

Key point: RoRo is usually 40 to 55 percent cheaper per heavy unit than containerisation once disassembly and reassembly are included. The advantage narrows for light trucks below about 2.6 metres overall height, which can sometimes be containerised complete, and it disappears entirely where no RoRo service exists.

How Long Does Each Method Take from Chinese Port to Delivery?

Transit time has two components buyers routinely conflate: sailing time and waiting time. Sailing times are as in the table above. Waiting time is the interval between the truck being ready at the port and the vessel departing, and it is where schedules are lost.

RoRo services on the main lanes run two to four times per month from Shanghai, Guangzhou, Tianjin and Qingdao. On secondary lanes the service can be monthly or irregular, and a truck that misses a sailing waits two to four weeks for the next. Book three to six weeks before readiness and require the vessel name and cut-off in writing.

Container services are far more frequent, typically weekly, so waiting time is usually under a week. Containerisation also serves landlocked destinations through a corridor port with onward road or rail haulage, which RoRo cannot do unless the discharge port allows the trucks to be driven out.

For a fleet order with contractual penalties, compare total door-to-door time. A RoRo sailing of 30 days plus a 20 day wait is slower than a container sailing of 35 days plus a 5 day wait and 4 days of reassembly, and the second option is usually more predictable.

Which Method Damages Trucks Less?

Neither method is damage-free and the risk profiles differ rather than one being simply better. Understanding them determines what the buyer should photograph and which insurance clause to insist on.

  • RoRo handling damage. Vehicles are driven on and off by stevedores and lashed by crew. Expect minor cosmetic damage on 2 to 5 percent of units on busy routes: scratched paint, dented steps, damaged mirrors, cracked light lenses. Serious damage is uncommon but occurs, usually from deck movement in heavy weather or a lashing failure.
  • RoRo exposure. Vehicles sit on open decks exposed to salt spray and weather. Underbody corrosion protection should be specified for long voyages, and bright work and exposed connectors benefit from protection.
  • Container crane damage. Containerised trucks are lifted by crane, and damage occurs at lift points, from lashing tension, or from shifting inside the box. The rate is lower than RoRo but individual incidents can be more severe.
  • Reassembly risk. The largest container-specific risk is reassembly quality. A cab refitted without correct torque, a harness reconnected wrongly or an air line cross-connected produces faults that appear weeks later and are attributed to the vehicle rather than to the reassembly.
  • Pilferage. Containerised cargo is better protected against theft of tools, batteries, radios and small components. On routes where this is a known problem, containerisation has a clear advantage.

Whichever method is used, require a documented pre-shipment condition report with photographs of all four corners, the cab, the windscreen, the instrument panel and the chassis plate, taken immediately before loading. That report is what a claim is assessed against; without it an insurance claim becomes an argument about when the damage occurred.

When Should I Choose Container Shipping Instead of RoRo?

RoRo is the default for most complete truck exports from China, but there are five situations in which containerisation is the correct answer.

  1. Single-unit orders. RoRo pricing has a fixed component; below roughly three units the per-unit cost can exceed containerisation.
  2. Landlocked destinations. If the trucks cannot be driven from the discharge port, containerisation through a corridor port is often the only practical route.
  3. Lanes with no RoRo service or very irregular sailings. Weekly container services beat a monthly RoRo vessel when the delivery date matters.
  4. SKD or CKD programmes. Where the buyer assembles locally, knockdown shipment in containers is the intended method, and it can reduce duty where local assembly is favoured by tariff.
  5. High pilferage risk. A sealed container is the better answer for vehicles shipped with tools or high-value loose components.

A middle path is worth knowing: break-bulk on flat rack containers, where a partly disassembled truck rides on an open frame. This suits oversize vehicles exceeding RoRo deck height limits and is priced between the two methods, but needs careful lashing and an experienced forwarder.

Key point: Ask two questions before accepting a freight quote. Is there a named RoRo vessel with a confirmed cut-off date, and does the discharge port allow the truck to be driven off and cleared locally? If either answer is no, containerisation is usually correct regardless of the price difference.

How Should I Prepare a Truck for Either Method?

Preparation is a joint responsibility. For RoRo, fuel should be reduced to a quarter tank or less as the carrier requires, the battery isolated, and the vehicle free of personal effects and loose cargo. It should arrive with a valid key, a charged battery and enough fuel to be driven off at destination.

For containerisation, the disassembly list should be agreed in writing before work starts, with photographs at each stage and all fasteners, brackets and trim bagged, labelled and shipped with the unit. A reassembly checklist with torque values and a post-reassembly road test should be part of delivery, and the buyer should not accept the truck until that test is complete.

Under both methods the documents matter as much as the physical preparation. Bill of lading, packing list, certificate of origin, commercial invoice, insurance certificate and any inspection certificate must be consistent in vehicle identification number, model and quantity. Inconsistency is the most common cause of customs delay, and demurrage of USD 100 to USD 300 per container per day accumulates quickly.

Conclusion

RoRo is the right answer for most complete truck exports from China on cost, typically USD 1,800 to 3,200 per heavy unit to West Africa against USD 4,500 to 7,500 containerised including disassembly, provided a service runs to the discharge port. Containerisation is right for single units, landlocked destinations, irregular lanes and knockdown programmes, and offers better protection against pilferage and weather. Neither is universally correct.

The decision should turn on four inputs rather than on freight rate alone: unit count, the discharge port's ability to receive a driven vehicle, the confirmed sailing schedule against the contractual delivery date, and the cost and quality of reassembly at destination. Buyers who compare only the rate line end up with trucks that arrived cheaply and late, or on time and reassembled badly.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should you ship trucks from China by RoRo or container?

Ship by RoRo when a car carrier service runs to your discharge port and the order exceeds about three units, because a complete 6x4 tractor costs roughly 1,800 to 3,200 USD by RoRo to West Africa versus 4,500 to 7,500 USD containerised with disassembly. Choose container shipping for single units, landlocked destinations, or routes with no RoRo service.

How much does RoRo shipping cost for a truck from China?

RoRo freight for a complete heavy truck runs about 1,200 to 2,200 USD to Gulf ports, 1,800 to 3,200 USD to West Africa, and 2,200 to 3,800 USD to East Africa or South America, based on cubic volume and deck space. Add port and handling charges of 200 to 600 USD and marine insurance at about 0.3 to 0.6 percent of cargo value.

Can a complete truck fit inside a shipping container?

A complete 6x4 tractor at roughly 3.9 metres overall height does not fit a standard 40ft container with 2.69 metres internal height, so it must ship on a flat rack or open top with the cab, air deflector, exhaust stack and often the wheels removed. Light trucks under 2.6 metres high can sometimes ship complete in a 40ft high-cube container with ramps.

How long does RoRo shipping take from China to Africa or the Middle East?

RoRo transit is about 18 to 25 days from Shanghai or Qingdao to Jebel Ali, 28 to 38 days to Lagos or Tema, and 30 to 42 days to Dar es Salaam or Durban, plus 3 to 7 days for loading and discharge. Sailings are typically monthly on secondary routes, so waiting for the next vessel can add 15 to 30 days to the total.

Is RoRo or container safer for shipping trucks?

RoRo carries a higher rate of minor cosmetic and handling damage, typically 2 to 5 percent of units with scratches or dented bodywork, because vehicles are driven and lashed by stevedores. Containerised units are better protected from weather and pilferage but risk crane and lashing damage, and reassembly after disassembly introduces its own defect risk if not supervised.

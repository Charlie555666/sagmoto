X6s Tipper PTO and Hydraulic Hoist Pump Maintenance

2026-11-06Maintenance9 min read

The SAGMOTO X6s is a 6x4 construction tipper rated at 270 to 300 hp from the Cummins ISD or Weichai WP7 engine families, built for quarry, pit and infrastructure duty where the body is raised and lowered constantly. The tipper body is raised by a front-mounted hydraulic hoist fed from a power take-off bolted to the transmission case. On a 25 tonne payload cycle the PTO engages hundreds of times per shift, and the hoist pump sees shock loads every time the body drops and the relief valve dumps flow. Shaanxi Fenghan Trading delivers the X6s to infrastructure contractors across Africa, the Middle East and Central Asia where dusty, high-shock sites are the norm, so a PTO and hydraulic discipline is the difference between a tipper that earns revenue and one parked with a seized pump. The procedures below assume the standard HYVA-style 4-stage telescopic hoist but apply to the single-stage underbody ram as well.

X6s PTO and Hoist Layout

The power take-off on the X6s is a flanged, air-shifted unit mounted on the left transmission case, driven by a gear set that splits engine torque to the hoist pump at a 1.0:1 ratio. A dash-mounted air switch sends 7.0 bar to the PTO shift cylinder, which engages the dog clutch and lights the cab indicator. The hoist pump is a fixed-displacement gear unit rated at 63 litres per minute and 200 bar, bolted to the PTO flange with a splined coupling guarded by a torque arm. Pressure flows through a 3/4 inch steel line to the hoist control valve and on to the ram, with a 25 micron return filter protecting the 90 litre tank. The whole circuit is open-centre, so the pump circulates idle flow back to tank whenever the body is stationary and the valve is centred.

Never engage the X6s PTO while the transmission is in gear or the clutch is released. The dog clutch needs the input shaft stopped; engaging under load shears the engagement dogs and fills the case with steel, taking the transmission with it. Neutral, clutch in, then air shift.

PTO Engagement Sequence and Checks

The correct X6s engagement order is: bring the truck to a stop, place the gearbox in neutral, depress the clutch, wait for the neutral confirmation lamp, then toggle the PTO air switch and release the clutch slowly. The cab indicator must show engaged before raising the body. If the PTO refuses to engage, check the 7.0 bar air supply at the shift cylinder, the condition of the 12 volt solenoid coil rated at 1.2 Amps, and the dog-clutch grease that hardens in cold sites and prevents travel. A grinding on engagement means the dogs are worn and the unit should be pulled before it jams. Disengage only after the body is fully down and the hoist valve centred, or the pump cavitates against a locked ram.

Hydraulic Oil Specification and Capacity

The X6s hoist circuit runs on an ISO VG 46 anti-wear hydraulic oil with a zinc additive package, chosen for its film strength under the 200 bar spike at the relief valve. In Arctic sites below minus 20 degrees a VG 32 is substituted to keep the pump from cavitating on a cold start, while desert operations above 45 degrees ambient use VG 68 to maintain film at high sump temperature. The total fill is 90 litres including the tank, lines and ram, and the oil is changed on a contamination basis rather than fixed kilometres because dust ingress dominates wear. A clean system holds the oil at NAS 9 cleanliness; above NAS 11 the pump life halves. The reservoir breather is a 10 micron desiccant cap that must be changed every 500 operating hours.

Seal and Coupling Inspection

The X6s hoist circuit has five seals that fail on a known schedule. The PTO output seal at the pump flange weeps first, dripping VG 46 onto the chassis and lowering tank level slowly. The hoist control valve spool seals harden with heat and let the body creep down under load, a dangerous fault that must be caught at service. The ram wiper seals admit dust that scores the chrome and leaks past the chevron pack; a weeping ram at the top of stroke is the visible tell. The splined coupling between PTO and pump should be pulled every 1,000 operating hours and the 30 mm hex coupling bolts retorqued to 55 Nm, because a loose coupling walks and destroys the pump bearing. The kit X6S-PT-SET covers all four shaft seals and the valve pack in one box.

A tipper body that slowly settles after you leave it is not a nuisance, it is a failed valve seal that can drop the load without warning. Test for creep by raising the body mid-stroke, closing the valve and watching for two minutes of downward travel. Any movement means the seal set comes out that day.

Pressure Testing Procedure

To verify the X6s hoist, fit a 0 to 250 bar gauge at the pump outlet test point and block the ram line with the supplied blank. Start the engine, engage the PTO, and read the dead-head pressure at 1,000 rpm; a healthy pump holds 195 to 205 bar before the relief valve cracks. If pressure builds slowly or falls under a held load, the gear faces are worn and the pump is renewed using part HP-X6S-63. Next, with the body raised and the valve centred, confirm the relief holds the system at 200 bar without creeping. Finally cycle the body up and down three times and watch the gauge return to zero at rest; a slow return to zero means the valve spool is sticking and needs the kit above. Record both cold and hot readings because VG 46 thins and drops pressure as the sump warms.

Preventive Service Schedule

Keep one spare HP-X6S-63 pump and one X6S-PT-SET on the site store for every ten X6s in the fleet, because a failed pump stops the tipper completely while a seal kit only weeps and can be planned. The VG 46 drum should be dedicated to the hoist circuit and never shared with the steering or transmission, since cross-contamination of additive types forms sludge that blocks the 25 micron filter and mimics a worn pump on the pressure gauge. A labelled, sealed drum with a dispensing nozzle prevents the wrong oil reaching the tank during a night shift when supervision is light, and that single discipline removes a whole class of avoidable hoist failures from the maintenance record.

An X6s on single-shift quarry duty should have tank level and breather condition checked every 250 operating hours, the return filter element swapped at 500 hours, and the coupling bolts retorqued at 1,000 hours. Oil is sampled for particle count every 1,000 hours and changed when it crosses NAS 11 or shows free water above 200 parts per million. The PTO air cylinder and solenoid are exercised and greased every 500 hours so cold-site engagement never sticks. Ram and Body Alignment Checks

Beyond the pump and seals, the X6s hoist life depends on the body sitting true on the chassis so the ram extends straight. A twisted sub-frame bends the ram tube and the ram binds at the top of stroke, reading as a pressure spike the pump cannot sustain. Check the body pivot bushes for wear every 1,000 hours and replace the 40 mm bronze bushes before they elongate, because a sloppy pivot lets the body rock and side-loads the ram. The front trunnion and the rear hinge should be greased with a lithium EP grease every 250 hours through the nipples, and the relief valve crack pressure re-confirmed at each major service so the system never exceeds the 200 bar design ceiling. A body that leans to one side on the way up almost always traces to a worn pivot rather than the hydraulics, and correcting the pivot first saves an unnecessary pump change.

Common Site Faults and Fixes

The X6s hoist develops a short list of field faults. Slow body rise with normal engine speed means a worn pump or a restricted suction strainer, fixed by the HP-X6S-63 pump or a strainer clean. A body that rises then stalls points to a relief valve set too low, adjusted up to the 200 bar crack. Jerky extension is trapped air from a low tank, cured by topping and bleeding the ram at the top port. Overheating oil on long lifts means the cooler is blocked with dust and needs a pressure wash. A PTO that kicks out under load has a weak air holding spring and needs the shift cylinder reseal.

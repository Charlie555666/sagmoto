SAGMOTO i5 Reducer Oil Service for Electric Truck Fleets

2026-11-17Maintenance8 min read

The i5 is a battery-electric light truck with a single-speed reduction gearbox between the motor and the drive axle. Unlike a diesel truck, it has no clutch, no multi-ratio box and no exhaust, so the list of routine consumables is short and the reducer oil is one of the few fluids that still needs regular attention. That oil lubricates the reduction gears and bearings, carries heat away from the motor-side interface and protects copper-containing components that a conventional heavy-duty gear oil can attack. This article covers the fluid specification and interval, a correct drain and fill, how to interpret the magnetic plug, seal and interface checks, noise and backlash diagnosis, and how an oil analysis programme fits a busy electric fleet.

Why the i5 reducer is different

A single-speed reducer turns motor speed down to wheel speed in one fixed ratio, so it sees high input speed and strong torque reversal rather than the varied loads of a stepped gearbox. In the i5 the gearcase sits close to the motor, and in many layouts the two share a seal and a cooling circuit. The lubricant therefore has to do two jobs at once: form a film strong enough for the gear teeth and stay chemically gentle enough for the copper and electronic materials near the motor. This is why the fluid choice matters more than the oil capacity, which is modest at roughly 1.5 to 2.0 litres depending on build.

Because an electric motor delivers instant torque from rest, the reducer takes a sharp load every time the vehicle pulls away, often with no gradual clutch engagement to soften it. That shock shows up at the gear teeth and at the splined coupling to the motor, and it makes backlash and coupling condition more important than on a conventional driveline.

Oil specification and change interval

The i5 calls for a synthetic low-viscosity gear fluid, typically an API GL-4 grade around 75W-80 or an OEM-named e-axle fluid, and the vehicle manual should be treated as the final authority. Do not substitute a GL-5 hypoid oil simply because it is described as gear oil; the extreme-pressure additives in GL-5 are designed for steel hypoid gears and can corrode the yellow metals used in parts of the electric drive unit. Mixing two fluid types is equally unwise, since additive incompatibility can cause foaming and a loss of lubrication exactly where the motor and gearbox meet.

On normal urban delivery duty the reducer oil should be changed every 60,000 km or every 24 months, whichever comes first, while fleets doing constant stop-start work, carrying maximum payload or operating in hot climates should shorten this to 30,000 km or 12 months. Electric trucks spend a lot of time at low speed under high torque, which is hard on the oil film, so the severe-duty interval is the right choice for most municipal and last-mile operators. Record the date and kilometres at each change so the next one is driven by data rather than memory.

Drain and fill procedure

Drain the reducer while it is warm after a short run, because warm oil suspends fine wear particles that would otherwise cling to the casing. Remove the fill plug first, then the drain plug, so the unit can vent as it empties and a blocked filler is discovered before the oil is gone. Let the stream slow to a drip, clean the magnetic plug and inspect it before refitting, then refill through the fill opening until oil just reaches the lower edge of the hole with the vehicle level. Refit both plugs to the torque in the manual and wipe the casing clean so a future leak is easy to spot.

After filling, run the vehicle slowly and recheck the level, since air trapped in the gearcase can give a low initial reading. Overfilling is a real risk in a compact reducer; excess fluid is churned into foam, raises internal pressure and is forced out through the output seal. Keep the drained fluid for analysis if the fleet runs a sampling programme, since a single bottle tells more than a visual check at the drain pan.

Key point: Use the GL-4 or OEM e-fluid specified for the i5 reducer, never a GL-5 hypoid oil. With the vehicle level, fill only to the lower edge of the fill hole and recheck after a slow run.

Reading the magnetic drain plug

The magnetic plug is one of the cheapest condition sensors on the i5. A light, even coating of fine metallic paste after a normal service interval is expected, because gear teeth bedding in shed microscopic particles that the magnet holds. What matters is a change: a plug carrying large flakes, visible scoring marks or a noticeably heavier deposit than the previous service points to active wear and should trigger an inspection of the gears and bearings. Photograph the plug at every drain and keep the images beside the service record so the trend can be judged objectively.

Non-metallic debris is equally informative. A gritty feel in the oil or a brown, sludge-like film suggests water ingress or fluid breakdown, while bright copper-coloured particles may point to wear at a bushing near the motor interface.

Seal leaks and the motor interface

Inspect the output shaft seals and the joint between the reducer and the motor at every service. A small damp film around a seal lip is common, but oil collecting in a drip or thrown onto the floor indicates the lip is worn or the shaft surface is scored. On the i5, oil migrating toward the motor is more serious than an external weep, because fluid entering the motor housing can damage insulation and windings. Any sign of oil on the motor side of the joint should stop the vehicle until the seal is renewed and the coupling inspected.

The motor-to-gearbox coupling deserves a close look because electric torque reversal loads the splines hard. Check for play by rotation with the vehicle safely lifted, listen for a knock when torque changes from drive to coast, and confirm that the motor mounts are tight so the gearcase is not carrying alignment loads it was not designed for.

Noise and backlash diagnosis

Reducer noise falls into two broad types, and separating them saves a great deal of guesswork. A high-pitched whine that changes with road speed usually comes from gear mesh or a bearing, while a single clunk on taking up drive or on lifting off points to excess backlash or a loose coupling. Measure backlash at the output flange with a dial indicator, comparing the figure with the manual; a value that has grown steadily over successive services means internal wear even if no noise is yet audible. A small amount of backlash is necessary for lubrication and thermal growth, so the goal is to keep it within specification rather than to eliminate it.

Road-test the i5 at low speed, at city speed and on a gentle deceleration, noting when the sound changes, because load condition is a better clue than volume. A gear whine that only appears under acceleration often reflects a tooth contact pattern problem that fresh oil will not cure, and it should be investigated by stripping the unit rather than masked with a thicker fluid.

Oil analysis for electric fleets

An oil analysis programme makes particular sense for electric trucks because the oil changes are infrequent and the fluid carries the only continuous record of internal wear. Draw a consistent sample from the drain stream at every change, using a clean bottle and the same procedure each time, and test for viscosity, water content, particle count and wear metals such as iron and copper. A rising iron trend suggests gear or bearing wear, while copper can indicate a bushing or motor-side component beginning to rub. The cost is small against an unplanned reducer rebuild.

Build a baseline from a known healthy vehicle in the fleet, then compare each truck against it rather than against a generic limit. Heating and water are the two enemies that analysis reveals early; water breaks down the additive package and promotes corrosion, while sustained high temperature thickens the fluid and reduces film strength in the gear mesh. Where a fleet notices repeated water readings, check the breather and the seal at the motor joint before assuming the oil itself is at fault.

Key point: Sample the reducer oil at every change and watch the trend in iron, copper and water. A single out-of-range result is a prompt to investigate, not a reason to fit a thicker oil and carry on.

Parts to stock and workshop planning

A depot running i5 vans or light trucks should hold the correct GL-4 or e-axle fluid, drain and fill plugs with sealing washers, output shaft seals and the motor-side seal, plus a coupling set for the highest-mileage vehicles. A dial indicator for backlash, a torque wrench covering the plug range and clean sample bottles complete the kit. Because the reducer holds little oil, the cost of stocking the right fluid is modest, but using the wrong grade is one of the quickest ways to damage an electric drive unit. Electric drivelines remove many of the maintenance tasks a diesel fleet lives with, but they do not remove the need to look after the few fluids that remain. Keep the correct low-viscosity fluid in stock, sample it at every change and pay attention to what the magnetic plug and the noise under load are saying. An i5 reducer is a simple unit by design, and with clean oil and a sound motor interface it will run quietly for the life of the vehicle, leaving the fleet free to focus on charging and route planning instead of unexpected driveline repairs.

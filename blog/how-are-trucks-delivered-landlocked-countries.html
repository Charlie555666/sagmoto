How Are Trucks Delivered to Landlocked Countries Like Zambia or Kazakhstan?

Oct 13, 2026Buying Guide9 min read

Quick answer: Trucks for landlocked countries are delivered by sea freight to a neighbouring coastal port followed by overland haulage, or by rail via China-Europe corridors. Zambia typically routes through Dar es Salaam, Kazakhstan by rail via Khorgos or Alashankou, and Walvis Bay serves as an alternative port for Zambia and Zimbabwe.

Delivery is the part of an export truck purchase that buyers in coastal markets never worry about and buyers in landlocked markets worry about first. A fleet in Dar es Salaam or Mombasa receives trucks the way most people receive freight: off a ship, through a port, and onto a transporter. A fleet in Lusaka or Almaty has no port of its own, and every truck must cross at least one international border and several hundred to several thousand kilometres of someone else's road or rail to reach them. That extra leg is where cost, time and risk are made or lost.

This article explains the two dominant delivery models for SAGMOTO trucks into landlocked markets: sea freight to a neighbouring coastal port plus overland haulage, and rail via the China-Europe land corridors. We use Zambia and Kazakhstan as the worked examples because they sit at opposite ends of the delivery problem, one in southern Africa and one in Central Asia, and because the correct route for each is a clear illustration of the principles that apply to every landlocked destination.

How Are Trucks Shipped to a Landlocked Country Like Zambia?

Zambia has no coastline, so every imported truck arrives by sea to a neighbouring port and then travels overland. The two realistic gateways are Dar es Salaam in Tanzania, roughly 1,800 km by road from Lusaka, and Walvis Bay in Namibia, roughly 2,300 km away. Dar es Salaam is the shorter and more heavily used corridor; Walvis Bay is the alternative when the Dar corridor is congested or when serving the southern or western provinces of Zambia and neighbouring Zimbabwe.

The sea leg carries the trucks from a Chinese load port, typically Shanghai or Ningbo, to the gateway port in about 28 to 35 days, depending on the routing and the transhipment. At the port, the trucks are cleared, plated for transit if required, and either driven or low-loaded onto transporters for the overland leg. Driven delivery is common for complete trucks and is cheaper; transporter delivery is used for CKD kits or where road permits restrict self-driving.

The overland leg is the part that demands local knowledge. It involves transit permits, overweight or oversize escort where applicable, fuel and border handling on the corridor, and a receiving depot in Zambia that can take the trucks and complete local registration. Buyers should treat the overland leg as a managed project with a named forwarder rather than as a default line item, because the variance in that leg is what moves the total transit time.

Key point: For Zambia, Dar es Salaam is the default gateway at about 1,800 km to Lusaka; Walvis Bay is the alternative at about 2,300 km. Plan the overland leg with a named forwarder, because that is where transit time variance lives.

What Rail Routes Serve Kazakhstan and Central Asia?

Kazakhstan is a different delivery problem. It shares a long land border with China and sits directly on the China-Europe rail corridor, which means a large share of trucks for the Kazakh market can be moved by rail through the Khorgos or Alashankou border crossings rather than by sea at all. Rail transit from Xi'an to Almaty is typically 18 to 30 days, and it avoids the ocean entirely, which removes a whole class of port and weather risk.

The rail option is attractive not only for speed but for cost structure. Per unit, rail haulage across the corridor is typically 30 to 50 percent lower than a comparable full overland road haulage, and it is far more predictable than a multi-border road journey. The constraint is capacity and inland clearance: rail works best when the buyer has a receiving point near a rail hub and when the border crossing is not congested.

For a fleet buying heavy prime movers for Central Asian work, the rail corridor also has a strategic logic. A E1st flagship tractor truck 560HP delivered by rail to Almaty can be working on the same corridor it arrived on within days of clearance, and the buyer avoids the coastal detour entirely. The same logic applies to tippers and SAGMOTO dump truck models 6x4 8x4 units destined for Kazakh or regional mining and infrastructure work.

How Much Does Overland Delivery From a Coastal Port Cost?

The cost of the inland leg is the part most often underestimated, because the ocean freight quote a buyer receives rarely includes the journey from the port to the depot. Overland haulage from a coastal gateway to an inland depot typically costs USD 1,500 to 4,500 per unit, driven by distance, mode and the permits required. The Zambia-to-Dar es Salaam corridor commonly runs around USD 2,000 to 3,500 per truck including escort and transit permit, while longer corridors such as Walvis Bay to Harare sit toward the upper end.

Several variables move that number. Self-drive delivery is cheaper than transporter delivery but ties up drivers and adds kilometres and risk to the truck before it is even in service. Route congestion, fuel price on the corridor, border delays and the need for police or overweight escort all add cost. A buyer comparing two gateway ports should compare total delivered cost to the depot, not port-to-port ocean freight, because the inland leg can be a larger share of the total than the ocean leg for a distant inland destination.

Which Ports Are Used as Gateways for Landlocked Markets?

The choice of gateway is a function of geography, corridor reliability and the buyer's inland customs capacity. For East Africa, Dar es Salaam and Mombasa are the principal gateways, with Dar serving Zambia, the DRC copperbelt and parts of Malawi, and Mombasa serving Uganda, Rwanda, Burundi and South Sudan via the inland corridors. For southern Africa, Walvis Bay has grown as a gateway for Zambia, Zimbabwe and Botswana because it offers a less congested alternative to the traditional routes and a good road corridor inland.

For Central Asia, the gateway is not a seaport at all but the rail border crossings at Khorgos and Alashankou, which connect the Chinese rail network to Kazakhstan and onward to Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan and beyond. The rail gateway is chosen for speed and cost rather than for any port equivalence, and it reflects the fundamentally different geography of the Central Asian land corridor compared with the African sea-plus-road model.

Each gateway has trade-offs. Dar es Salaam is short but can congest; Walvis Bay is reliable but longer; Mombasa is mature but serves a different corridor; and the rail gateways are fast but capacity-bound. A buyer should choose the gateway on total delivered cost and transit reliability for their specific inland point, not on habit or on which port the supplier uses by default.

Should I Use Sea-Plus-Road or Full Rail Delivery?

For most landlocked African destinations, sea-plus-road through Dar es Salaam or Walvis Bay is the practical default, with total transit of 35 to 55 days from factory to depot and a delivery model that is well established by experienced forwarders. It is flexible, it handles CKD kits and complete trucks alike, and it does not depend on rail capacity that may be allocated elsewhere. The cost is the longer transit and the exposure to road-corridor congestion.

For Kazakhstan and the wider Central Asian region, full rail via Khorgos or Alashankou is usually the better answer, at 18 to 30 days and a lower cost per unit, provided the buyer has rail-connected receiving capacity and the border is flowing. Where rail capacity is constrained or the final destination is far from a rail hub, a hybrid of rail to the border region plus a short road leg is the pragmatic compromise.

The decision should be made per order, not per policy. A buyer taking a single trial unit to Lusaka has no reason to engage rail; a buyer taking a fifty-unit programme to Almaty should absolutely use the rail corridor. The right answer is the one that minimises total delivered cost and risk for that specific quantity, destination and timing, and a good export supplier will model both options rather than pushing one.

Conclusion

Delivery to a landlocked country is a solved problem with two mature models: sea freight to a neighbouring coastal port followed by overland haulage, and rail via the China-Europe corridors. Zambia illustrates the first, with Dar es Salaam at about 1,800 km and Walvis Bay at about 2,300 km as the gateways, and total transit of roughly 35 to 55 days. Kazakhstan illustrates the second, with rail through Khorgos or Alashankou delivering in about 18 to 30 days at a lower per-unit cost than road haulage.

The buyer's job is to treat the inland leg as a planned, priced component of the landed cost, to choose the gateway on total delivered cost and reliability rather than on habit, and to match the mode to the order size and destination. For a single trial unit, sea-plus-road is simplest; for a fleet programme into Central Asia, rail is usually decisive. Whichever route fits, the delivery plan should be agreed and costed in the same document as the truck specification, so that the price you compare is the price delivered to your depot, not the price at a port you cannot use.

Frequently Asked Questions

How are trucks shipped to a landlocked country like Zambia?

Trucks for Zambia are delivered by sea to Dar es Salaam about 1,800 km by road or to Walvis Bay about 2,300 km away, then hauled overland, with total transit of roughly 35 to 55 days from factory to depot. The Dar es Salaam corridor is the shorter and more commonly used route.

What rail routes serve Kazakhstan and Central Asia?

Kazakhstan is served by rail through the Khorgos or Alashankou border crossings on the China-Europe corridor, with transit of about 18 to 30 days from Xi'an to Almaty and costs typically 30 to 50 percent lower than full overland road haulage for the same distance.

How much does overland delivery from a coastal port cost?

Overland haulage from a coastal port to an inland depot typically costs USD 1,500 to 4,500 per unit depending on distance and mode, with the Zambia-to-Dar es Salaam corridor running around USD 2,000 to 3,500 per truck including escort and permit.

Which ports are used as gateways for landlocked markets?

The principal gateway ports are Dar es Salaam and Mombasa for East Africa and Walvis Bay for southern Africa, plus the Khorgos and Alashankou rail gateways for Central Asia, placing inland depots roughly 1,800 to 2,300 km from a seaport or about 1,100 km by rail from the border crossing.

Should I use sea-plus-road or full rail delivery?

For most landlocked African destinations, sea-plus-road through Dar es Salaam or Walvis Bay is the practical default at 35 to 55 days, while rail via Khorgos is faster for Kazakhstan at 18 to 30 days when rail capacity and inland clearance are available.

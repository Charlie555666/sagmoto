SAGMOTO E3 MAX: Payload, GVWR and Body Volume Explained

2026-11-18Product Deep Dive10 min read

Three numbers decide whether an eight-wheel tipper earns or loses, and buyers routinely confuse them. GVWR is the legal and structural ceiling. Tare is what the truck weighs empty. Body volume is how much space the material occupies. Payload is what is left of GVWR after tare, and the body volume has to be sized so that the material which fills it weighs no more than that payload. Get the relationship wrong and you either leave capacity unused on every trip or you run illegal and overload the axles.

The SAGMOTO E3 MAX is the heavy 8x4 tipper in the range, built around the Weichai WP12.430E50 at 430 horsepower and 2,060 Nm with a 31 tonne GVWR and bodies from roughly 20 to 24 cubic metres depending on specification. That combination is powerful enough to be worth optimising carefully, because a one tonne payload error on a 22 tonne payload is a 4.5 percent revenue error on every trip for the life of the truck.

Key point: Body volume must be matched to material density, not chosen by habit. At 31 tonnes GVWR and a 10.5 tonne tare, the payload is 20.5 tonnes, which means a 20 m³ body is right for 1.0 t/m³ material and a 12 m³ body would be right for 1.7 t/m³ rock. An oversized body on dense material guarantees overloading.

GVWR, payload and volume are three different things

GVWR is set by the weakest structural element: frame, axles, springs, brakes or tyres. It is a limit, not a target, and on an 8x4 tipper it is usually 31 tonnes in export configurations, with heavier variants available for mining. Payload is GVWR minus tare, and tare includes the chassis, cab, body, hoist, fuel, driver and any equipment. Body volume is a geometric property that determines how many cubic metres of material the truck can carry at all.

The link between them is material density. Payload divided by density gives the volume the body should have for that material. A body that is larger than that number will be filled to the top by a loader and will then be overweight; a body that is smaller will leave payload unused. Neither error is visible on a specification sheet, which is why so many tippers are mis-bodied for the material they actually carry.

The table explains why a fleet carrying crushed rock and a fleet carrying coal need different trucks even though both want a 31 tonne GVWR tipper. The rock fleet needs a 11 to 13 m³ body with a heavy floor; the coal fleet needs 22 to 24 m³ and can use a lighter body. Buying one body specification for both guarantees that one of them is wrong, and in practice the rock operation is the one that ends up overloaded because the loader fills the body to the top regardless.

Key point: Ask for the density first, then the body. Sand at 1.5 t/m³ and coal at 0.9 t/m³ need bodies that differ by 70 percent in volume for the same legal payload, and no single body specification serves both.

Axle distribution on an 8x4

An 8x4 has two steering axles and a tandem drive group, and the load distribution between them is as important as the total. The front group typically carries 13 to 15 tonnes and the rear tandem 16 to 18 tonnes at 31 tonnes GVW, and the body position determines how the load splits. Move the body forward and the front group takes more; move it back and the tandem takes more.

PositionFront groupRear tandemEffect
Body too far backUnderloadedOverloadedFails weighbridge, tandem and tyre wear
Body centred per load diagramWithin limitsWithin limitsLegal and balanced

The correct position comes from the body builder's load diagram, which calculates the centre of gravity of the loaded body and positions it so that each axle group lands within its rating. That diagram should be produced before the body is built, not checked after it is fitted. On the E3 MAX the wheelbase options allow the body to be positioned correctly for most materials, provided the body builder is given the density and the axle ratings rather than just the body length.

The tare budget

Tare is where payload disappears. A standard E3 MAX 8x4 chassis-cab sits around 9.5 to 10.5 tonnes; a heavy-duty body with an 8 mm floor and 6 mm sides, a heavy hoist and a reinforced subframe can add 2.5 to 3.5 tonnes, pushing tare to 13 tonnes and payload down to 18 tonnes. That is 2.5 tonnes of lost payload on every trip, which on 5 trips a day and 300 days is 3,750 tonnes a year.

The right tare depends on the material's abrasiveness. For sand, gravel and grain a lighter body with a replaceable wear plate is cheaper overall, because the body is not being battered by large rock. For blasted rock and demolition debris the heavier floor pays for itself by avoiding repair downtime. Set the tare budget before inviting quotations, and specify the plate thickness and the hoist type so that quotations are comparable rather than optimistic.

Body steel specification and wear life

The body is a consumable in a way the chassis is not. A floor carrying blasted rock at 1.8 tonnes per cubic metre, dropped from a loader bucket two metres above, will wear through a 6 mm plate in two to three years. The specification decision is therefore about wear rate versus tare, and the answer differs by material even within one quarry.

A wear-resistant floor plate is often the better answer than simply thicker mild steel, because it achieves longer life at lower weight. On an E3 MAX where payload is worth roughly USD 3.50 per tonne delivered, 400 kg of saved tare is worth USD 1,400 a year at five trips a day, which over five years pays for a considerable amount of wear plate. The calculation should be done per material, not per habit.

Key point: Wear plate beats thick mild steel on both life and weight. A 8 mm wear-resistant floor typically outlasts a 10 mm mild steel floor while weighing less, which is payload saved every trip for the life of the body.

Where the structural margin goes

An 8x4 tipper at 31 tonnes GVW working a quarry is not operating at its structural limit; it has margin for the torsional inputs of broken ground and the shock loads of loading. That margin is deliberately built in, and it is consumed by overloading. Running at 35 tonnes on every trip does not fail the truck immediately, but it consumes the fatigue life at roughly double the rate, and the frame, springs and body mounts will show it within three to four years.

The honest position is that a truck specified for 31 tonnes and consistently run at 35 is a truck that will need frame and suspension work at year four rather than year eight. If the operation genuinely requires 35 tonnes, specify the heavier frame and suspension package rather than overloading the standard one, because the cost of the upgrade is much lower than the cost of the repairs plus the downtime. The lighter package is only appropriate if the payload discipline is real.

Key point: Structural margin is not spare capacity; it is fatigue reserve for rough ground and loading shock. Running 4 tonnes over on every trip consumes that reserve at roughly double rate and moves frame and spring repairs from year eight to year four.

Verifying on a weighbridge

Every new tipper should be weighed empty and loaded on a weighbridge before it enters service, with the axle loads recorded. That single measurement validates the tare assumption, the body position and the payload, and it establishes a baseline for the driver's load height gauge. Fleets that skip it discover the error months later, when the first fine arrives or the first spring fails.

The measurement should be repeated at each service interval for the first year, because body repairs, added liners and accumulated mud change tare. A 200 kg increase in tare is 1 percent of payload, and it is invisible without the scale. Conclusion

The E3 MAX is only as good as the arithmetic behind its body. At 31 tonnes GVWR with a realistic 10.5 tonne tare, payload is 20.5 tonnes, and that number divided by the material density gives the correct body volume — roughly 13 m³ for gravel, 11 to 13 m³ for crushed rock, 20 m³ for coal and 25 m³ or more for grain. Position the body from a load diagram, hold tare to a budget, respect the structural margin and verify on a weighbridge. Get those four right and the truck carries the payload it was paid for, legally, for eight years.

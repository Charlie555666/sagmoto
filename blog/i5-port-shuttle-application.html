Electric Port and Terminal Shuttle on the SAGMOTO i5 Platform

2026-11-11Industry Application8 min read

Container terminals and inland logistics yards are under growing pressure to cut local emissions while keeping tractors moving through two or three shifts a day. The SAGMOTO i5 electric truck, rated at 120 to 160 kilowatts peak with a 100 to 140 kilowatt hour battery pack and a permanent magnet electric motor, is well matched to the short, repetitive cycles of a port or terminal shuttle where top speed is rarely above forty kilometres per hour and the vehicle returns to a depot every few hours. Because the i5 was designed for urban delivery with a realistic range near 200 kilometres, the same chassis becomes a natural platform for terminal shuttling once the body is adapted to a fifth wheel or drawbar trailer coupling.

Why the i5 fits a yard duty cycle

A yard duty cycle is fundamentally different from line-haul driving. The vehicle accelerates from a standstill, travels a few hundred metres, stops, reverses, couples or decouples a trailer, and repeats this pattern for the whole shift. Regenerative braking recovers a large share of the energy from each stop, so the effective energy draw is far lower than the battery size alone would suggest. The i5 motor delivers full torque from zero revolutions, which gives the shuttle crisp pull-away under a loaded trailer without the clutch wear that shortens the life of a diesel yard tractor used in the same stop-start pattern.

The low centre of gravity from a floor-mounted battery also improves stability when a shuttle jockeys a loaded container chassis through tight terminal lanes. Drivers transferring from diesel units notice the absence of idle vibration and exhaust heat, which reduces fatigue during night shifts where the terminal runs continuously and the cab temperature would otherwise climb. The i5 retains the standard SAGMOTO air brake circuit and ABS plus ESC, so yard safety systems such as automatic braking on obstacle detection can be layered on without re-engineering the foundation brakes that already meet the type approval.

Size the battery for the worst shift, not the average shift. A hot afternoon with full reefer loads and a congested gate can lift energy use by thirty percent, and a shuttle that runs flat mid-shift forces a costly trailer recovery by a second unit.

Opportunity charging at the crane and depot

Opportunity charging means topping up the battery during natural idle moments rather than waiting for a full depot recharge. At a quay crane or a rail interchange, a shuttle may wait two to five minutes while a container is lifted, and a correctly placed charging point can add meaningful range in that window. The i5 supports standard depot charging overnight and can accept opportunity top-ups at defined power levels during the shift without harming pack life when the thermal management is respected and the state of charge is kept inside the recommended band.

Terminals should plan charging infrastructure alongside the vehicle order, because the civil works for cable routes and transformer capacity take longer to approve than the truck delivery. A phased approach starts with overnight depot charging for a first fleet and adds opportunity points once the duty data confirms where the idle windows fall, so the operator avoids paying for chargers that sit unused beside a lane the shuttle never visits during a shift.

Never rely on a single charging point for the whole shuttle fleet. A fault or planned maintenance on one charger should not ground every electric tractor, so size the depot with at least one spare circuit per shift pattern and a manual fallback procedure.

Battery size versus shift length

The choice between a 100 kilowatt hour pack and a 140 kilowatt hour pack is a trade between purchase price, vehicle weight and shift coverage. A single eight hour shift of yard shuttling typically draws between 80 and 120 kilowatt hours depending on the number of loaded moves, the gradient of the terminal and the use of cab heating or cooling. The larger pack gives headroom for a double shift or for peak summer loads without a mid-shift charge that would pull the tractor off the lane at the worst moment of the day.

Operators evaluating the platform can explore the SAGMOTO new energy electric trucks line-up to compare the i5 against heavier electric models where the yard also handles out-of-gate drayage. Matching pack size to the measured shift rather than to a marketing number prevents both stranded range on a busy day and wasted payload from an oversized battery that the duty cycle never actually depletes across a normal week of operation.

Terminal emission rules driving electrification

Many port authorities have set phased targets that restrict diesel equipment in certain terminal zones, and some jurisdictions offer lower berthing fees or gate priority to carriers that demonstrate clean yard equipment. An electric shuttle helps a terminal meet these rules without waiting for a full fleet replacement, because the yard tractor is one of the highest-utilisation and highest-idle vehicles on site. Early adopters also benefit from grant programmes that subsidise charging infrastructure as part of a wider decarbonisation plan that the terminal would otherwise fund entirely from capital.

The i5 produces no tailpipe emission at the point of use, which improves air quality for dock workers who spend shifts adjacent to moving equipment. Noise reduction is a secondary benefit that matters for terminals bordering residential areas, where night-shift diesel idling generates complaints that electric units avoid entirely, and where a quieter yard can extend the permitted operating window without triggering a noise abatement order from the local authority.

Specifying tyres, fifth wheel and drawbar for yard work

Yard shuttles run at low speed but under high drawbar loads when pulling a loaded container chassis, so tyre selection should favour sidewall strength over a high-speed rating. A fifth wheel or drawbar coupling must be specified to the trailer fleet on site, and the i5 frame should be reinforced at the mounting point to absorb the pitching loads of repeated coupling. Low-profile yard tyres reduce step-in height for drivers who enter and exit the cab dozens of times per shift, which lowers fatigue and the minor-injury risk that comes with constant climbing.

Lighting and warning systems deserve attention because yard traffic mixes trucks, straddle carriers and pedestrians. Additional amber beacons, proximity sensors and a robust mirror or camera package reduce the risk of minor impacts that, while rarely injurious, generate downtime and paperwork. The i5 cab already supports the standard SAGMOTO electrical architecture, so these additions draw from existing fused circuits rather than requiring a separate harness that complicates the upfit and the later fault diagnosis.

Total cost of ownership versus diesel yard trucks

The purchase price of an electric shuttle is higher than an equivalent diesel unit, but the ownership case rests on energy and maintenance savings. Electricity per kilometre costs a fraction of diesel, and the i5 removes engine oil, fuel filters, exhaust after-treatment and clutch assemblies from the service schedule. Brake wear drops sharply thanks to regeneration, so foundation brake components last far longer in stop-start yard duty and the periodic inspection of the air system becomes the dominant recurring cost rather than consumable replacement.

A terminal running twenty diesel yard tractors can model the switch by converting the highest-utilisation units first and measuring the real energy bill against the diesel baseline. The i5 near-200-kilometre design range is comfortably above the few kilometres a shuttle covers in a shift, which means battery degradation rather than daily range is the factor that sets replacement timing over a ten year horizon, and that horizon is predictable enough for a finance lease to be structured against it.

The comparison is clearest when the terminal expresses both options in the same currency per container move rather than per vehicle, because the electric unit shifts the cost centre from fuel pumps to the electricity tariff and the amortised charger. A terminal that already pays a favourable industrial rate gains the largest advantage, while one on a peak-priced commercial tariff should model the charging schedule against time-of-use bands before committing the fleet to a full conversion that assumes cheap overnight power it may not actually receive.

Maintenance planning also changes shape with the electric shuttle. The i5 removes the periodic engine service entirely, which frees workshop bay time that a diesel yard fleet spends on oil and filter changes, but it introduces a high-voltage inspection discipline that the depot must train for and document. The net effect is usually fewer unplanned stops, because electric drivetrains have fewer wearing parts in the power path, yet the planned work that remains is more specialised and should not be underestimated when the maintenance contract is written at the start of the deployment.

Operator training and safety in mixed traffic

Electric shuttles are quiet, which is an advantage for the environment but a hazard in mixed yards where pedestrians may not hear an approaching vehicle. Training must cover the silence factor, the behaviour of regenerative braking, and the correct response to a low-charge warning that differs from a diesel low-fuel situation. Drivers should also learn the safe isolation procedure before any high-voltage inspection, even though routine checks remain within the standard pre-trip regime that every yard operator already performs at the start of a shift.

The i5 terminal shuttle is a pragmatic first step into yard electrification because it reuses a proven urban delivery chassis, fits the short repetitive cycles of port work, and gives terminal managers a measurable emissions and cost win without a full fleet gamble. Fleets that pilot the i5 on their busiest lane gain the duty data needed to electrify the rest of the yard with confidence and to size the charging estate against a real, observed load profile rather than a vendor forecast.

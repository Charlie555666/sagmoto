SAGMOTO E3 MAX vs X6: Medium vs Heavy Truck TCO Comparison Guide

2026-10-22Comparison Guide12 min read

Two Trucks, Two Different Jobs

The SAGMOTO E3 MAX and the SAGMOTO X6 are not competitors in the sense of one being better — they occupy different weight classes with different economic logic. The E3 MAX is a 12-18 tonne GVWR medium truck with 160-220 HP, built for distribution, agricultural and urban-materials work. The X6 is a 31-40 tonne GVWR heavy truck, offered as 6x4 or 8x4 with WP10/WP12 engines producing 350-430 HP and up to 2,000 N·m of torque, built for bulk, construction and mine-support haulage. The real question a fleet manager faces is not "which is better" but "where is the tonnage breakpoint at which the heavier, more expensive X6 actually costs less per tonne moved?"

This comparison answers that with hard numbers and a duty-based decision table. We deliberately avoid vague advice; the goal is to let you size your fleet to the work instead of over-buying iron you cannot load.

The X6 only wins on unit economics when you can consistently load it above roughly 18-20 tonnes. Below that payload, you are hauling dead weight in chassis, tyres and fuel — and the E3 MAX will deliver a lower cost per tonne-kilometre every time.

Purchase Price and Fixed Cost Gap

The ex-works price of an X6 is materially higher than an E3 MAX, and so are the fixed costs that follow it: heavier tyres, higher insurance, a heavier licence class and greater scheduled-maintenance parts. But fixed cost is only half the story; a truck that carries 24 tonnes instead of 11 tonnes moves more revenue per trip, and that scales the effective cost down.

Read the table as a ratio, not an absolute, because regional landed costs vary. The decisive metric is not the purchase price but the cost per tonne-kilometre, which we model next.

Cost per Tonne-Kilometre: Finding the Breakpoint

Assume a representative mixed route of 150 km one way. The E3 MAX at 10 tonnes payload burns less fuel and costs less to run, but moves 10 tonnes. The X6 at 24 tonnes moves 2.4 times the payload for roughly 1.5 times the fuel and 1.8 times the fixed cost. The arithmetic favours the X6 only when payload stays high. We estimate the crossover sits around 18 tonnes of consistent load: below it, E3 MAX is cheaper per tonne-km; above it, the X6 pulls ahead.

This is why duty profile matters more than price. A fleet doing 11-tonne distribution loops should never buy an X6 to "have spare capacity" — the spare capacity is a perpetual tax. Conversely, a fleet moving 25-tonne aggregate daily is leaving money on the table with an E3 MAX that needs three trips to the X6's one.

Mix the fleet instead of maxing one class. The cheapest real-world answer is often a base of E3 MAX units for light and variable work plus a smaller number of X6 units for the heavy, consistent lanes — not a single homogeneous fleet.

Driver, Licence and Resale Considerations

The X6 requires a heavy-vehicle licence class and typically a more experienced driver, which carries a wage premium and a narrower hiring pool in many markets. The E3 MAX can be driven by a medium-licence operator, widening your recruitment and reducing training cost. Resale also differs: E3 MAX units hold value in the broad distribution and agricultural secondary market, while X6 units resell best to mining and construction buyers — a narrower but stable segment.

Maintenance commonality is a point in SAGMOTO's favour. The X6 shares the WP engine family with the broader heavy range and the E3 MAX shares the lighter platform's parts within the medium family, so a mixed fleet still keeps two compact parts bins rather than five. For context on the heavier end, the SAGMOTO dump truck models 6x4 8x4 sits alongside the X6 in the same heavy-weight logic.

Five-Year Total Cost of Ownership Model

A purchase decision made on sticker price alone is a decision made on the smallest number. Over a five-year horizon the acquisition cost typically represents only 25-35 percent of the lifetime cost; fuel, tyres, maintenance and residual value dominate. Model the two trucks on the same annual tonnage and the breakpoint becomes obvious. If your annual moved tonnage is low and variable, the E3 MAX's lower fuel and tyre burn and its stronger residual in the broad secondary market win clearly. If your tonnage is high and steady, the X6's per-trip revenue advantage compounds and overtakes its higher running cost well inside the five-year window.

The discipline is to build the model on your own lane data, not a brochure claim. Take your dominant lane's distance, your realised payload, your local diesel price and your actual tyre life, then compute cost per tonne-kilometre for each platform. Fleets that run this simple spreadsheet almost never regret the outcome, because the numbers expose the idle-capacity tax that kills so many over-specced X6 purchases.

Tax, Duty and Environmental Factors

Import duty and VAT are applied on the customs value, so a more expensive X6 attracts proportionally higher upfront tax — another point against buying heavy iron you cannot load. Some markets also tier road-user charges and axle-weight levies by GVWR, which can add a recurring cost to the X6 on public roads. Factor these into the five-year model rather than treating them as noise.

Emissions and noise rules are tightening across African metros, and the E3 MAX's smaller, lower-output engine is easier to keep compliant in urban low-emission zones than a 430HP X6. For a fleet doing significant in-city work, the E3 MAX's lighter regulatory footprint is a quiet but real advantage that rarely appears in a first-pass comparison.

Sizing a Mixed Fleet: A Worked Example

Consider a distributor moving 90 tonnes per day. Run it on E3 MAX units at 11 tonnes per trip and you need roughly eight trips; run it on X6 units at 24 tonnes and you need about four, but only if those lanes are consistently full. The realistic optimum is five E3 MAX units handling the variable 6-12 tonne distribution and city drops, plus two X6 units reserved for the steady 24-tonne bulk lane. That mix uses 7 vehicles instead of 8 all-heavy or 9 all-light, and it matches iron to tonnage on every lane.

The worked example also shows why "one size" fails: an all-X6 fleet wastes capacity on light drops and an all-E3 MAX fleet triples trip count on bulk. The mixed fleet is the only configuration where every truck spends most of its life in its efficient payload band, which is the entire point of the comparison. Re-run the same logic on your own daily tonnage split and the correct ratio of E3 MAX to X6 falls out of the arithmetic.

One caution: do not let the mixed ratio drift toward the X6 out of a desire for "headroom." Idle capacity on a heavy truck is a permanent cost, not a safety margin, and the spreadsheet will flag it the moment a lane's average load drops below the 18-tonne crossover. Keep the ratio disciplined and revisit it every six months as your tonnage profile changes.

Conclusion

Choose the SAGMOTO E3 MAX when your loads are mostly under 18 tonnes, variable, or urban — its lower fixed cost and lighter footprint win on cost per tonne-kilometre. Choose the SAGMOTO X6 when you can reliably load 20 tonnes or more on a consistent lane, where its payload advantage overwhelms its higher purchase and running cost. Most profitable fleets run both: E3 MAX for the light and variable, X6 for the heavy and steady. Size the iron to the tonnage, and the TCO takes care of itself.

SAGMOTO i9 Electric Trucks in Cote d'Ivoire: Urban Distribution Costs in Abidjan

2026-10-19Market Analysis11 min read

Abidjan is the West African city where electric trucks make the most commercial sense

Cote d'Ivoire is the largest economy in the Franc Zone of West Africa, and Abidjan is its logistics heart: a port city of several million people whose metropolitan distribution, cocoa logistics and container feed all move by road, in congestion, six days a week. That combination of fixed depots, mapped urban routes, severe traffic and diesel priced well above the world average is exactly the condition under which a battery-electric truck pays back. The SAGMOTO i9, a pure-electric distribution truck with a 131 kWh lithium iron phosphate pack, is the model worth putting against that brief.

This analysis works through the Abidjan urban-distribution case for the i9: what the 131 kWh LFP pack and the 250 to 320 km real-world range actually cover, how the total cost of ownership compares with diesel once electricity and diesel prices are set honestly, what the hot and humid climate does to battery life, and what the charging and import picture looks like for a Cote d'Ivoire fleet operator.

The i9 specification and what the numbers mean in Abidjan

The i9 carries a 131 kWh lithium iron phosphate battery and delivers real-world range of 250 to 320 km depending on load, duty cycle and ambient temperature. That is not a long-haul specification and is not meant to be; it is a regional distribution and port-logistics specification, and in Abidjan that covers most commercial work. Energy consumption in urban duty will typically land at 0.9 to 1.2 kWh per km when ambient heat, air-conditioning load and stop-start congestion are accounted for, which puts real range at the lower end of the band on a fully loaded, air-conditioned route and near the upper end on a moderate-load route with lighter congestion.

Total cost of ownership: electricity versus diesel in Abidjan

The honest way to compare an electric truck with a diesel truck is not sticker price; it is total cost per kilometre over a defined ownership period. Diesel is the largest single line in any Abidjan urban distribution budget, and its price in Cote d'Ivoire sits well above the world average once logistics and scarcity are included. Electricity, even at commercial industrial tariffs, costs a fraction of diesel per unit of delivered work, and the gap widens on congested routes where a diesel truck idles and an electric truck recovers energy through regenerative braking.

Second, the uncertainty to model honestly is battery residual value, because Cote d'Ivoire has no established used-electric-truck trade; prudent fleets treat end-of-term battery value as a range rather than a number and secure capacity-retention terms in writing at the point of order.

Hot and humid climate: what it does to the battery

Abidjan is hot and humid for most of the year, with ambient temperatures commonly in the low thirties and high relative humidity. Lithium iron phosphate chemistry is the right choice for this climate for three reasons. It tolerates high ambient temperature better than nickel-based chemistries, which matters when a truck sits in 33 degree heat with solar load in a depot yard. It has a long cycle life relative to the duty, which matters when the business case depends on spreading battery capital over many years. And it is more forgiving of deep discharge and of irregular charging windows, which matters where grid supply can be intermittent.

The specification response is thermal management. Confirm the battery cooling capacity for sustained 33 to 35 degree ambient and for slow-speed operation in traffic, because an electric truck in Abidjan stop-start duty generates heat in the pack even at low speed. A depot that shades its charge points and manages charge scheduling to cooler night hours will protect pack life materially better than one that charges in full sun at midday.

Key point: LFP is the correct chemistry for Abidjan's heat and humidity. Protect pack life with shaded charge points, night charging and confirmed cooling capacity for 35 C ambient; do not charge in full solar load.

Cocoa and port logistics: the duty cycles that fit

Cote d'Ivoire is the world's largest cocoa producer, and the crop's logistics chain - moving beans from inland collection points to Abidjan port warehouses and then to container terminals - is a natural electric-truck candidate on the shorter, fixed legs. Port drayage between the Abidjan container terminals and inland depots is another strong fit, because the routes are mapped, the distances are moderate, and the vehicles return to base. FMCG and retail distribution from a fixed warehouse to urban drop points completes the picture.

The applications that do not fit are equally clear: inter-city trunk haulage beyond roughly 150 km one way without mid-route charging, remote supply where no depot charging exists, and any duty where daily mileage is unpredictable. A fleet that matches the i9 to a mapped route will hit its modelled payback; a fleet that runs it on a diesel duty cycle will be disappointed. Buyers evaluating the wider option set should look at the full SAGMOTO new energy electric trucks range rather than a single model, because the decision is usually a fleet-mix one: electric units on mapped urban routes, diesel units on everything else.

Charging infrastructure and the depot question

The binding constraint on an i9 deployment in Cote d'Ivoire is charging, not vehicle availability. Abidjan's grid is more reliable than in many regional neighbours, but industrial connection capacity and depot electrical upgrade lead time must be planned before trucks are ordered. The workable configuration is a grid connection sized to the fleet's nightly demand, with charging scheduled to off-peak windows where the tariff structure permits, and a managed load that avoids tripping the depot supply during peak commercial hours.

For a ten-truck fleet each consuming 90 to 130 kWh per night, the nightly depot demand is substantial but tractable. The practical answer is to design the depot charging as a project, with a qualified electrical contractor, a defined connection capacity and a charge-management system, rather than to bolt chargers onto an existing outlet. Fleets should also plan around payload, confirming that each candidate route is volume-limited rather than weight-limited before committing battery mass.

Import duty and the Franc Zone settlement

Cote d'Ivoire operates within the West African Economic and Monetary Union and uses the CFA franc, a currency pegged to the euro. That removes the exchange-rate volatility that complicates truck imports elsewhere in Africa and lets a buyer model landed cost in a stable currency. Import duty, VAT and the union's common external tariff apply to a CBU electric truck, and the declared specification on the invoice must match the vehicle exactly, because the clearing process treats a mismatch between invoice, chassis number and road test as a clearance delay.

The commercial structure therefore runs through confirmed letters of credit and the regional banking system, with transaction cost sitting in confirmation fees and tenor rather than in currency risk. The honest framing for a board is this: on mapped high-utilisation urban routes in Abidjan, the i9 pays back its incremental cost over a four- to six-year horizon on energy and maintenance alone, before any brand or carbon benefit, and it does not pay back on long or unpredictable routes at all.

Service capability and the high-voltage question

An electric truck has far fewer wear items than a diesel truck - no engine oil, filters, injectors, turbocharger or aftertreatment system - but it has high-voltage systems that require trained technicians and diagnostic tooling that general workshops do not hold. The practical answer is a service agreement at the point of order, technician training delivered with the first batch, and a critical spares kit covering contactors, fuses, coolant pump and charge components. Fleets should also specify battery warranty terms in writing, including capacity retention at a defined cycle count, because that is the term that protects the residual-value assumption.

Conclusion

Cote d'Ivoire is not an early-adopter market for electric trucks by choice; it is one by economics. High diesel prices, severe urban congestion, fixed depots and mapped distribution routes around Abidjan are exactly the conditions under which a 131 kWh LFP truck with 250 to 320 km of real range makes commercial sense, provided the fleet solves charging and service before it solves vehicle selection.

For an Abidjan fleet operator, the next step is a route audit: measure daily distance, payload, stop count and congestion before choosing the vehicle. Electrify measured routes, not assumed ones, and the i9 will size the depot charger correctly and hit its modelled payback. Model the truck against your current diesel cost per kilometre, and the answer for most metropolitan distribution and cocoa-port legs will be clear.

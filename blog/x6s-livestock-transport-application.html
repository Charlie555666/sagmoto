SAGMOTO X6s Livestock Transport Body and Welfare Configuration Guide

2026-11-04Industry Application9 min read

Why the X6s chassis works for livestock haulage

The SAGMOTO X6s medium-heavy chassis carries a factory rating of 270 to 300 horsepower from either the Cummins ISD or the Weichai WP7 engine family, which places it directly in the weight class that regional abattoirs, feedlots and livestock agents use for daily cattle and sheep movement. A typical livestock build keeps the wheelbase near 5,600 mm so the crate body stays within legal overall length while leaving adequate rear overhang for a hydraulically operated tail ramp. Operators running between farms and regional saleyards usually cover 120 to 260 km per one-way trip, so the X6s torque curve matters more than peak speed because most climbing happens fully loaded on single-lane rural roads.

Compared with lighter municipal chassis the X6s gives the structural reserve needed for a steel crate that can weigh 1,800 to 2,400 kg empty before any animal enters. That reserve shows up as reduced frame flex when the deck is unevenly loaded with restless stock, which in turn lowers the risk of fatigue cracks at the body mounting points over a five to seven year service life. The 24,000 kg gross vehicle weight rating leaves room for a 2,100 kg crate plus 13,500 kg of live cargo while keeping the front axle within its 6,500 kg limit on the drive to the yard.

Specify the X6s with the air-suspended cab and the engine exhaust brake when the route mixes descending gravel passes with loaded cattle, because controlled descent speed reduces animal slip injuries more than any single floor feature.

Cattle and sheep crate body configurations

Two crate layouts dominate the X6s livestock application. The single-deck cattle crate uses 1,200 mm high side rails with vertical bars spaced at 100 mm to prevent heads from slipping through, and it suits animals above 300 kg where a second level would exceed deck height limits. The double-deck sheep crate stacks two 900 mm compartments with a removable centre floor, lifting total carrying area from roughly 18 square metres to 34 square metres while holding overall height under 4,000 mm for low bridges and underpass clearance.

Deck loading starts from the rear ramp and moves animals forward, with the heaviest beasts placed over the rear axle group to keep the front axle within its 6,500 kg limit. A centre partition that swings on a top hinge lets the driver split a mixed load, and the partition locks with a pin rather than a latch so vibration cannot release it during transit. The partition also doubles as a moving wall that pushes stock toward the unloading gate without a handler entering the pen.

Ventilation and animal welfare practice

Live cargo generates heat and moisture that must leave the crate faster than it builds, especially when ambient temperature passes 28 degrees Celsius. The X6s livestock body uses a roof vent row of adjustable louvres running the full crate length, giving a free airflow opening of around 0.9 square metres, supplemented by side vents at 400 mm above the deck so stale air is drawn upward and out rather than recirculated at animal height. A roof scoop ahead of the first pen captures slipstream even at low speed so the system works in stockyard queues where the truck is barely moving.

Stocking density good practice keeps each adult beef animal on at least 0.55 to 0.7 square metres of deck and each mature sheep on 0.3 to 0.4 square metres, figures that should be treated as planning minimums rather than targets because restless stock need room to shift footing. Journey time planning should budget a rest stop with water every 8 to 10 hours of continuous movement, and during summer the standing time at saleyards should stay under 2 hours before unloading to avoid heat stress in densely packed pens. Drivers log the ambient temperature at each stop so a welfare officer can reconstruct the conditions of any given trip.

Set the roof louvres fully open whenever ambient temperature exceeds 25 degrees and keep a spare side-vent panel aboard, because a jammed vent on a hot afternoon is the single most common cause of welfare complaints on livestock runs.

Suspension and ride quality for live cargo

The X6s leaf-spring rear suspension can be ordered with auxiliary air bags that trim vertical acceleration on the deck from roughly 1.4 g over a pothole to about 0.9 g, a difference animals feel as fewer sudden drops that trigger panic trampling. Front parabolics with a softer rate than the construction tipper specification further isolate the cab and the front of the crate from sharp inputs on broken bitumen. The air bags are driver-adjustable from the dashboard so the same chassis can run firm when empty and soft when fully loaded.

Ride quality is not only comfort. Cattle that arrive with bruised hindquarters from repeated deck impacts lose value at the scale, and sheep that spend a trip fighting for balance burn the live-weight margin a seller depends on. A smooth setup therefore protects revenue rather than just compliance, and many operators report 1 to 2 percent better delivered live weight after switching from a stiff tipper chassis to the air-assisted X6s configuration. The improvement is largest on routes that mix fresh asphalt with degraded rural seals inside a single 200 km leg.

Wash-down and hygiene regime

Biosecurity starts the moment the previous load leaves the yard. The X6s crate should be pressure-washed at 70 to 90 bar with water above 60 degrees Celsius to strip organic matter, then disinfected with a registered product and left to dry for at least 30 minutes before the next animal boards. A sealed floor with a central drainage channel that exits behind the rear axle keeps effluent off the chassis, and the channel slope should be no less than 1 in 40 so slurry does not pool under the deck and accelerate frame corrosion.

A weekly deep clean adds a foam contact wash to the bar and rail surfaces where bacteria colonise, and the water tank should hold at least 600 litres so a full wash needs no external refill between remote farms. Operators moving between properties with different health status log each wash with time and location, because that record is what an inspector asks for during a disease investigation. The 600 litre tank also feeds a deck misting nozzle for heat management, so one plumbing circuit serves both hygiene and welfare.

Ramp, non-slip flooring and loading safety

The hydraulically lowered tail ramp settles at a 14 to 18 degree angle for cattle and a slightly steeper 20 to 22 degree angle for sheep that move more willingly on a confident slope, and the ramp surface carries a welded chequer plate plus removable rubber strips for wet-day grip. Inside the crate the floor uses ribbed aluminium with 10 mm standing ribs at 120 mm pitch, which gives hooves purchase without the bruising of bare steel grid. The rubber strips clip off for pressure washing so the grip surface itself stays sanitary.

Loading should happen from a level yard wherever possible because a ramp angled against a slope doubles the climb effort for heavy beasts and raises the chance of a fall. A side access gate at the front of the crate lets the handler reach an animal that goes down without walking the full deck, and the gate latch is a two-step release so stock cannot shoulder it open mid-transit. The gate opening is wide enough for a single animal but narrow enough that two cannot bolt through together during an unloading rush.

Rural traction, water and feed provisioning

Many livestock movements finish on unsealed laneways where the X6s 6x4 drive and a rear diff lock earn their keep, particularly after rain when a two-wheel-drive chassis would cut its own ruts. Tyre choice matters as much as driven axles, and a 11R22.5 rib tread at 6.5 bar front and 7.5 bar rear balances field grip with saleyard tarmac wear. The diff lock engages from a cab switch and should be used only below 15 km/h to avoid driveline wind-up on a sealed surface.

Axle load distribution with a fully loaded crate

A 24,000 kg GVW X6s with a 2,100 kg steel crate and 14,500 kg of stock must keep the front axle at or below 6,500 kg and the rear tandem within 18,000 kg. Placing 60 percent of live weight over the rear group and 40 percent mid-body achieves this, but a lopsided pen layout can shift 800 kg onto one rear wheel and push that tyre past its 3,150 kg rating on a corner. The driver should walk the deck after loading and shift one or two animals if the chassis visibly leans.

SAGMOTO X7 vs E3: Which Medium-Duty Truck Fits Your Fleet?

September 10, 2026Comparison Guide8 min read

Within the SAGMOTO lineup, the X7 and E3 occupy overlapping territory in the medium-duty segment, and fleet buyers frequently ask which one they should choose. The two trucks share a philosophy — simple, robust, parts-friendly platforms for emerging-market duty — but they are engineered for different centers of gravity. The X7 is the heavier, more torque-focused truck built for full loads on demanding roads; the E3 is the lighter, more economical distribution specialist. Choosing correctly between them is worth more than any negotiation discount: a mis-specified medium-duty truck costs money every single operating day. This guide breaks the comparison down into the factors that actually decide fleet performance.

Positioning at a Glance

The fundamental difference is duty-class. The X7 ranges from 12 to 18 tonnes GVW with engine options up to 210 HP and 780 Nm of torque — a genuine heavy-medium truck. The E3 spans roughly 8 to 16 tonnes GVW with the YCS04 engine family topping out at 185 HP and 650 Nm. In practical terms: the X7 is built for jobs where the truck is regularly full and the terrain fights back; the E3 is built for jobs where speed of delivery, fuel economy, and maneuverability dominate.

Engine Character: Torque Where It Matters

The X7's YC4E 210 HP engine deserves specific attention. Its 780 Nm of peak torque, delivered low in the rev range, transforms the truck's capability on gradients. A 16-tonne GVW X7 carrying a full load of construction materials up an 8 percent grade holds speed where the E3 185 HP — with 130 Nm less torque — will lose momentum and force downshifts. Over a day of mountain distribution work, that difference compounds into meaningful schedule time and clutch wear.

The E3's YCS04 counters with efficiency and smoothness. Its four-cylinder design is lighter, its fuel consumption runs 2–3 L/100km lower in comparable duty, and its parts costs are the lowest in the SAGMOTO medium-duty range. For urban FMCG distribution where trucks run 150–250 km per day at moderate loads, the YCS04's economics are hard to beat.

Key Insight: The most common specification mistake fleet buyers make is choosing the E3 to save USD 4,000–5,000 per unit on acquisition, then running it at full GVW on hilly routes. The overloaded-duty fuel penalty, accelerated clutch and brake wear, and slower cycle times typically erase the savings within the first two years. Match the truck to the route's sustained grade and the load's regularity, not to the best-case duty cycle.

Chassis and Body Considerations

The X7's heavier frame and suspension components are designed around 16–18 tonne operations, which produces a secondary benefit: durability headroom when the truck runs at 12–14 tonnes. Fleets that mix route types appreciate this — an X7 running light urban routes is over-engineered but lasts; an E3 running heavy rural routes is under-engineered and fails early. Body options are similar across both (box, stake, refrigerated, water tanker, tipper), but the X7 additionally supports heavier vocational bodies such as 8 m³ concrete mixer drums and heavier compaction equipment, which are beyond the E3's PTO and frame ratings.

For mixer and municipal applications, the X7's YC4D 180 HP configuration with mixer drum has become a popular small-batch concrete solution in secondary cities where a full-size mixer chassis is uneconomical — a segment in which the E3 simply cannot participate.

Cab, Comfort, and Driver Factors

Both trucks offer practical, easy-clean cabs designed for working environments rather than showroom appeal. The differences are marginal but real: the X7's cab sits on a slightly heavier chassis with better isolation, giving a more settled ride at full load; the E3's lighter package gives it a maneuverability edge in dense urban streets and market-area loading zones. Both offer air conditioning, and both use controls and instruments that any experienced driver operates without training. In markets where driver retention matters, the X7's fuller torque reserve reduces driver fatigue on demanding routes — an intangible but frequently cited preference among drivers who have used both.

Fuel Economics Compared

Fuel is the largest operating cost for both trucks, and the comparison is duty-dependent. The table below shows indicative annual fuel cost at 70,000 km per year with diesel at USD 1.00/litre:

Duty Profile X7 Fuel Cost / Year E3 Fuel Cost / Year
Urban distribution, moderate loads USD 11,900 (17 L/100km) USD 9,800 (14 L/100km)
Regional mixed routes USD 13,300 (19 L/100km) USD 11,200 (16 L/100km)
Hilly rural, full loads USD 14,000 (20 L/100km) USD 12,600 (18 L/100km, stressed)

On flat urban duty, the E3 saves roughly USD 2,000 per year in fuel — recovering its lower purchase price quickly. On hilly full-load duty, the gap narrows to USD 1,400, and the E3's stressed operation adds maintenance costs that flip the total-cost comparison in the X7's favor.

Maintenance and Parts

Both trucks are engineered for low-cost maintenance, but their parts ecosystems differ in reach. The YCS04's parts are inexpensive and increasingly available through Chinese-truck parts channels in Africa, Southeast Asia, and Latin America. The Yuchai YC4D/YC4E family benefits from Yuchai's enormous global installed base — these engines power everything from generators to marine units — making YC4 parts arguably the most widely distributed medium-duty engine parts in the developing world. In remote operating regions, this parts depth is a decisive factor, and it is the strongest single argument for the X7 where both trucks are technically adequate.

Service intervals are similar (10,000 km oil changes under normal duty, shortened for severe duty), and both use conventional, workshop-friendly layouts with no proprietary diagnostics required — any competent diesel workshop can maintain either truck with a standard toolkit and a parts supply.

Decision Framework

  • Choose the X7 when: routes include sustained gradients; loads regularly exceed 12 tonnes; the truck will carry vocational bodies (mixer, heavy compaction, large water tanker); the operating region favors Yuchai's parts network; or fleet duty cycles are mixed and unpredictable.
  • Choose the E3 when: duty is predominantly urban or regional-flat; loads are moderate and predictable (FMCG, parcel, light construction supply); fuel economy dominates contract competitiveness; purchase capital is tightly constrained; or high daily frequency with low tonnage defines the work.
  • Choose a mixed fleet when: operations span both profiles — a common and efficient pattern is one X7 per three E3s in distribution fleets that occasionally carry heavy or hilly assignments.

The E3 remains the cheaper truck over five years in mixed duty — as it should be, being the lighter tool. The X7's justification is not cost parity but capability: it does work the E3 cannot, and on the routes where that capability is needed, the alternative to an X7 is not an E3 but a heavier and more expensive truck altogether.

For operators still undecided after route analysis, a practical compromise exists: pilot both. A two-truck pilot — one E3, one X7 — run for a full quarter over the fleet's actual route mix generates decision data no specification sheet can provide: measured fuel per route, driver preference, maintenance events, and true cycle times. The pilot's cost is trivial against a ten-unit commitment, and Fenghan Trading supports pilot purchases with the same configuration service and documentation as fleet orders. Many of our medium-duty customers began exactly this way.

Conclusion

The X7 and E3 are not competitors so much as complements spanning the medium-duty spectrum. The E3 is the efficiency benchmark for urban and regional distribution; the X7 is the capability benchmark for heavy-medium duty, vocational bodies, and demanding terrain. Fleet buyers should specify from their route profiles outward — grade, load regularity, body requirements, and parts geography — and let those factors choose the truck.

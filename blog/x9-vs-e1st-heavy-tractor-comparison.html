SAGMOTO X9 vs E1st: Heavy Tractor Choice for Long Haul and Site Work

2026-11-24Comparison Guide9 min read

This comparison needs a clear framing at the outset, because the SAGMOTO X9 and the E1st are not direct substitutes. The E1st is the flagship long-haul tractor, powered by a Cummins Z14 producing 560 HP and 2,550 N m through a 16-speed AMT or manual gearbox in a 6x4 layout. The X9 is a four-wheel-drive platform family, spanning light 4x4 chassis from 4.5 t GCW to heavier variants around 25 t GCW. The real question is therefore linehaul efficiency versus site and off-road capability, not which truck has the bigger engine.

Duty cycle: linehaul versus site work

Long-haul operation rewards low fuel burn, high average speed and driver comfort over long stretches. The E1st is built for that: a 560 HP flagship with GCW capability quoted from 18 to 100 t, a deep sleeper cab and gearing tuned for steady cruising. Its 16-speed AMT keeps the Z14 near its efficiency sweet spot across varying gradients, reducing the fuel penalty of hill work without demanding constant manual shifting from the driver.

Site and off-road work asks different questions. A quarry or remote construction access road needs traction on loose surfaces, low-range gearing and a chassis that tolerates twist. That is the X9 domain. Its all-wheel-drive layout puts torque at all four wheels, and the shorter wheelbase and higher ground clearance of the platform suit ramps and uneven ground where a long-wheelbase 6x4 tractor would scrape its belly or bog down in soft material.

Driveline torque capacity and gradeability

Work a starting-grade example to make the comparison concrete. For the E1st, take 2,550 N m of engine torque, a first gear ratio near 13.5, a final drive of 3.7 and a driveline efficiency of 0.85, with a loaded tyre radius around 0.51 m. Tractive effort is torque times the product of the ratios and efficiency divided by radius, giving roughly 212 kN. At a 49 t gross combination that is about 44 percent of weight, which corresponds to a low-gear starting grade ability in the mid-20 degree range.

For a heavier X9 variant with roughly 1,200 N m, an 8-speed first gear near 7, a transfer-case low ratio of 2.0, a 4.5 final drive and 0.85 efficiency on a 0.5 m radius, tractive effort climbs to about 129 kN. Against a 25 t machine that is over 50 percent of weight. The number looks higher, but the meaningful advantage is that all four wheels are driven, so the X9 converts that effort into motion on a surface where a 6x4 would simply spin its rear tyres.

Key point: The E1st delivers more absolute torque at 2,550 N m for heavy linehaul, but the four driven wheels of the X9 and its low-range transfer case make it the better choice where traction, not engine output, is the binding constraint.

Fuel burn and cost per kilometre

Fuel is where the two separate most clearly. At 40 t on a rolling highway cycle a 560 HP flagship typically records around 32 to 38 L per 100 km, while a lighter X9 on site duties might run 20 to 28 L per 100 km. The X9 looks cheaper per litre consumed, but it also covers far fewer kilometres per shift and carries less, so cost per tonne-kilometre favours the tractor on long routes.

A fair model uses your own duty cycle. Multiply annual kilometres by fuel consumption and price, then add tyres and scheduled maintenance. Linehaul fleets will usually find the E1st wins on cost per tonne-kilometre despite higher absolute burn, because one tractor moves far more freight between cities. Site operators see the opposite pattern, where the ability to reach an awkward bench matters more than the fuel curve.

Key point: The E1st lower cost per tonne-kilometre on highways comes from moving far more freight per shift, not from a smaller fuel burn; on a short site cycle the X9 is the more economical tool.

Resale and service network

Resale depends on the buyer pool in your region. A 6x4 flagship tractor such as the E1st is widely understood by long-haul operators and financiers, which supports residual values where highway freight is strong. A four-wheel-drive X9 platform sells into a narrower but loyal niche of construction, mining and municipal buyers, and residual strength there depends on local site activity rather than on national freight volumes.

Service network should be checked at the variant level. Both trucks use Cummins powertrains where fitted and Fast gearboxes across much of the range, but the X9 transfer case and the E1st 16-speed AMT have different spare requirements. Confirm that your nearest dealer stocks the wear items for the exact configuration before committing to a fleet purchase.

RHD, LHD and chassis configuration

Export markets add a further layer. Steering position, emission specification and axle ratios must match the registration rules of the destination country, and a tractor destined for cross-border linehaul needs a different lighting and document set from a site machine that never leaves a province. Confirm these details on the proforma invoice rather than assuming a standard build will clear customs.

Chassis configuration also affects payload and traction. The E1st is a 6x4 tractor intended to pull a trailer, so its fifth wheel height and kingpin setting must suit the trailers in your fleet. The X9 is chosen as a complete platform for bodies or equipment, so the relevant numbers are frame height, ground clearance and the location of the transfer case. These are different engineering questions and should be specified separately.

Driver comfort and cab comparison

Long-haul comfort is a measurable economic factor. A flagship such as the E1st is specified with a deep sleeper, a smooth-shifting AMT and cab isolation tuned for steady highway cruising, all of which help a driver complete long legs without excessive fatigue. Fleets that run two-driver crews report that cab quality influences recruitment, and recruitment cost is a real line in the operating budget that deserves more attention than it usually receives.

The X9 cab is designed around shorter site cycles rather than multi-day trips. It prioritises visibility, durability and ease of entry over sleeper volume, which suits a driver who climbs in and out many times a shift. Expecting a site platform to double as a long-haul sleeper usually disappoints both the driver and the fleet manager, so match the cab to the shift pattern rather than to the prestige of the model.

Tyre, brake and maintenance cost by duty cycle

Tyre cost tracks the surface. The E1st on highway runs large steer and drive tyres that wear slowly and can be retreaded, while the X9 on site uses heavier tread patterns that wear fast on rock and may be damaged by sharp edges. A cost model that uses a single tyre price for both trucks will understate the X9 operating cost on abrasive ground and overstate its apparent advantage.

Brake wear tells a similar story. Linehaul braking events are few and gentle, so the E1st drive axles may go a long service interval before linings need attention. Site work involves frequent stops and gradient descents, which heat brakes and shorten lining life on the X9. Regenerative braking is not available on these diesel platforms, so the difference is borne by consumables and by the time spent in the workshop.

Recovery, insurance and cross-border considerations

Insurance and recovery costs differ sharply by duty cycle. A flagship tractor on a long highway lane is straightforward to insure and, if it stops, straightforward to recover with a standard heavy recovery unit. A four-wheel-drive X9 working a remote bench may require a specialised recovery vehicle and a longer call-out, which raises both the premium and the cost of a single incident. Include those figures in the ownership model rather than treating them as unlikely exceptions.

Cross-border work adds documents. A linehaul E1st crossing several countries needs customs carnets, consistent axle-load declarations and a cab that meets the destination emission rules. A site X9 rarely leaves one jurisdiction but may need temporary import permits and a clear description of special equipment on the invoice. Budget for the administrative time as well as the fees, because a truck held at a border earns nothing while the paperwork is resolved.

For fleets operating in more than one country, build a small matrix of the documents each model needs on each lane. It is a tedious exercise done once and it prevents the far more tedious exercise of unloading a truck at a border crossing to prove what it is carrying.

Neither truck is a compromise version of the other. They solve different transport problems, and a fleet that understands which problem it has will rarely regret the choice.

Choosing the right tool for the route

In practice many operators end up needing both types. A contractor may use X9 units to move plant and material inside a mine while contracting E1st tractors for the long leg to a port. Treating them as complementary tools, each specified for its own duty cycle, avoids the common mistake of buying a flagship tractor for work it will never see or a site platform for highway miles it cannot economically cover.

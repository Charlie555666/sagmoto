SAGMOTO X5 Payload and Frame Deep Dive: Rated GVW Versus Real Load

2026-11-24Product Deep Dive9 min read

Payload figures on a sales sheet rarely match the load a truck can legally and safely carry. The SAGMOTO X5 is a 4x2 medium cargo chassis rated in the 10 to 18 t GVW band with Cummins or Yuchai engines around 180 to 240 HP, and the gap between its brochure payload and a real operating load comes from body mass, fuel, tools and the way the axles share weight. This deep dive sets out the arithmetic that turns a GVW figure into a usable payload, then checks frame strength and tyre capacity against it.

GVW, curb mass and usable payload

Gross vehicle weight is the total the chassis may weigh on the road, fixed by the axle ratings and by registration. Usable payload is GVW minus curb mass, minus the body, minus fuel, tools and driver. A 4x2 X5 with an 18 t GVW and a 6.5 t curb mass leaves 11.5 t before the body is fitted. Fit a 2 t dropside and a full fuel tank and the real payload falls close to 9 t, not the headline figure.

This is why buyers should specify the body before signing. A steel tipper body can weigh a third more than an aluminium box, and that difference comes straight out of revenue-earning capacity. Ask the body builder for a weighed certificate rather than a design estimate, because a few hundred kilograms of overestimate compounds over hundreds of trips and erodes margin without anyone noticing.

Front and rear axle load split

GVW alone does not tell the whole story. The load must be distributed so neither axle exceeds its rating. A typical 4x2 X5 might carry 6 t on a 6.5 t front axle and 12 t on a 12 t rear axle at full GVW. Shifting the body forward by 300 mm moves roughly half a tonne onto the front axle, which can create a front overload even while the rear remains under its limit.

Under-loading the front axle is equally undesirable because it reduces steering stability and braking effectiveness on a wet road. Load distribution should be checked at both the lightest and heaviest operating conditions, and the check repeated with the body fitted and loaded at the intended centre of gravity height.

Key point: A 4x2 X5 can be legal at gross weight and still have an overloaded rear axle if the body sits too far back, so the axle split deserves more attention than the GVW headline.

Frame section modulus and where it is needed

For a 4x2 chassis the frame carries the body between the cab and the rear axle, and the governing property is again the section modulus of the rails. A channel around 250 mm deep with a 7 mm web gives roughly 180 to 200 cubic centimetres. With a 10 t payload distributed over a 4.5 m wheelbase, the mid-span moment is on the order of 55 kN m, which over a 190 cubic centimetre section yields about 290 MPa.

That figure is close enough to the yield of common chassis steel to justify reinforcement on the heaviest 18 t variant. A bolted or welded fishplate in the centre span restores margin, but it must be matched to the body mounting points so the load enters the reinforced region rather than ending just before it, which would shift the stress concentration to the transition.

Tyre load index and dual fitment

4x2 X5 variants usually run single rear tyres on lighter ratings and duals on the heavier end. A 245/70R17.5 tyre with load index 136/134 is rated around 2,800 kg single and 2,500 kg dual. Two on the front axle give 5,600 kg of nominal capacity, which only just covers a 6 t axle, so the heavier front tyre option is needed at full GVW rather than the base fitment.

The general rule is to leave at least ten percent headroom between summed tyre capacity and the rated axle. Tyres running near their limit generate more heat, wear faster and are far more sensitive to underinflation. Fleet records that track casing failures usually trace them back to a load index chosen on price rather than on the axle rating it was meant to serve.

Key point: The light 245/70R17.5 fitment on a 4x2 X5 needs upgrading at full GVW, because two tyres offer only 5,600 kg of capacity against a 6 t front axle rating.

Options that erode the payload figure

Curb mass is not fixed. A fleet specification that adds a steel body, a larger fuel tank, a spare wheel carrier, a rear lift and a tool box can consume more than a tonne before a single pallet is loaded. Each option is individually reasonable and collectively decisive. The disciplined approach is to maintain a mass schedule that lists every item with its weighed contribution, and to update it whenever a supplier substitutes a component during production.

Body material is the largest lever. An aluminium dropside can save several hundred kilograms against steel, and that saving is pure payload on every trip. The trade-off is repair cost and resistance to impact from a loading bucket, so the choice should follow the cargo. A fleet carrying bagged cement in sacks will not damage aluminium, while one handling scrap metal will destroy it within a season and should accept the heavier steel body.

Registration and insurance also follow the declared GVW band. A truck specified at 18 t carries higher annual charges in many jurisdictions than one at 12 t, which is a recurring cost that dwarfs the purchase-price difference. Select the lowest GVW that actually covers the route and reserve the heavier variant for operations where density and volume both demand it.

Suspension and axle rating interaction

Axle ratings assume a particular suspension and tyre combination. Fit a heavier spring pack to carry more load and the unladen ride becomes harsh, which accelerates body fastener loosening and driver fatigue. Fit softer springs for comfort and the axle can bottom out under a full load, transferring impact directly into the frame. The X5 specification should be chosen as a package of axle, spring and tyre, with the loaded and unladen states both checked on paper.

Leaf-spring suspension remains common on the 4x2 X5 because it is robust and cheap to repair. Its progressive rate means the last few hundred kilograms of load are carried at a higher effective stiffness, which is useful near GVW but reduces empty-ride quality. Air suspension is smoother but adds cost and complexity that is hard to justify on a medium cargo truck unless the cargo is delicate or the fleet runs mostly on poor surfaces.

Loading practice and driver training

Even a correctly specified X5 can be overloaded by poor loading practice. Dense material placed at the tail shifts weight off the front axle and onto the rear beyond its rating, while the same material spread evenly along the body stays within limits. Train loaders to distribute weight from the cab forward and to avoid concentrating the heaviest items at one end of the floor.

A second discipline is the daily tyre check. Pressure loss of ten percent raises rolling resistance and heat build-up, which lowers the effective load rating of the tyre. A simple gauge and a written pressure schedule checked before the first trip of the day cost very little and prevent the slow casing damage that surfaces later as a blowout.

Drivers should also be told what to do when a load looks borderline. Stopping and re-spacing a load takes minutes, whereas an overweight ticket or a damaged rear axle costs days. A clear rule that permits the driver to refuse an unsafe load is worth more than any onboard weighing device fitted after the event.

Weighbridge compliance and contract clauses

What matters commercially is the load that can be presented at a weighbridge without correction. Write the target payload, the body tare and the axle limits into the purchase contract so that the supplier, the body builder and the operator are working to one set of figures. Include a clause requiring a weighbridge ticket at delivery, because a certificate produced at the factory cannot account for fuel, spare wheel and the equipment a fleet adds before the truck enters service.

Drivers also need a clear loading rule. Mark the body at the permitted centre of gravity and train loaders to stop there, since even a correctly specified chassis becomes noncompliant when dense material is stacked at the tail. A simple painted line has prevented more overload fines than any onboard scale.

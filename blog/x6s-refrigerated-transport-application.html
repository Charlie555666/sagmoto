SAGMOTO X6s Refrigerated Transport: Chassis, PTO and Reefer Power

2026-11-24Industry Application9 min read

A refrigerated truck is two machines sharing one chassis: a vehicle that moves cargo and a cooling system that protects it. The SAGMOTO X6s, a medium-duty platform offered with Cummins or Weichai engines in the 240 to 300 horsepower band and roughly 950 to 1,200 Nm, is a common base for a reefer body because it has enough power and payload for a seven to ten metre insulated box without the cost of a heavy tractor. The specification that matters, however, is not the engine rating alone. It is how the refrigeration unit takes its power, how well the body holds temperature and how the operator accounts for the fuel that the reefer consumes. This guide works through those choices for a fleet buying refrigerated transport on the X6s chassis.

Cold-chain cargo punishes weak specification. A load that starts at the correct temperature can be spoiled by a slow pull-down, a leaking door seal or a reefer that loses power during a traffic delay. Unlike general cargo, a temperature excursion cannot be repaired at the destination; the value of the load is simply gone. A buyer therefore has to think about the truck as a system: engine and gearbox, power take-off or generator, insulation thickness, door hardware, airflow inside the box and the records that prove the temperature stayed in range. Each element is modest on its own, but a weakness in any one of them can ruin the whole delivery.

Key point: Choose the reefer power source before you choose the body builder. A chassis PTO, a direct-drive unit and an auxiliary generator each impose different demands on the engine, the wiring and the service schedule.

Chassis PTO, belt drive and direct-drive reefers

The most common way to power a reefer on a medium truck is a gearbox-mounted power take-off that drives a small alternator or a compact generator. The PTO draws mechanical power from the engine through the gearbox, and the generator feeds the refrigeration unit while the truck is running. This arrangement is simple, relatively inexpensive and avoids carrying a second engine, but it ties cooling capacity to engine speed: at a low idle the output may be limited, so the driver must manage engine revs during long stops. A PTO system also adds a seal and a shaft to the gearbox that need regular inspection, and the extra load raises fuel consumption by a noticeable amount over a full day.

A belt-driven compressor, taken from the engine crankshaft, is another route, usually on smaller boxes where the cooling demand is modest. It is mechanically simple and cheap to maintain, but it adds a belt load and a pulley at the front of the engine and reduces the flexibility to run the reefer when the truck is parked for a long period. A direct-drive reefer unit with its own dedicated engine is the most independent option: it can hold temperature while the truck is switched off, which matters for overnight parking or a long wait at a distribution centre. The trade-off is a second engine to service, more noise at the dock and its own diesel tank. The right choice depends on how long the truck stands still with a load on board.

Auxiliary generator sets and when they pay

An auxiliary generator set mounted on the chassis frame is an alternative to a PTO or a direct-drive reefer engine. It provides a stable electrical supply for an electric refrigeration unit regardless of the truck engine's speed, so the box can be held at temperature while the main engine is off. For a fleet that does overnight parking or waits for hours at a port or a cross-dock, that independence can protect cargo and reduce main-engine idling. A genset has its own fuel tank, service intervals and exhaust, and it adds weight and noise to the vehicle. The buyer must confirm that the frame has space for the unit and that the exhaust and fuel lines can be routed safely away from heat sources and the insulated body.

When comparing a genset with a PTO, calculate the full-day fuel and service picture rather than the purchase price alone. A PTO system uses the truck engine, which is efficient at converting fuel into power but must be kept running during a stop. A genset consumes its own diesel at a modest rate and spares the main engine the idle hours, which can reduce long-term engine wear. A direct-drive reefer sits between the two, with its own small engine and its own maintenance. Fleets carrying high-value pharmaceuticals or fresh produce often accept the extra complexity because a single temperature excursion costs far more than the additional equipment. For lower-value frozen goods on a short route, a well-maintained PTO system is usually sufficient.

Key point: An auxiliary genset earns its keep when the truck parks for hours with a load. If the route involves only short stops, a chassis PTO is simpler, lighter and cheaper to maintain.

Insulation, body thickness and door seals

The refrigeration unit can only hold temperature if the body keeps heat out. Insulation thickness is chosen from the cargo temperature range and the ambient climate: a chilled load at around two to eight degrees in a hot country needs a thicker wall than a frozen load moving through a mild one. A typical insulated body uses a closed-cell foam core between an outer skin and an inner liner, with thickness commonly in the range of 75 to 120 mm depending on the specification. Thicker walls cut heat ingress and reduce the duty on the reefer, but they also reduce internal volume and add weight, so the buyer needs to balance thermal performance against payload. corners, floor joints and the door frame are the weak points where heat leaks in, and they deserve as much attention as the panel thickness.

Door seals are the most neglected part of a refrigerated body. A compressed or torn seal lets warm air in every time the door is opened and lets cold air escape on the road. The driver can test a seal by closing a strip of paper in the door and feeling the resistance when it is pulled out; a weak grip at any point signals a seal that needs replacement. Door hardware should hold the seal under compression across the whole perimeter, and the hinges and latches need regular adjustment. A body that looks sound but has poor seals will make the reefer work continuously, raising fuel burn and shortening compressor life. Fleets that treat seals as a consumable item, replaced on a schedule rather than after a failure, keep their temperature records clean.

Pull-down time, temperature logging and compliance

Pull-down time is the period needed to bring the cargo and the air inside the box from the loading temperature to the target set point. A reefer should be run before loading so the box is already cold when the product goes in; putting warm cargo into a warm box and expecting the unit to catch up during the journey is a common cause of spoilage. Airflow matters as much as capacity: the load must be stacked so that air can circulate around and through the pallets, with a gap beneath the floor and clear space at the rear door. A driver who blocks the airflow with a tightly packed load will see a steady rise in temperature even though the reefer is running correctly.

Temperature logging provides the evidence that the cold chain held. A data logger records the box temperature at set intervals and produces a trace that can be shown to the customer or the regulator at delivery. Modern units can transmit an alert if the temperature leaves the allowed band, which lets a fleet intervene before the whole load is lost. For a compliance-driven customer, the ability to hand over a clean temperature record is as important as the truck itself. Buyers should confirm that the logger is calibrated, that its sensor is placed in a representative position and that the records can be downloaded in a format the customer accepts. A reefer truck without reliable logging is selling a promise it cannot prove.

Fuel burn, payload and the 240 to 300 horsepower question

The reefer adds a second fuel consumer to the truck. Depending on the power source and the outside temperature, refrigeration can account for a noticeable share of total diesel use, and on a hot day with frequent door openings that share rises sharply. A fleet should measure fuel per delivery and separate the reefer's contribution where possible, because a rising figure often points to a leaky seal, a dirty condenser or a failing compressor rather than to the engine. Keeping the condenser clean, the filters changed and the door seals tight is the cheapest way to control that line in the budget. Buyers reviewing the SAGMOTO cargo truck flatbed box stake range can compare body and chassis combinations to see how insulation weight affects available payload.

Is a 240 to 300 horsepower engine enough for a refrigerated body? In most cases yes. A reefer box is heavier and less aerodynamic than a plain cargo body, but the X6s in this power band has ample torque at medium speeds and the payload to carry a typical seven to ten metre insulated box. The engine is not being asked to accelerate like a tractor; it is maintaining a steady speed with a moderate load while the reefer takes its share of power through the PTO. A 240 horsepower unit suits a shorter box on a regional route, while a 300 horsepower unit is the safer choice for a longer body, hilly terrain or a hot climate where the reefer works hard. The decision should follow the gross vehicle weight and the route profile. Specified that way, the X6s delivers a refrigerated truck that protects the load without the running cost of an oversized engine.

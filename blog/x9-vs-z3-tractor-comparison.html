SAGMOTO X9 vs Z3 Tractor Truck: Which 6x4 Prime Mover Suits Your Fleet Better?

2026-10-19Product Comparison10 min read

The two SAGMOTO 6x4 tractors that fleets actually debate

Within the SAGMOTO range, two 6x4 prime movers sit close enough on paper that fleet buyers routinely ask which one to order. The X9 is built around a Weichai WP13 rated at 550 hp with 2,550 Nm of torque, engineered for gross combination weights reaching 100 tonnes in heavy-haul configuration. The Z3, detailed in the Z3 tractor truck 520HP Cummins M13 guide, is built around a Cummins M13 rated at 520 hp with 2,500 Nm, paired with the Fast Gear 12JSD240TA twelve-speed, and optimised for 40 to 49 tonne long-haul and regional corridors. Both are 6x4 tractors in the same SAGMOTO tractor trucks prime mover family, so cab architecture, air brakes, axles and aftersales are broadly shared. What differs is the duty each powertrain is built for, and that difference decides the five-year result.

This comparison works the decision the way a fleet engineer should: powertrain first, then gross combination weight and terrain, then fuel behaviour, then cab and parts, then the total cost of ownership. The conclusion is not that one is better; it is that each is the cheaper answer on a different lane, and the mistake is buying the wrong one for the work.

Powertrain comparison: WP13 550HP against M13 520HP

The headline gap is 30 hp and 50 Nm, but the commercial gap is in where the torque lives and what it lets the driver avoid. The X9's Weichai WP13 holds 2,550 Nm and is geared for heavy starts and sustained grades; the Z3's Cummins M13 holds a 2,500 Nm plateau from 1,000 to 1,400 rpm and is geared for relaxed cruising at 40 to 49 tonnes. The extra 50 Nm matters most above 55 tonnes and on long climbs, where the X9 holds a gear the Z3 would give up. Below 49 tonnes the difference is marginal and the Z3's lower acquisition and slightly lower fuel burn win.

Reading the torque figures correctly

The 50 Nm advantage is less important than the duty envelope. At 45 tonnes on flat highway the Z3 works at roughly 65 to 75 percent of capability, which is healthy and efficient; the X9 works at a lower load factor and returns almost identical consumption. Asked to hold 60 to 80 tonnes up a sustained grade, the Z3 climbs a gear lower at higher rpm, burning more fuel and adding thermal load, while the X9 keeps the reserve to stay in its efficient band. On a heavy-haul run the X9 may make 30 to 40 percent fewer downshift events than a Z3 on the same road, and each avoided downshift saves fuel, clutch material and driver fatigue.

Key point: Below 49 tonnes buy the Z3 for lower capital and lower cost per kilometre. Above 55 tonnes, on sustained grades or on contractually binding schedules, the X9 pays for itself in driveline life and schedule reliability.

GCW and typical duty: long-haul trunk versus heavy-haul

The gross combination weight envelope is the real selector. The Z3 is the correct answer when the route is flat or gently rolling and GCW sits below roughly 49 tonnes: national trunk routes, port-to-inland container feeds, cross-border desert highway and regional distribution at legal limits. The X9 takes over when GCW exceeds 55 tonnes, when grades run longer than two or three kilometres, or when schedule adherence on a climbing route is commercially binding. Heavy-haul permit work, multi-axle low-loader transport and mining concentrate haulage all fall into the X9 envelope, because the 100 tonne GCW rating reflects frame, axles, cooling and braking dimensioned for a range the Z3 is not built to enter.

Specify against the heaviest combination you will regularly pull, not the average. A fleet running 46 tonnes daily with occasional 55-tonne permit loads should buy the X9 rather than stretch the Z3, because near the top of a tractor's band, braking distances grow, tyre life falls and driveline wear accelerates. A Z3 worked at 55 tonnes every day is a tractor that will technically do the job while quietly costing more per kilometre for five years.

Fuel economy: where the litres actually go

Fuel is 30 to 40 percent of operating cost per kilometre, and the counter-intuitive part is that the more powerful truck is sometimes the more economical one on the right duty. The Z3 at 44 tonnes sits near its efficient island; move it to 60 tonnes or onto sustained grades and the driver holds lower gears at higher rpm, pushing the engine away from its optimum. The X9 at 60 tonnes remains near its own optimum, so the crossover where the X9 becomes the more fuel-efficient choice arrives as early as 50 tonnes on hilly routes.

Driver behaviour alone moves consumption 8 to 12 percent on either tractor, which is why telematics-based coaching belongs in the specification regardless of which model is chosen.

Cab, comfort and driver retention

Both tractors share SAGMOTO cab architecture, but the spec level usually differs by duty. The Z3 is most often ordered as a high-roof sleeper for multi-day Gulf and cross-border corridors, with stationary HVAC and a mattress good enough to protect driver retention on the Dammam-Dubai pattern. The X9 heavy-haul units are frequently specified with the same sleeper for long mining and permit corridors, because the operator lives in the truck for the length of the haul. The difference is that the X9's cab must withstand harder suspension input at higher GCW, so its mountings and seat damping are specified a step up. For most fleets the cab question is a trim question, not a structural one, and either platform can be built to the same sleeper standard.

Maintenance cost and parts commonality

This is where the two models are closer than buyers expect, which is good news for a mixed fleet. Both run conventional air-braked mechanical chassis with no proprietary electronic lockout, both use standard diagnostic protocols readable with widely available service tools, and both share the regional heavy-truck parts trade for filters, belts, seals and bearings. The X9's Weichai WP13 and the Z3's Cummins M13 use different filter and seal kits, but both are stocked by established distributor networks. The 12JSD240TA on the Z3 and the heavy-duty gearbox on the X9 share Fast Gear service philosophy, so a workshop trained on one is productive on the other. A fleet running both should standardise consumables and run one diagnostic platform, which keeps inventory and training complexity manageable.

Key point: Compare on cost per tonne-kilometre, not cost per kilometre. At 44 tonnes the Z3 is the cheaper unit to run; at 70 tonnes the X9 moves freight at roughly 17 percent lower cost per tonne-kilometre even though its per-km cost is higher.

The selection decision tree

Use this sequence to place your lane against the two envelopes. Fix the heaviest regular GCW, map the longest sustained grade, estimate annual kilometres, then choose.

  1. If your heaviest regular GCW is 49 tonnes or below and your grades are short, buy the Z3 and take the lower capital and the lower cost per kilometre.
  2. If your heaviest regular GCW exceeds 55 tonnes, or your route has sustained grades above two kilometres, buy the X9 for schedule reliability and driveline life.
  3. If you run both duty types, run a mixed fleet and put the X9 on the heavy or hilly lane and the Z3 on the flat lane; standardise consumables and diagnostics.
  4. If altitude is a factor, confirm the altitude calibration on the chosen engine, because both the WP13 and the M13 lose output at 4,000 m without it.
  5. Specify final drive against cruise speed and tyre size, not by habit; bad gearing costs more fuel over a vehicle's life than any option package costs to buy.

Conclusion

The X9 and the Z3 are not competing versions of the same truck. The X9, with its Weichai WP13 at 550 hp and 2,550 Nm, is engineered for the upper end of the duty spectrum, with GCW capability reaching 100 tonnes and the cooling, braking, frame and driveline to match; it is correct wherever payload exceeds 55 tonnes, wherever grades are sustained, and wherever schedule adherence on difficult terrain is commercially binding. The Z3, with its Cummins M13 at 520 hp and 2,500 Nm and the Fast Gear 12JSD240TA, is the lowest cost-per-kilometre answer for 40 to 49 tonne long-haul and regional work, and it is the more capital-efficient unit for most trunk-haul fleets.

For a fleet buyer the sequence is straightforward. Fix the heaviest regular GCW, map the longest sustained grade, estimate annual kilometres, then place the lane against the two envelopes. Inside the Z3 envelope, buy the Z3 and take the saving. Outside it, buy the X9, because the alternative is a tractor that will technically do the job while quietly costing more per kilometre for five years.

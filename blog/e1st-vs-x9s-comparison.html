SAGMOTO E1st vs X9s Tractor Comparison 2026

2026-11-06Comparison Guide9 min read

Fleet planners comparing the SAGMOTO E1st and the SAGMOTO X9s are not choosing between a good truck and a bad one; they are matching two deliberately different prime movers to two different jobs. Shaanxi Fenghan Trading Co., Ltd. positions the E1st as its 560 hp flagship for 100-tonne cross-continent work and the X9s as a 400-460 hp regional tractor for 60-tonne corridor and port duty. This comparison sets the numbers side by side so a buyer can see where each earns its place, and where overspending on the flagship would simply waste capital on power the lane never uses.

Overview: two SAGMOTO prime movers, two jobs

The E1st is built around a Cummins Z14 engine rated at 560 hp with a gross combination weight ceiling around 100 tonnes, aimed at heavy regional and long-haul trains where a 40-foot container plus a full fuel load and a steep grade demand unbroken reserve. The X9s uses a Weichai WP10 or WP12 engine in the 400-460 hp band with a GCW around 60 tonnes, aimed at port shunt, corridor freight and bulk moves where the extra tonnes of the E1st would sit idle. Both share the SAGMOTO cabin philosophy, Fast transmissions and 13-tonne-class rear axles, but they diverge the moment you look at engine displacement, cooling package and the suspension rating needed to carry the heavier train. In deployment terms the E1st is the unit you send on the 1,200 km copper corridor or the 90-tonne rig move, while the X9s is the unit you keep cycling on the 200 km port-to-inland-depots loop where it will accumulate more cycles per year and reward its lower running cost. Resale profiles also differ: the E1st holds value among heavy-corridor operators who need the Z14 specifically, while the X9s trades actively in the broader regional-tractor market because its power band suits the majority of African freight.

Key takeaway: pick by GCW, not by horsepower envy. If your train never exceeds 60 tonnes, the X9s does the job for roughly 30 percent less; if you regularly pull 90-100 tonnes, only the E1st has the reserve to do it safely.

Engine and drivetrain comparison

The gap between the two engines is not just horsepower; it is displacement, torque curve and the supporting cooling and braking systems. The Cummins Z14 in the E1st delivers its 560 hp with a long, flat torque plateau that holds on mountain passes without downshifting repeatedly, while the Weichai WP12 in the X9s peaks lower but is lighter, cheaper and more than adequate below 60 tonnes. Both use a Fast 12-speed manual or automated gearbox; the E1st's version is rated for higher input torque and pairs with a retarder as standard for long descent control. The E1st's cooling pack is sized for sustained 45°C ambient with a full grade climb, so its radiator and fan clutch are physically larger than the X9s's, and that extra thermal headroom is what lets it hold speed where a smaller engine would heat-soak and derate. The X9s, by contrast, benefits from the lighter Weichai's quicker warm-up and lower internal friction on short, cold-start-heavy port shifts, where the engine rarely reaches the temperatures where the E1st's oversized cooling earns its weight.

Payload, GCW and duty-cycle fit

Duty cycle is the deciding factor. The E1st justifies itself on lanes where the tractor plus trailer plus cargo regularly approaches 90-100 tonnes, such as mining equipment moves, bulk cement trains or triple-axle reefer work on long corridors. The X9s is the right call for a 40-foot container at legal corridor weights, a 30-tonne tipper train on a mine-to-plant loop, or port container shuffle where the unit may move only 50-150 km per trip. Running an E1st on a 55-tonne container lane wastes roughly 100-140 hp continuously and adds purchase, tyre and fuel cost for power that never engages. Conversely, asking an X9s to hold 95 tonnes on a 6 percent grade risks overheating and premature clutch wear. A useful field test is to weigh your three heaviest typical trains for a month: if the 90th-percentile train is under 65 tonnes, the X9s is the honest answer; if it sits above 85 tonnes, the E1st is the safe answer; only a fleet straddling the 65-85 tonne band faces a genuine toss-up, and even then the heavier bias usually wins because an underpowered tractor on a bad day is more expensive than a slightly overpowered one on a good day.

Matching rule: if your heaviest realistic train is under 65 tonnes, the X9s is the technically correct choice; if it is over 80 tonnes, the E1st is not optional, it is required for safe grading and braking margin.

Fuel economy and operating cost

The E1st burns more fuel in absolute terms because it carries a larger displacement and a heavier driveline, but on a heavy lane it can hold a higher average speed and spend less time in low gears, which narrows the per-tonne gap. The X9s returns better litres-per-100-km on light and medium work, typically in the 31-36 litre range loaded at 55-60 tonnes, while the E1st on a 90-tonne train sits nearer 42-48 litres per 100 km. The meaningful metric is fuel per tonne-km: on heavy lanes the E1st often wins, on light lanes the X9s wins decisively. Buyers should model their own lane rather than trust a single fleet's headline number, because the crossover point depends entirely on how close to GCW the truck actually runs. Tyre wear follows the same logic: the E1st's extra 8-10 tonnes of train weight scrubs drive-axle tyres faster on a heavy lane, but on a light lane that same weight is simply dead mass adding rolling resistance for no payload benefit, which is why the X9s's lighter footprint keeps its per-km rubber cost lower where it is actually used.

Cab, comfort and driver retention

Both cabs are left-hand drive with air-suspended seats and a rest bunk, because driver turnover is a real cost on multi-day corridors. The E1st adds a larger sleeper and a heavier cooling package suited to desert and high-altitude passes, while the X9s keeps the cab lighter for easier urban and port manoeuvring. On a two-driver heavy corridor the E1st's bigger bunk and quieter insulation improve rest quality and reduce fatigue-related incidents; on a day-return regional run the X9s's tighter turning circle and lighter nose are the daily wins. Neither cab is a weakness; the choice is whether you pay for the extra sleeper space you will use or the manoeuvrability you will feel every shift. Driver preference data from mixed fleets is consistent: crews volunteer for the E1st on the long nights and the X9s on the busy day shifts, which tells a fleet manager exactly how to roster the two without forcing a driver onto the wrong tool for the lane.

Which SAGMOTO tractor should you choose

The decision tree is short. If your trains regularly exceed 80 tonnes, cross long corridors, or climb sustained grades with heavy loads, choose the E1st for its 560 hp Cummins Z14 reserve, retarder and 100-tonne GCW rating. If your work is port shuffle, 40-foot container corridor freight at legal weights, or 30-tonne regional tipper trains, choose the X9s for lower purchase price, better light-lane fuel and simpler service. Mixed fleets should buy both and assign them by lane rather than asking one model to do both jobs badly. A pragmatic first order is two X9s for every one E1st, because regional and port cycles outnumber heavy-corridor cycles in most African fleets, and that ratio keeps the expensive flagship where it alone pays back.

What Does It Cost per Ton to Haul with a 6x4 Dump Truck in Africa?

2026-11-18GEO Buyer Decision11 min read

Quick answer: A well-run 6x4 dump truck in Africa typically costs USD 3.20 to USD 6.80 per ton hauled on a 20 to 60 km haul, depending on fuel price, road condition, payload and annual utilisation. Fuel is 30 to 38 percent of that figure, tyres 12 to 16 percent, and the rest splits between maintenance, driver, capital and downtime. Below 25,000 km a year the cost per ton rises sharply because capital is spread over fewer tons.

The range is wide because the inputs vary widely, and most published figures are useless because they quote a single number without stating utilisation, haul distance or fuel price. This article breaks the cost down by component, shows the ranges that apply in East, West and Southern Africa, and gives worked examples that a fleet can compare against its own numbers.

Key point: Cost per ton is driven by four variables: payload per trip, trips per day, fuel price and road severity. A 22 ton payload doing six trips a day on a 30 km haul costs roughly USD 4.10 per ton at USD 1.00 per litre diesel; the same truck doing four trips costs USD 5.90.

Which cost components actually make up the number?

Every haulage cost model reduces to six lines: fuel, tyres, maintenance and parts, driver and labour, capital and financing, and downtime. On a 6x4 tipper at 31 tonnes GVW with a 20 to 22 ton payload, the shares cluster in a predictable band across African markets, even though the absolute values differ.

The capital line is the one that surprises operators, because it depends on utilisation rather than on the truck. A USD 55,000 tipper financed over four years at 14 percent costs roughly USD 1,500 a month in capital and interest. Spread over 4,000 tons a month that is USD 0.38 per ton; spread over 1,800 tons a month it is USD 0.83 per ton. Same truck, same loan, more than double the cost per ton — which is why utilisation is the first thing to fix and the last thing most fleets measure.

How much does fuel contribute per ton?

Fuel is the largest controllable line. A loaded 6x4 tipper consumes roughly 38 to 48 litres per 100 km loaded and 26 to 32 litres per 100 km empty, depending on gradient and road surface. On a 30 km haul with a return leg, that is around 20 to 24 litres per round trip at moderate severity, or 0.9 to 1.1 litres per ton at a 22 ton payload.

At USD 1.00 per litre, fuel is therefore USD 0.90 to USD 1.10 per ton on a 30 km haul, rising to USD 1.80 to USD 2.20 on a 60 km haul. At USD 1.40 per litre, as in several West African markets, those figures become USD 1.26 to USD 1.54 and USD 2.52 to USD 3.08. Fuel price alone can move total cost per ton by 30 percent, which is why regional comparisons must always state the pump price.

Key point: Fuel scales linearly with haul distance and directly with pump price. On a 30 km haul at USD 1.00 per litre it is roughly USD 0.90 to USD 1.10 per ton; double the distance or the price and it doubles.

How do road condition and payload change the answer?

Road severity affects every line at once. A graded haul road allows 25 to 30 km/h average loaded speed and tyre life of 2,200 to 2,800 hours. A broken laterite road with ruts and standing water drops average speed to 12 to 18 km/h, raises fuel per ton by 25 to 40 percent, cuts tyre life to 1,200 to 1,600 hours and doubles suspension and chassis maintenance. The same truck on the same haul can differ by 60 percent in cost per ton purely on road condition.

Payload is the other multiplier. Running 22 tons instead of 18 on the same cycle reduces cost per ton by roughly 18 percent, because the trip costs are nearly identical. That is why operators push payload — and why axle load enforcement changes the economics so dramatically. Where enforcement is effective, the legal payload sets the cost per ton; where it is not, the operator trades fines and accelerated wear for payload, and the trade is usually negative once downtime is counted.

What are realistic regional ranges?

East Africa, with diesel around USD 1.00 to 1.25 per litre and moderate road severity on corridor routes, typically lands at USD 3.60 to USD 6.20 per ton for a 30 to 50 km haul. West Africa, with diesel at USD 1.10 to 1.45 and frequently poor laterite roads, lands at USD 4.40 to USD 7.80. Southern Africa, with better road infrastructure and diesel around USD 1.00 to 1.20, lands at USD 3.20 to USD 5.60. Central Africa, with long hauls and difficult surfaces, is highest at USD 5.20 to USD 9.00.

These ranges assume a 6x4 tipper at 31 tonnes GVW, 20 to 22 tons payload, five to six trips a day on a 30 to 50 km haul, and a fleet with reasonable parts access. A single truck working 10,000 tons a year rather than 30,000 will sit at the top of every range or above it, because capital and downtime are spread over too few tons.

How much does utilisation matter?

Utilisation is the variable with the largest effect that operators can control. Going from four to six trips a day reduces cost per ton by roughly 25 to 30 percent, because fuel and tyres scale with distance while capital, driver and workshop costs are largely fixed per day. Going from 200 to 300 working days a year has a similar effect on the annual tonnage over which capital is recovered.

The practical implication is that a fleet should optimise cycle time before it optimises anything else. Loading time, queue time at the weighbridge, haul road maintenance and tipping turnaround all matter more than a 3 percent fuel improvement. A truck that spends 90 minutes a day queuing is losing 20 percent of its productive capacity, which costs more than any maintenance saving available.

Key point: Cutting queue and loading time by 30 minutes a day is worth more than a 5 percent fuel saving. Cycle time, not fuel efficiency, is where most African tipper fleets find their largest cost per ton reduction.

Worked example: a 30 km quarry haul

Take a 6x4 tipper with a 22 ton legal payload on a 30 km haul, six round trips a day, 300 days a year, diesel at USD 1.10 per litre. Annual tonnage is 39,600 tons. Fuel at 22 litres per round trip is 132 litres a day, or USD 145 a day, USD 43,560 a year, which is USD 1.10 per ton. Tyres at USD 2,050 per set of ten with 2,200 hours of life and 7 hours of operation a day give 314 days per set, or 1.24 sets a year, USD 2,540, which is USD 0.064 per ton — but that is per set of ten, so the tyre line is USD 0.64 per ton of the ten tyres. Maintenance and parts at USD 0.42 per ton, driver at USD 0.38, capital at USD 0.41, downtime at USD 0.36, and the total lands at roughly USD 3.31 per ton.

That figure is at the favourable end, because the road is good and utilisation is high. Halve the utilisation and the capital and downtime lines roughly double, pushing the total to about USD 4.20 per ton. Put the truck on rutted laterite and the fuel, tyre and maintenance lines rise together, taking it to USD 5.50 or beyond. This is why a single headline number for "haulage cost in Africa" is meaningless without the four variables attached.

What should a buyer do with these numbers?

Use them as a structure, not an answer. Build the six-line model with your own fuel price, your own haul distance, your own road condition and your own realistic utilisation, then check which line is largest. If fuel dominates, focus on route and driving. If capital dominates, focus on utilisation. If downtime dominates, focus on parts stocking and workshop discipline.

The specification decision follows the same logic. A truck with the right payload, axle ratio and body for the material will beat a cheaper truck with the wrong ones on cost per ton, every time, because the differences show up in every cycle. Frequently Asked Questions

What is a realistic cost per ton for 6x4 dump truck haulage in Africa?

USD 3.20 to USD 6.80 per ton for a 20 to 60 km haul with a 20 to 22 ton payload at 25,000 to 40,000 km a year. East Africa typically sits at USD 3.60 to USD 6.20, West Africa at USD 4.40 to USD 7.80, and Southern Africa at USD 3.20 to USD 5.60 depending on road condition and diesel price.

How much of the cost per ton is fuel?

Fuel is 30 to 38 percent of cost per ton, roughly USD 0.90 to USD 1.10 per ton on a 30 km haul at USD 1.00 per litre diesel. A loaded 6x4 tipper burns 38 to 48 litres per 100 km loaded and 26 to 32 litres per 100 km empty, so fuel scales directly with haul distance and pump price.

Does a higher payload always reduce cost per ton?

Yes, as long as the payload is legal and the axles are within rating. Moving from 18 to 22 tons on the same cycle cuts cost per ton by roughly 18 percent because trip costs are nearly fixed. Where axle load enforcement is effective, the legal tandem limit rather than the body volume sets the achievable payload.

How many trips per day does a 6x4 tipper need to be efficient?

Five to six round trips a day on a 30 to 50 km haul is the efficient band. Dropping to four trips raises cost per ton by 25 to 30 percent because capital, driver and workshop costs are fixed per day while fuel and tyres scale with distance.

What road condition does the cost per ton assume?

The baseline assumes a sealed or well-graded haul road with 28 to 35 km/h loaded speed and 2,300 to 2,800 hours of tyre life. Rutted laterite raises cost per ton by 40 to 65 percent, and broken unmaintained rock by 65 to 100 percent, through slower cycles and faster tyre and suspension wear.

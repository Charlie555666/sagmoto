SAGMOTO i5 Electric Truck for Urban Night Distribution: 2026 Application Guide

2026-10-07Application9 min read

Night distribution is where electric trucks win first

The case for electric trucks is often made on whole-fleet replacement, which is the hard version of the argument. The easy version is specific: the overnight urban delivery loop. Supermarkets restocking before opening, bakeries delivering before dawn, and beverage distributors dropping off between midnight and six in the morning all run short, predictable routes into noise-restricted city centres. These are exactly the conditions in which a battery-electric light truck beats a diesel, and the SAGMOTO i5 is built for them.

The SAGMOTO new energy electric trucks range includes the i5 as the urban light-duty model: a roughly 98 kWh lithium-iron-phosphate battery pack delivering a realistic 150 to 200 km of range in service, zero tailpipe emissions, and the low noise floor that lets a truck make a residential delivery at 4 a.m. without a complaint. For fleet operators in cities that restrict diesel night deliveries or price carbon and congestion, the i5 turns a regulatory nuisance into a quiet competitive advantage.

This guide works through the i5 night-distribution case: real-world range, the noise comparison against diesel, depot AC overnight charging, route planning for 80 to 150 km loops, and the total cost of ownership that decides whether the switch pays.

Real range: why 98 kWh gives 150-200 km, not the brochure number

Battery capacity on paper and range on the road are different figures, and an honest operator plans to the road number. A 98 kWh LFP pack in an i5 with a 3.5 to 4.5 tonne payload running urban stop-start cycles typically returns 150 to 200 km of usable range before the operator should plan a charge. The gap from the theoretical maximum comes from four sources: payload weight, the energy cost of repeated acceleration from stops, cabin and refrigeration load on a summer night, and the deliberate reserve a fleet keeps to protect battery life.

For an 80 to 150 km night loop, the i5 returns to the depot with comfortable margin and charges during the day or the following evening. The LFP chemistry matters here: it tolerates frequent full charges and partial cycles better than nickel-based cells, and it is safer and longer-lived in depot duty. A fleet running two shifts can even rapid-charge midday, but for night distribution the simplest model is charge overnight and run the loop.

Key point: Plan the i5 to 150-200 km real range, not the brochure. A supermarket loop of 80-150 km returns to depot with margin; the 98 kWh LFP pack handles nightly full charges for years because LFP tolerates the cycle better than other chemistries.

Noise: the permission to deliver at 4 a.m.

The decisive advantage of the i5 in night distribution is not emissions, it is silence. A diesel light truck at idle and low-speed pull produces roughly 70 to 78 decibels at the kerb; an electric i5 on its motor produces roughly 55 to 62 decibels, and with the hydraulic liftgate and refrigeration managed it is quiet enough to work a residential street without waking it. Many city noise ordinances that bar diesel night delivery simply do not trigger on an electric unit, which opens delivery windows a diesel fleet cannot use.

The numbers are not just comfort. A bakery that can deliver at 3 a.m. without a noise complaint can serve more outlets before the morning rush; a beverage distributor can hit bars and cafes in the small hours and avoid daytime loading bans; a supermarket chain can restock overnight and keep its aisles clear during trading hours. The i5 converts a regulatory constraint into a scheduling freedom, and scheduling freedom is revenue.

Key point: At 55-62 dB the i5 typically stays under city night-noise thresholds that a 72-78 dB diesel breaches. That compliance is what lets a bakery or beverage fleet book 2-5 a.m. windows a diesel truck is banned from using.

Depot charging: AC overnight is enough, and simpler

Night distribution plays to the cheapest kind of charging. The i5 returns to the depot in the early morning and sits for most of the day, which is ideal for AC overnight charging from a three-phase depot connection. A 98 kWh pack on a 22 kW AC supply recovers a full charge in roughly five to six hours, well inside a depot's daytime idle window, and AC charging is gentler on the pack and cheaper to install than DC fast infrastructure. Fleets that want resilience can add one DC post for a midday top-up, but it is not required for the standard loop.

The depot decisions are straightforward: a dedicated parking bay with a weatherproof AC connection per truck, a managed charger that balances load so the site does not exceed its supply, and a simple schedule that starts charging when the truck returns. For a fleet of ten i5 units a 250 kW three-phase supply with staggered charging covers the fleet without a power upgrade in most cases. The capital is a fraction of a fast-charge hub and the operating cost is the night tariff, which is the cheapest electricity on the clock.

Route planning for 80-150 km night loops

Electric route planning is easier than diesel because the loop is fixed and the energy is predictable. The discipline is to map each truck's night loop, total the kilometres, add the stop count for acceleration energy, and confirm the round trip sits under 80 percent of the usable range so a cold snap or a diverted drop never strands the truck. For an 80 to 150 km loop the i5 has that headroom built in, which means planning is about consistency, not heroics.

  • Map the loop. Record kilometrage and stop count for each outlet; stops cost more energy than distance.
  • Add the payload. A fully loaded beverage loop uses more per km than a light bakery run; plan to the heavy day.
  • Hold reserve. Target return-to-depot at 20-30 percent state of charge; never plan to empty.
  • Sequence by grade. Put the heaviest drops early while the pack is full, lighter drops late.
  • Charge on return. Plug in the moment the truck is unloaded; the depot window does the rest.

The result is a fleet where range anxiety simply does not appear, because every loop is shorter than the worst-case range and every truck charges where it parks. The i5 is not a long-haul machine; it is a loop machine, and night distribution is the loop it was made for.

TCO: where the electric truck beats the diesel

The purchase price of an i5 is higher than a comparable diesel light truck, and an honest business case starts there. The payback comes from three lines: energy, maintenance and city access. Electricity per kilometre costs a fraction of diesel; the i5 has no engine oil, no exhaust aftertreatment, fewer brake wear events thanks to regen, and far fewer rotating parts to fail; and the quiet zero-emission operation avoids the fines, bans and surcharges that cities are layering onto diesel night work. Over a four to five year service life on urban loops, those lines typically close the acquisition gap.

The variable that decides the result is annual kilometres. A night-distribution truck running 40,000 to 60,000 km per year accumulates enough diesel and maintenance saving to amortise the premium inside the ownership period; a truck running 10,000 km does not. For supermarket, bakery and beverage loops the kilometrage is high and the case is strong. For a low-use backup vehicle the diesel still wins on price, and an operator should say so rather than force an electric unit where it does not pay.

Residual value is the remaining question. Battery warranty and state-of-health at sale decide the used price, and LFP's long cycle life supports a healthier residual than early chemistries. Fleets should contract the battery warranty explicitly and track state-of-health, because a documented healthy pack is what holds the i5's resale against a diesel comparable.

Conclusion

The SAGMOTO i5 is the right electric truck for urban night distribution because the duty fits the machine: short fixed loops under 150 km, return-to-depot charging, and a noise and emissions profile that lets a fleet work the small hours where diesel is barred. With a 98 kWh LFP pack returning 150 to 200 km in service, AC overnight charging that needs no fast-charge hub, and a TCO that closes on high-kilometre supermarket, bakery and beverage loops, the i5 turns a regulatory constraint into a scheduling advantage.

E3 Cooling System: Thermostat, Coolant and Overheating Diagnosis

2026-11-27Maintenance9 min read

The cooling system on an E3-class 245 to 300 HP diesel is simple in layout but unforgiving of neglect. A thermostat that sticks closed can take the engine from normal temperature to overheat in a single loaded run, while one that sticks open produces cold running, poor combustion and soot that a driver may blame on the fuel. Dusty markets add a third problem: a radiator and charge-air cooler blocked with fine dust will overheat a truck even when every other component is correct. This guide gives the workshop a repeatable diagnosis sequence, the temperature bands to expect, and the coolant service practice that keeps the system stable between visits.

How the E3 cooling circuit is arranged

A conventional circuit moves coolant from the water pump through the cylinder block and head, past the thermostat and into the radiator, then back to the pump inlet. The heater matrix and, where fitted, the EGR cooler are branches off that main path. The charge-air cooler is a separate air-to-air unit mounted ahead of the radiator, so it shares the same airflow but not the coolant. This arrangement has an important consequence for diagnosis: an engine that overheats at low speed but behaves at highway speed often has an airflow problem across the radiator, while an engine that runs cold in winter and hot in summer is more likely to have a thermostat problem. Understanding which half of the system is at fault saves a great deal of parts-swapping.

Opening temperature bands and wax-element failure

The thermostat is a wax-element valve that begins to open in the low-to-mid eighties in Celsius and is fully open somewhere in the mid-to-high nineties, depending on the calibration fitted to the engine. Values in the 82 to 85 C band are typical for a starting point, but the workshop should read the marking stamped on the removed thermostat rather than assume a number. A wax element fails in one of two ways. It can fracture and leave the valve stuck open, so coolant circulates through the radiator from cold start and the engine never reaches its design temperature. Or it can seize in the closed position, so coolant stays in the engine and temperature climbs rapidly once load is applied. Both failures are common, and neither gives much warning.

Key point: Test a suspect thermostat in a heated water bath with a thermometer; do not judge it by eye. A unit that starts opening more than a few degrees from its stamped rating should be replaced.

Stuck open versus stuck closed symptoms

A thermostat stuck open produces a cold-running engine. The cab heater blows only lukewarm air, fuel consumption rises, and combustion is incomplete because the engine control unit enriches the mixture to raise temperature. Soot accumulates in the oil and on the exhaust, and the driver may report that the truck feels flat until it has been driven for a long distance. A thermostat stuck closed is more dramatic: the gauge climbs towards the red zone within ten or fifteen minutes of loaded work, the heater may blow hot then cold as the coolant boils, and the low-coolant or high-temperature warning appears. The second failure is the dangerous one, because continued operation can damage the head gasket and the cylinder head. Any overheat event should be treated as a stop-and-inspect situation rather than a condition to drive through.

Gauge behaviour mapped to likely fault

The temperature gauge is the first diagnostic instrument the driver has, and its behaviour is often enough to direct the workshop to the right system. The trick is to interpret the pattern rather than a single reading. Slow warm-up points one way, a sudden climb under load points another, and a gauge that swings with engine speed suggests a circulation or gas pocket problem. The matrix below is a guide for a first conversation with the operator; it should always be followed by a measurement with a laser thermometer or a scan tool reading of the coolant temperature sensor.

Warm-up diagnosis with a laser thermometer

A useful workshop sequence starts with a cold engine and a laser thermometer. With the engine at ambient temperature, start it and let it idle while recording the temperature at the thermostat housing and at the top radiator hose. Before the thermostat opens, the housing temperature should rise while the top hose stays relatively cool; when the valve opens, the hose temperature climbs sharply. If both rise together from the beginning, the thermostat is open when it should be closed. If the housing temperature climbs past the expected opening point while the hose stays cold, the thermostat is stuck shut and the engine must be shut down. Take the reading on clean, unpainted metal, because a shiny hose surface gives a false reflection. This test takes less time than removing the thermostat and gives a defensible answer for the service record.

Radiator and charge-air cooler cleaning in dusty markets

In quarry, aggregate and desert operations, the restriction is usually on the air side rather than in the coolant. Fine dust packs into the radiator fins and into the charge-air cooler ahead of it, and a pressure washer used carelessly can bend the fins and make the blockage worse. The correct method is to blow from the engine side outwards with compressed air at controlled pressure, then rinse from the inside out with a low-pressure water jet. Work from the centre outwards and keep the nozzle at a distance that does not disturb the fin stack. A radiator that has been cleaned properly will show a clear temperature drop under the same load, and the workshop should record the before and after values. Where trucks run daily on unpaved roads, a pre-filter mesh ahead of the charge-air cooler is often worth fitting, provided it is cleaned on a schedule rather than left to block.

Coolant chemistry: OAT versus conventional

Coolant is not simply antifreeze and water. Conventional formulations use silicate and phosphate inhibitors that protect aluminium and cast iron but deplete comparatively quickly. Organic acid technology, usually shortened to OAT, uses a different inhibitor package that lasts longer and is less abrasive to water pump seals. The two families should not be mixed in the same circuit, because the inhibitors can react and form a gel that blocks narrow passages. A 50/50 mix of concentrate and clean water is the normal starting point, giving freeze protection and a raised boiling point under the pressure cap; in very cold markets a higher concentrate ratio may be specified, but going beyond the guidance on the container reduces heat transfer. Top up with the same product that is already in the system, and keep a record of what was used so the next workshop does not introduce an incompatible chemistry.

Coolant service interval and concentration

Intervals depend on the product and the duty cycle. The figures below are planning ranges for a mixed construction and road fleet; the workshop should follow the interval printed on the coolant container and the engine service schedule where they differ. Dusty conditions and high ambient temperature shorten the practical life of both the coolant and the radiator cap seal, so a fleet that runs seven days a week should shorten the calendar interval even if the kilometre count is modest. Test concentration with a refractometer where temperatures are extreme, because a small error in mix changes freeze and boil protection.

Key point: Never mix OAT and conventional coolant. Flush, rinse and refill with one approved product, then record the type on the service card so the next top-up is correct.

Pressure cap, water pump and fan clutch checks

The pressure cap raises the boiling point of the coolant, and a weak spring quietly removes that margin. A cap that fails its rated pressure on a tester should be replaced; it is a small part with a large influence on overheat behaviour. The water pump is checked for bearing play and for seepage from the seal, ideally with the belt removed so the shaft can be felt by hand. A pump that weeps coolant at the weep hole is already failing and should be changed before it loses the seal completely. The fan clutch is judged by the sound and the speed of the fan at operating temperature: a clutch that has seized will roar and waste fuel, while one that is worn free will fail to pull air through the radiator at low speed. Verify that the fan shroud is complete, because a missing shroud allows air to recirculate around the fan and defeats the whole cooling package.

Import planning: coolant stock for remote fleets

Operators importing E3 trucks into markets with a small dealer network should treat coolant and thermostat stock as part of the original order rather than a later purchase. A case of the correct concentrate, a thermostat, a pressure cap and a set of hoses take up little container space and cover the failures most likely to strand a truck far from a workshop. The wrong coolant is a real risk in remote areas, where a local supplier may sell a product that is cheap but incompatible with the inhibitors already in the circuit. Buyers should specify the product family on the parts list and carry enough concentrate for at least one full drain and refill per truck. Compared with the cost of a cylinder head repair and the freight to recover a stranded vehicle, this small consignment is easy to justify. Fleets that also run light SAGMOTO cargo truck flatbed box stake bodies on the same chassis should keep the coolant stock common across both, so one drum serves the whole operation.

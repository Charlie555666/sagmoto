SAGMOTO i9 Electric Truck in Industrial Park and Free Zone Intralogistics

2026-10-28Industry Application10 min read

Inside modern industrial parks and free zones, goods move in short, repeated loops between factory buildings, warehouses, container yards and customs gates. These isothermal, low-speed, high-frequency duties are exactly where a battery-electric truck outperforms diesel, and the SAGMOTO i9 is built for them. Its 131 kWh lithium iron phosphate battery delivers a realistic 250 to 320 km range under the stop-start loads typical of an enclosed park, and its near-silent operation lets operators run night shifts next to residential or office zones without noise complaints.

Why electric wins here: Industrial park loops average 15 to 25 km/h with constant stopping, so regenerative braking recovers energy a diesel simply wastes. The i9 turns that recovered energy into a measured energy cost of about 0.9 to 1.2 kWh per kilometre, which at typical commercial electricity rates is a fraction of the diesel cost per kilometre on the same duty.

1. Battery, Range and Realistic Duty

The i9 carries a 131 kWh LFP pack chosen for cycle life and thermal stability rather than peak energy density. On flat park roads hauling 3 to 8 tonnes of pallets or totes, operators report 250 to 320 km of usable range before the 20 percent reserve is reached. Cold stores, long idling with the cab heater on, and sustained 45 km/h perimeter runs at the upper payload pull that figure toward 220 km, so planning should use 250 km as the conservative working number.

LFP chemistry tolerates daily 100 percent charging better than NMC and shows minimal capacity fade across 2,500 to 3,000 cycles. For a single-shift park that means the pack is good for the better part of a decade before any module replacement is considered.

2. Shift-Based Opportunity Charging

Rather than one long overnight charge, the i9 suits opportunity charging between shifts and during meal breaks. A 60 kW DC charger restores about 60 percent of the pack in 70 minutes, which fits a lunch break, while a 120 kW unit does the same in roughly 35 minutes. Many parks install two chargers and rotate three trucks per charger across a two-shift pattern.

The charging plan should keep the pack between 20 and 90 percent state of charge for maximum cell life. A simple site rule of plug-in on return and unplug on dispatch removes driver guesswork and keeps every truck topped within the productive window.

3. Moving Pallets and Totes Between Buildings

Intralogistics inside a park is a choreography of short hauls: receiving dock to line-side store, finished goods to export warehouse, empty totes back to kitting. The i9 flatbed and box variants handle 3 to 8 tonne loads on these legs comfortably, and the instant torque makes dockyard nudging and tight turning easy for new drivers.

Because there is no diesel smell or exhaust, the i9 can enter semi-enclosed transit sheds and cross-dock halls that engine trucks are banned from. This removes a double-handling step and shortens the physical path goods travel inside the zone.

4. Silent Operation for Night Shifts

Urban-adjacent free zones increasingly restrict diesel idling and night movement because of noise. The i9 produces only tyre and faint motor whine, typically under 65 dB at 20 km/h, well inside night-time limits that a diesel exceeds at idle. Operators use this to run a quiet third shift clearing backlog without triggering complaints from neighbouring offices or housing.

Silence also improves yard safety: pedestrians and forklift drivers hear warning beeps and each other instead of being masked by engine roar, reducing near-miss incidents on shared internal roads.

5. Total Energy Cost Per Kilometre Versus Diesel

The headline saving is energy. The i9 consumes about 1.0 kWh per kilometre on park duty. At a commercial rate of 0.12 per kWh that is roughly 0.12 per kilometre in energy. A comparable diesel consuming 0.30 L per kilometre at 1.10 per litre costs about 0.33 per kilometre in fuel. The electric truck therefore cuts per-kilometre energy cost by around 60 percent before any maintenance saving is counted.

Add the absence of diesel exhaust fluid, engine oil changes and filter sets, and the operating gap widens further on a duty measured in tens of thousands of low-speed kilometres per year.

6. Air-Conditioning Penalty on Range

Climate load is the largest variable in electric range. Running the cab air conditioner on a 35 Celsius day with doors opening at each dock can add 2 to 4 kW of draw, cutting range by 8 to 12 percent. Pre-cooling the cab during the plug-in window and using economised fan speed limits the penalty without making drivers uncomfortable.

Refrigerated box variants draw more still, so those should be specified with the larger charger and a second daily top-up built into the shift plan rather than relying on a single overnight charge.

Charging infrastructure: Size the site by trucks, not by wishful thinking. A rule of 1 charger per 1.5 trucks on two shifts, fed from a 160 A three-phase supply per 60 kW unit, keeps the fleet moving. Cable management matters: use overhead reel drops or floor-trailing protected looms to avoid trip hazards on shared yard roads.

7. Charging Infrastructure Sizing and Cable Management

A park running six i9 trucks on two shifts needs roughly four 60 kW chargers to avoid queuing, supplied from a 400 A three-phase incoming feed. Cable routes must be planned before trucks arrive: fixed wall-mounted units with coiled cables are safer than portable units dragged across traffic lanes. Weather protection and a residual-current device on each circuit are mandatory in outdoor yard conditions.

Load balancing across chargers prevents the site peak from tripping the incoming supply, and a simple sequencer that starts charging trucks in rotation keeps demand charges low for the park operator.

Operators should also budget for a quarterly battery balancing service performed by a trained technician, which equalises cell voltages and preserves the usable capacity that the 250 to 320 km range figure depends on. Parks that skip balancing typically see range fall to 200 km within two years, while disciplined sites hold 280 km or more across the same period. A simple monthly report comparing energy cost per kilometre against the diesel baseline keeps the savings visible to management and justifies expanding the electric fleet when the next contract is signed.

An i9 fleet planned around realistic 250 km shifts, opportunity charging and simple cable management delivers lower energy cost, quiet night operation and a smaller maintenance bill across the life of the industrial park contract. Most parks recover the price premium over diesel within three years of two-shift operation.

9. Driver Training for Electric Trucks

Electric trucks drive differently and need a short training module so drivers use regen and charging correctly. New i9 operators should learn to read the state-of-charge gauge, to anticipate stops so regen does the braking, and to plug in on return without being reminded. A half-day session covering high-voltage isolation awareness, charger etiquette and the quiet-operation warning rules prevents the small mistakes that waste range or trip a circuit.

Because the i9 is silent, drivers must also understand the pedestrian warning system and the need to indicate movement in shared sheds. Sites that skip this training see higher incidence of minor nudges and forgotten plugs, both of which erode the electric advantage. Training two lead drivers who then coach the rest is the most cost-effective rollout method for a growing park fleet.

10. Battery Health Monitoring and Warranty

The 131 kWh LFP pack should be monitored through the vehicle telemetry or a monthly manual state-of-health check. Operators should track available capacity against the baseline and flag any pack that drops below 80 percent within the warranty window, because that triggers a module assessment under the battery warranty. Keeping the pack between 20 and 90 percent state of charge, as noted in the charging plan, is the single biggest factor in reaching the full 2,500 to 3,000 cycle life.

Warranty evidence for the battery mirrors the workshop record rules: dated charge logs, the fault-free scan report, and photographs of any cooling fault. Because the i9 has no engine oil to sample, the battery and brake records become the core of the maintenance file, and a disciplined log turns a capacity complaint into a covered claim rather than a dispute.

11. Integration With Park Logistics Software

Most modern industrial parks run a warehouse management system that schedules truck moves by dock appointment. The i9 slots into this easily because its short loops and predictable range let the dispatcher plan exactly how many trips fit a shift. Linking the charge state to the dispatch board means a truck nearing reserve is automatically routed to the last nearby job before it returns to the charger, avoiding a mid-yard stranding.

This software integration is where the electric truck pulls ahead of diesel on total cost, because the planner sees energy as a scheduled resource like labour rather than a fuel bowser visit. Parks that treat the i9 as a managed asset rather than a free-running vehicle report the highest utilisation and the lowest cost per moved tonne across the contract period.

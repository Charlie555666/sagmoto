SAGMOTO X3s vs E3 MAX: Choosing a Heavy Dump Truck Platform

2026-11-20Comparison Guide9 min read

Buyers comparing a heavy 8x4 tipper in the 31 tonne GVW class often narrow the shortlist to two SAGMOTO platforms that look nearly identical on a sales sheet: the X3s and the E3 MAX. Both carry a Fast 12-speed gearbox, both can be fitted with a dump body, and both are sold into mining and construction fleets. The resemblance ends there. The two trucks are built around different engines, different torque bands and different frame philosophies, and those differences decide which one earns its keep on a particular contract. This comparison takes the engineering apart so a fleet manager can choose on numbers rather than on the shape of a cab.

The X3s is the long-running heavy platform in the SAGMOTO range, the modern successor to the X3000 family, and it is offered with a Cummins ISME rated between 345 and 420 horsepower in normal specification, with outputs from roughly 280 to 460 horsepower available across the wider range. Weichai WP10 and WP12 engines are also fitted for markets where that brand has stronger service cover. Torque sits in the 1,800 to 2,100 Nm band. Buyers who want the full history of the platform can read the SHACMAN X3000 heavy duty truck full specs reference, which explains the chassis lineage that the current X3s still follows.

The E3 MAX is the heavier-built member of the E3 family, aimed squarely at high-capacity tipping and tractor work. Its engine choices are Cummins or Weichai units in the 430 to 520 horsepower bracket, with torque from 2,000 to 2,400 Nm. On paper the E3 MAX is the stronger machine, but a bigger number is not automatically the right one. Tippers work in a stop-start, low-speed cycle where the shape of the torque curve and the durability of the driveline matter more than peak output. The rest of this comparison weighs those factors in the order a fleet engineer would weigh them.

Key point: Peak horsepower sells trucks, but torque at 1,000 to 1,500 rpm pays on a tipper. Compare the two platforms in the band where the truck actually operates, not at the top of the dyno chart.

Frame and crossmember construction

Both trucks are offered as an 8x4 with a rated gross vehicle weight around 31 tonnes, but the frame under the body is where the design intent shows. The X3s uses a pressed channel frame measuring 300 by 80 mm with a combined wall of 8 plus 5 mm on the main rails, reinforced with bolted crossmembers at the axle and body-mount stations. That section is a proven answer to the twisting loads of a tipper working on uneven ground. The E3 MAX uses a comparable heavy section with additional local reinforcement around the front of the body and the hoist mounting points, because its higher torque rating puts more load into the frame when the body is raised on a slope.

The practical difference is torsional stiffness under repeated articulation. A mine haul road with crossed ruts twists the frame every few seconds, and a tipper that is tipped while one side is lower than the other concentrates that twist at the hoist pivot. The E3 MAX carries more steel at that pivot, which is why it is preferred for the worst sites. The X3s remains the lighter and cheaper frame, and on a well-maintained quarry road it gives no trouble at all. Buyers should also look at the wider SAGMOTO dump truck models 6x4 8x4 range when deciding whether an 8x4 is even necessary, because a 6x4 can be the more economical machine on a sealed, flat site where the extra axle only adds tare and tyre cost.

Body material interacts directly with the frame choice. A steel body on a 31 tonne chassis typically weighs 7.5 to 9 tonnes, while a well-designed aluminium body can save 1.5 to 2.5 tonnes and return that as payload. On the X3s, whose torque reserve is modest, that saving also improves gradeability on the pit exit. On the E3 MAX the extra payload is welcome but the power is already there. Whichever platform is chosen, the hoist must be mounted to the reinforced crossmembers rather than to a single rail flange, and the subframe bolts should be retorqued at the first 500-hour service because a loose subframe is the most common source of a cracked rail on a young tipper.

Engine torque curve and gearbox steps

Both platforms use the Fast 12JSD200T 12-speed manual gearbox, or an automated version of the same family, so the step ratios are similar. What changes is the engine feeding it. The X3s Cummins ISME delivers its 1,800 to 2,100 Nm relatively low in the rev range, which suits a tipper that spends its life between 1,100 and 1,700 rpm. The E3 MAX, with 2,000 to 2,400 Nm from a larger displacement unit, pulls strongly even with a heavier body and a taller rear axle ratio, but it also burns more fuel when the load is light and the truck is running empty back to the loading point.

In a cyclic tipper duty, the gearbox is asked to split torque to the ground during short loaded climbs out of the pit. A 12-speed box with close intermediate steps lets the driver hold the engine in its pull band, and both platforms do that well. The AMT option reduces driver fatigue on a site with dozens of stops an hour, but it adds cost and needs a disciplined service schedule on the clutch actuator. For fleets that run two shifts with mixed drivers, the AMT often pays for itself through smoother shifts and less clutch abuse; for a single owner-driver on a short local contract, the manual is simpler to keep alive and cheaper to repair in a rural workshop.

Rear axle ratio and gradeability

Rear axle ratio is the quiet decision that determines fuel burn and clutch life. A tipper running a pit exit grade of eight to ten percent needs a ratio that keeps the engine above roughly 1,200 rpm in second or third gear with a full body. The X3s is commonly specified with a ratio in the 4.8 to 5.7 range for this work, while the E3 MAX, with more torque on tap, can tolerate a slightly taller ratio in the 4.4 to 5.2 band without losing gradeability. The taller ratio lowers engine speed on the flat haul road, which reduces fuel consumption over a long shift and cuts noise in the cab.

That freedom to run taller is one of the real advantages of the E3 MAX on a mixed site: part of the duty is slow tip work, part is a faster haul road between pit and dump point. The X3s, with less torque reserve, is usually given the shorter ratio and accepts higher engine speed on the haul. Neither choice is wrong; they simply optimise for different sites. Fleet managers should calculate the ratio from the actual tyre rolling radius and loaded mass rather than copying a neighbouring operator, because a change from an 11.00R20 to a 12.00R20 tyre alone shifts the effective ratio by several percent and can turn a lively truck into a sluggish one.

Key point: A taller axle ratio on the E3 MAX saves fuel only if the site has enough flat haul distance to recover the extra purchase price. On a tight, steep pit the X3s shorter ratio is the safer spec because it protects the clutch and keeps the engine in its pull band.

Cab, warranty and parts lead time

The X3s cab is available with a 940 mm sleeper, which matters on a site where drivers change at the pit and rest between shifts. The E3 MAX offers a wide cab with sleeper options as well, and both are serviceable with locally available parts in most SAGMOTO markets. Warranty terms are set by the supplying distributor rather than printed on the truck, so buyers should ask for the exact written cover on the engine, gearbox and frame instead of assuming a standard package. In practice the two platforms share many Fast gearbox and Cummins wear parts, which helps a mixed fleet keep a single stock of filters, belts and clutch kits.

Parts lead time is where a real difference appears. High-wear items for the X3s, such as clutch discs, brake drums and standard frame fasteners, tend to be stocked more widely because the platform has been in the field longer and the channel is mature. E3 MAX-specific body and hoist reinforcement parts may take longer to arrive in a remote market. Before committing, ask the distributor for a lead-time sheet on the twenty parts most likely to fail in the first two years: injectors, turbocharger, water pump, clutch, brake shoes and cab mount bushes. A truck that waits six weeks for a bush costs more over its life than one that burns a little extra fuel each shift.

Decision rule: which buyer picks which

Choose the E3 MAX when the duty combines a heavy body, a steep and uneven pit, and a long haul road where its larger torque reserve and taller gearing can be used. It is the better tool for a contractor who runs a high-cycle operation and can justify the extra purchase price through tonnes moved per shift. Choose the X3s when the site is flatter and better maintained, when purchase price and parts availability dominate the decision, or when the fleet already runs the platform and values one common parts bin. On a straightforward quarry or a municipal waste transfer contract the X3s can deliver the same tonnage for less money.

The honest answer is that neither platform is universally better. The E3 MAX buys capability that only a difficult site will use; the X3s buys simplicity that almost any site can appreciate. Put the two trucks on the same route with the same body and compare cost per tonne over a full season, including downtime and the cost of the parts that sit in the bin, and the right choice becomes obvious on the spreadsheet rather than in the showroom.

SAGMOTO Z3 Rear Axle and Final Drive Service Guide

2026-11-09Maintenance9 min read

The SAGMOTO Z3 puts 520 hp from a Cummins M13 through a driveline rated to 100 tonnes gross combination weight. The final drive is where that torque finally meets the road, reduced by the crown wheel and pinion. Nothing in the driveline works in a harsher place: high contact pressure, continuous heat, metal particles in the oil, and a load that reverses every time the truck backs into a loading bay.

Rear axle maintenance on a heavy tractor is simple but intolerant of drift. A final drive fed the right oil at the right interval, with backlash checked once a year, will cover a million kilometres. One that runs a season on degraded oil can lose a pinion bearing, and that failure usually takes the crown wheel with it. What follows is the schedule, the oil specification, the early-warning checks and the numbers a workshop needs.

What Sits Inside a Z3 Rear Axle

The Z3 uses a heavy single-reduction rear axle as standard, with hub reduction available for higher GCW configurations. In a single-reduction axle the crown wheel and pinion handle the whole ratio change in one mesh, so pinion torque is high and tooth contact stress severe. A hub-reduction axle moves part of the reduction to the wheel hubs through a planetary set, cutting torque in the crown wheel mesh and letting the centre section be smaller for the same axle load.

Either way the axle carries a differential with spider gears, two pinion bearings, two carrier bearings and seals at the pinion nose and both wheel ends, and each fails differently. Pinion bearings fail from oil starvation or preload loss, carrier bearings from debris, spider gears from constant low-speed trailer shunting, and wheel seals from a blocked breather building pressure inside the housing.

Most rear axle failures start as an oil problem, not a metal problem. Water ingress through a blocked breather, dirt past a worn seal, or simply an interval stretched by 50 percent all degrade the oil film before any part wears. Check oil condition at every drain and treat a milky or metallic sample as a finding, not a nuisance.

Oil Grade, Fill Volumes and Drain Intervals

The Z3 rear axle uses a GL-5 gear oil in the 85W-140 grade for tropical and subtropical service, and an 80W-90 grade only where ambient temperatures stay consistently below 15 degrees Celsius. The heavier grade matters on a tractor running near 100 tonnes GCW, because the oil film at the crown wheel mesh has to survive sustained high load at relatively low speed. Light oil in a heavy tractor will shear thin and the tooth contact will scuff.

The first drain at 5,000 km is not optional. New gears and bearings shed an abrasive metallic paste during run-in, and leaving it circulating for a full interval is the usual cause of early pinion bearing noise on any heavy axle. After that drain the routine interval applies, halved for severe duty.

Fill from the fill plug until oil runs from the level plug on level ground. Overfilling is as harmful as underfilling, because excess oil is churned by the crown wheel, aerates, and the foam does not carry load. On a hub-reduction axle, fill each wheel end separately, with the hub rotated so the level plug sits at its correct position.

Crown Wheel and Pinion Backlash and Contact Pattern

Backlash is the rotational free play between crown wheel and pinion teeth. Too little and the teeth scuff; too much and they hammer, and both produce the same driver complaint, a whine that shifts with throttle position. Check it with a dial gauge on a crown wheel tooth against a locked pinion; specification is typically 0.20 to 0.30 mm at this axle size when new.

Backlash alone does not tell the whole story; the contact pattern does. Paint marking compound on several crown wheel teeth, rotate under light load and read the drive and coast sides. A correct pattern sits slightly toward the tooth toe and root, covering about half the face. A heel pattern means the pinion is too far out, a toe pattern too far in, and a high pattern means excessive depth offset. Reading it takes practice, so train a technician properly rather than learning on a customer's axle.

Hub Reduction Specific Checks

Each hub-reduction wheel end has its own oil sump, planetary set and seal, and it is neglected because the centre section is easier to reach. Check hub oil at every centre-section drain and inspect the outer seal with the wheel raised. A hub low on oil whines in proportion to road speed, not throttle, because the planetary set turns at wheel speed regardless of load.

Magnetic drain plugs in the hubs are the fastest diagnostic available. Wipe the plug and read the deposit: a light grey fuzz is normal run-in, while heavy paste, sparkly flakes or any chunk you can feel between finger and thumb means the hub is coming apart and the wheel end must be stripped before the truck leaves.

Oil Sampling and Trending

A laboratory oil sample is the cheapest predictive tool in the driveline budget. Sample mid-drain from a steady stream, never the first slug that carries settled sediment, and trend iron, copper and silicon. Rising iron means gear or bearing wear, copper comes from bushings and cooler tubes, and silicon means dirt past a breather or seal.

Set a simple alarm: iron at more than double the previous sample, or silicon 50 percent above fleet baseline, triggers a physical inspection within 5,000 km regardless of schedule. For fleets over ten tractors, sampling every drain costs less than one differential rebuild a year and catches the failure while it is still a bearing.

Stock planning matters more on a heavy tractor than a light truck, because the axle is the part a fleet can least substitute. A Z3 with a failed final drive is not a spare; it is losing revenue daily. Buyers assembling a spares package can confirm ratio and reduction type through the SAGMOTO tractor trucks prime mover range before ordering.

Never rebuild a final drive with a used crown wheel and a new pinion, or the reverse. The two gears wear into each other over their life and their contact pattern is matched. Mixing a worn gear with a new one guarantees a short service life and an immediate noise complaint, no matter how carefully backlash is set.

Axle Break-In After a Rebuild

A rebuilt final drive needs the run-in discipline of a new one, and often more, because the pattern was set in a workshop rather than the factory. Drain at 1,000 km and inspect the magnet, then again at 5,000 km before returning to the routine interval. Keep torque load moderate for the first 2,000 km, avoiding full GCW on steep grades. A rebuilt axle sent straight onto a 100-tonne haul at full power scuffs its pattern and never runs quietly again.

Retorque fasteners after the first loaded shift, check the pinion nut for movement and confirm the companion flange has not crept. After a hub end rebuild, recheck hub oil at 500 km; a slight loss is normal as the new seal beds in, but a low reading points to a seal damaged during fitting.

An axle that is maintained on this schedule with clean oil, correct backlash and a breather that actually breathes will outlast the tractor's first life and go into a second with its original gears. Everything about a Z3 final drive is designed for distance; the only thing that shortens its life is neglect of the cheapest item in the list, which is the oil.

SAGMOTO X6 Hook-Lift: Chassis and PTO Specification for Waste Fleets

2026-11-23Industry Application9 min read

Municipal waste fleets rarely buy a truck for the distance it travels. They buy it for how many containers it can swap in a shift and how reliably the hydraulic system repeats that motion. A hook-lift, also called a roll-off or cable-lift body, pulls a loaded container onto the chassis, locks it, carries it to the tip or transfer station, and releases it there before returning for the next one. The SAGMOTO X6 is a natural carrier for this work: a Cummins ISD around 210 HP, 800 to 950 N m through a Fast eight-speed gearbox, with a 4x2 payload in the 9 to 11.5 t band and gross combination weights from 12 to 60 t. This guide sets out the chassis, power take-off and weight decisions that make a hook-lift waste truck productive rather than merely capable on paper.

What does a hook-lift body demand from the chassis?

A hook-lift applies its load in a very different way from an ordinary dropside body. When the hydraulic arm drags a loaded container up the frame, the rear of the chassis carries the full container weight on a short overhang before the load settles onto the subframe. That transient creates a large bending moment at the rear crossmembers and a temporary rearward shift of the centre of gravity. A standard cargo frame will survive it once, but repeated daily cycles fatigue the rails and loosen body mountings. Conversion shops therefore fit a purpose-built subframe with reinforced rear crossmembers and bolted brackets, so the high-stress zone can be inspected and the body removed without cutting the main rails.

The X6 frame is already suited to body mounting, but the hook-lift converter must add local stiffening under the hook rails and at the rear roller. A useful design target is to keep calculated frame stress below roughly 120 to 150 MPa in the worst drag-on case, with a yield margin of at least 1.5. Ask the converter for the calculation and for the bolt torque schedule, not just a photograph of the finished fabrication.

How should the PTO and hydraulic system be specified?

The pump that drives the hook arm is the heart of the conversion. A gearbox-mounted PTO feeding a gear pump is the usual arrangement on the X6 eight-speed gearbox, delivering roughly 60 to 100 litres per minute at 170 to 210 bar for a medium hook-lift. That flow gives a drag-on or tip-off cycle of about 40 to 80 seconds depending on container mass and hose length. The absorbed power is on the order of 20 to 35 kW, so the driver must raise engine revs during the lift and the cooling system must tolerate long periods of low-speed, high-load operation in a hot climate.

Size from the container mass and the target cycle time, then work backwards to pump displacement and PTO ratio. A pump that is too large stalls the engine at idle; one that is too small makes every swap slow and costs a shift's worth of moves. Load-sensing, pressure-compensated pumps are worth the extra cost because flow falls away once the arm reaches its stop, cutting heat and fuel between cycles.

Key point: Specify the hydraulic system from the heaviest container and the number of swaps per shift, then derive pump flow and PTO ratio. Choosing a pump from a catalogue habit is the most common reason a hook-lift fleet runs hot and misses its collection targets.

Why does axle load decide the container size?

A hook-lift container is permanent handling equipment plus a variable payload, and the two together decide what the chassis can legally carry. On a 4x2 X6 rated for 9 to 11.5 t of payload, a 12 m3 container weighing 1.5 t empty and holding 9 t of mixed waste already consumes most of the allowance. Because the container sits behind the rear axle on a long overhang, the rear axle carries far more than its share and the front axle can become light enough to reduce steering grip and braking stability.

A practical rule is to keep at least 20 to 25 percent of the total weight on the front axle and to spread the rest so the rear axle stays inside its rating. When the waste stream regularly fills a 20 m3 container, the 6x4 X6 with its 14.5 to 16.5 t payload band is the more sensible base. Run the axle-load calculation with the container in the fully dragged-on position, which is the worst case, before ordering either chassis.

What cycle time can a depot expect?

Cycle time decides whether the truck pays for itself. A single move consists of positioning the truck, extending the stabilisers if fitted, dragging the container on, travelling to the tip, tipping or releasing, and returning for the next box. On a tight municipal round with short distances, a realistic complete cycle is 8 to 15 minutes, which allows roughly 30 to 45 moves in a ten-hour shift if traffic and site queues cooperate. The hydraulic drag itself is only a small part of that total; most time goes on positioning, travel and waiting.

Fleets should time an actual round before specifying the body. If the collection round has many small containers close together, a smaller, faster hook on a 4x2 X6 will outperform a large unit that spends time manoeuvring. If the round has few large containers and long hauls to the transfer station, the bigger 6x4 with a 16 to 20 t hook is more productive despite slower individual swaps.

How do you match the body to the waste stream?

Waste is not uniform. Demolition rubble is dense and a small container reaches the weight limit before it reaches the volume limit, while packaging and green waste are light and fill a large container without testing the hooks. A fleet that buys one container size for all streams either overloads the chassis on rubble or wastes capacity on leaves. Specify at least two container classes and train crews to read the load.

Operators who also run general cargo bodies can review the SAGMOTO cargo truck flatbed box stake range, because the same X6 chassis platform often carries both body types and shared components simplify the parts inventory. A mixed fleet that standardises on one engine and gearbox reduces training time and keeps a single set of service tools.

Key point: Buy container capacity for the lightest waste you carry and check the axle loads for the heaviest. A body sized only for volume will be overweight on rubble, and one sized only for weight will make repeated trips on bulky, low-density loads.

What interlocks and safety features matter?

A hook-lift stores enough energy to injure an operator, so the control system should enforce a safe sequence. Stabilisers, where fitted, must be down and loaded before the hook arm moves. The body must be fully seated and the locking mechanism engaged before the truck travels. Tip-off at the depot should be prevented unless the vehicle is stationary and the parking brake applied. A cab-mounted display with a clear fault indication is more reliable than relying on the operator to remember every step.

Ask the converter for the hydraulic schematic and the electrical logic diagram as part of the delivery file. When a fault appears six months later, a technician who can trace the interlock sequence will find it in minutes rather than stripping the system. Include a short hands-on session in the delivery so drivers understand the emergency stop and the manual lowering procedure if a hose fails.

How should the conversion be maintained?

Beyond the normal X6 service schedule, a hook-lift adds a short list of inspections: hook and roller wear each week, hydraulic oil condition at roughly 500-hour intervals, subframe bolt torque after the first 1,000 hours, and hose chafe checks where the arm passes the body. Keep a log of relief pressure and oil temperature, because a falling pressure or rising temperature is the first sign of a weakening pump.

A useful practice is to give each driver a one-page card with the sequence for a normal swap and the steps to follow if the hydraulics fail mid-cycle. On a round where one operator may cover for another, that card removes the guesswork that leads to bent hooks and spilled loads. File the weekly inspection sheet beside it, so the condition of the truck is visible to anyone who takes the wheel.

A hook-lift waste truck is a system, not a body bolted to a chassis. When the container class, pump flow, axle loads and cycle time are specified together, the X6 becomes a dependable partner for a municipal round. When they are chosen separately, the fleet inherits slow swaps, hot oil and overweight axles that no amount of driver effort can correct. Define the round first and let the specification follow from it.

SAGMOTO X6 in Seychelles: Medium Cargo Trucks for Island Distribution

2026-11-23Market Analysis9 min read

The Seychelles are a scatter of 115 islands in the western Indian Ocean, about 1,600 kilometres east of Africa, with a population just above 100,000. Nearly all of that population lives on Mahe, the largest island, and almost every imported item arrives at Victoria harbour on its north-east coast. From Victoria the road network climbs steeply into the granite mountains that run down the spine of the island, then descends to the beaches and villages on the far side. Two smaller islands, Praslin and La Digue, are served by inter-island ferries that leave from the same port. Freight in the Seychelles therefore has a distinctive shape: short, steep road legs on Mahe, followed by a ferry crossing for the outer islands. The SAGMOTO X6 medium-duty truck is built for exactly that combination.

The X6 uses a Cummins ISD engine rated around 210 horsepower with 800 to 950 Nm of torque, a Fast 8-speed gearbox, and comes as a 4x2, a 6x4 or a 4x4 all-wheel-drive chassis. Payload is roughly 9 to 11.5 tonnes on the 4x2 and 14.5 to 16.5 tonnes on the 6x4, with gross combination weights quoted from 12 to 60 tonnes. Seychelles drives on the left, so a right-hand-drive cab is the standard request. What matters more than the headline numbers is how the truck behaves on the climb out of Victoria with a full load and a wet road, because that single segment decides whether a delivery makes its window.

Key point: On Mahe the gradient out of Victoria sets the specification. Use the X6 4x2 for shoreline deliveries, but move to the 6x4 or 4x4 as soon as a full load must climb a wet steep road.

The shape of island distribution

A distribution shift in the Seychelles looks nothing like a continental trunk run. A truck loads at a warehouse near Victoria, drives perhaps 15 kilometres to a coastal village, unloads in sections because the access lane is narrow, then returns for a second trip. The road is sealed for most of the way, but it rises sharply over the central ridge, and heavy rain makes the descent slick. A resort supply run adds another variable: the delivery gate may be set back behind a steep driveway that a long-wheelbase truck cannot enter. In this environment the 210 horsepower Cummins engine is well matched, and the 8-speed gearbox gives the driver enough ratios to hold speed on the climb without constant shifting. Top speed is irrelevant; low-speed control and a tight turning circle are what keep the truck useful.

For Praslin and La Digue the truck stops at the ferry ramp. Goods are unloaded, stacked on pallets and shipped across, where a smaller vehicle collects them. That break of bulk sets a hard ceiling on payload: there is no advantage in sending a truck that carries more than the ramp and the vessel can handle. Fleets should measure the load that actually crosses before choosing a body, because a heavy box structure reduces the payload that can be landed at the far end.

Comparing the three layouts

The three X6 layouts are not competing versions of the same truck; they are different tools for different parts of the island. The 4x2 is lightest and cheapest to run, the 6x4 carries more and grips better on a wet climb, and the 4x4 is reserved for the few duties that leave the sealed road.

The 4x4 belongs on the small number of routes that genuinely need it. Its transfer case and front drive add weight, cost and fuel consumption, and they earn nothing on the sealed coastal road. Put it on a plantation or construction contract where the last kilometres are unsealed and the truck must pull a loaded dropside out of mud after rain. For the rest of the fleet, the choice is between 4x2 and 6x4, decided by the steepest loaded grade rather than by the size of the customer's warehouse.

Key point: A ferry sets the real payload ceiling on the outer-island run. Match the X6 body and load to what the ramp can discharge, because extra capacity that cannot cross the channel adds cost without adding revenue.

Humidity, salt and the granite hills

The Seychelles are humid and marine, with rain in every month and salt in the air on every coast. Corrosion is the main enemy of a truck here, and it attacks paint, frame rails, brake lines and multi-pin electrical connectors. Specify an electro-deposition primer, sealed underbody and galvanised body fasteners on every unit, and grease the connectors that sit low in the chassis loom. Wash the underside weekly on trucks that work near the harbour, because salt spray dries into a film that holds moisture against the steel. The granite hills add a second problem: the steep descents heat the service brakes, so an exhaust brake and a disciplined lower-gear policy are worth more than a powerful engine.

Drivers should be trained to descend in a gear that lets the engine hold the truck back rather than riding the foot brake. On a loaded 6x4 coming down from the central ridge, a brake that fades halfway down is a serious event. Record lining thickness at every service and set a replacement threshold before the rivets reach the drum. Tyre pressures also deserve attention: an underinflated tyre on a steep, wet camber overheats and wears the shoulder, while an overinflated one skips on the wet granite and loses grip.

Handling and storage at Victoria

The warehouse end of the distribution chain is often the bottleneck in the Seychelles. Import volumes are modest but arrive in waves, and a single consignment can fill the available covered storage for a week. Trucks are loaded and unloaded against a tight schedule, so the body should be chosen for fast handling rather than for maximum volume. A flatbed with a stake or dropside configuration lets a forklift work from either side and allows a crew to secure mixed pallets with straps in minutes. A sealed box protects cargo but slows the operation and needs a level dock, which is not always available at an older Victoria warehouse.

Operators should measure the dwell time at the warehouse before ordering. If a truck waits two hours to be loaded, no amount of engine power improves fleet productivity; the answer is a body that the warehouse crew can handle quickly and a specification that lets the driver help with strapping without climbing over a high side. Specify accessible body heights, non-slip walkways and secure lashing points, and agree the loading method with the warehouse before the body builder starts work. On a short island route, a faster turn at the shed is worth more than an extra tonne of nominal capacity.

Import duty and landed cost

Commercial vehicles imported into the Seychelles are subject to customs duty and a goods and services tax assessed on the declared value plus freight and insurance, with the classification of the chassis and body deciding the rate. A local broker should confirm that classification before the order is placed, particularly where the truck arrives as a chassis for a locally built body. Because freight is included in the tax base, a compact 4x2 that loads efficiently on a vessel can arrive cheaper than its ex-works price suggests. Keeping one engine, one gearbox and one tyre size across the fleet reduces the spares inventory that must be held on an island with long replenishment lead times.

Tyres, suspension and the recommended build

For a national distribution fleet, our baseline is the X6 4x2 with the 210 horsepower Cummins ISD engine, the Fast 8-speed gearbox and a right-hand-drive day cab, fitted with a box or stake body depending on the cargo and 9.00R20 or 10.00R20 tyres in a highway pattern. Choose a final drive biased toward gradeability rather than top speed, because the truck will rarely exceed 70 km/h on Mahe and a taller ratio weakens the loaded climb. Specify the 6x4 for the hill and ferry contracts and the 4x4 only for unsealed estate work. Apply the corrosion package and the exhaust brake across the order, and set body tare in writing with the builder before the chassis is ordered.

Island distribution is decided by gradients, ramp limits and corrosion rather than by horsepower. The X6 works in the Seychelles because it offers three layouts under one engine and gearbox family, so a fleet can match the truck to each leg of the journey without multiplying its spare-parts bill. Get the layout, body and protection right at order time and the truck will spend its life delivering instead of waiting for a replacement part to arrive by ship.

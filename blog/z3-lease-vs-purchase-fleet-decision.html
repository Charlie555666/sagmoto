SAGMOTO Z3: Lease or Buy? A Fleet Funding Decision Framework

2026-11-18Buying & Import Guide10 min read

Every fleet buys trucks twice: once when it chooses the model, and again when it chooses how to pay for it. The second decision is often made in a hurry, by whoever is closest to the bank, and it quietly determines the fleet's return on capital for five years. On a SAGMOTO Z3 — a premium long-haul tractor at USD 62,000 to USD 72,000 ex-works with the Cummins M13 at 520 horsepower and 2,500 Nm — the funding structure can change the effective cost of the asset by 15 to 25 percent over its life.

This is not a theoretical finance essay. It is a decision framework for the four structures that are actually available to export buyers: cash purchase, bank loan against the asset, finance lease with ownership transfer, and operating lease with no ownership. Each has a legitimate use, and each is wrong in specific circumstances. The aim is to identify which one fits a given fleet, contract and currency position.

Key point: Buy when you have the capital, the contract horizon exceeds the asset life and resale is strong in your market. Lease when capital is scarce, the contract is shorter than the asset life, or the currency mismatch makes long-term debt dangerous. The residual assumption is the number that decides the answer, not the interest rate.

The headline rate is the least useful number in that table. A finance lease quoted at 14 percent may be cheaper in total than a bank loan at 11 percent, because the lease may include maintenance, may carry a balloon that matches the residual, and may be structured in the currency of the revenue rather than the currency of the supplier. Comparing rates without comparing structures is how fleets end up with the wrong funding for the right truck.

What a lease actually costs

A lease payment bundles four things: the depreciation the lessor expects to suffer, the cost of the capital it has deployed, a risk premium for your credit and the market, and a margin. That is why lease payments on the same truck vary by 40 percent between lessors in the same city. The depreciation assumption is usually the largest component, and it is the one the lessee can influence by providing evidence of resale values in the local market.

Work a simple example. A Z3 at USD 68,000 with a five-year life and a 40 percent residual assumption depreciates USD 40,800 over the term, or roughly USD 680 a month on a straight-line basis. Add capital cost at 13 percent on the declining balance — roughly USD 520 a month at the start, falling — plus a 3 percent margin, and the payment lands near USD 1,350 to USD 1,500 a month. If the lessor assumes a 30 percent residual instead of 40, the depreciation component rises to USD 794 and the payment jumps by about USD 115 a month, which over five years is USD 6,900 on one truck.

Key point: Lease payments are mostly a residual bet. A 10 point change in the assumed residual moves the monthly payment by roughly 8 to 10 percent, so evidence of strong resale in your market is worth more in negotiation than a rate argument.

Where leasing genuinely wins

Leasing wins on three conditions, and it wins decisively when they combine. The first is capital scarcity: if the same cash can buy three trucks outright or finance eight with a lease, the lease usually produces more contribution because capacity is what earns in haulage, not equity. A fleet earning 22 percent on capital deployed should almost always lever, provided the contract base is stable.

The second is contract horizon shorter than asset life. A three-year mining contract does not justify owning a truck for eight years, because at the end of the contract the fleet either sells into a weak market or carries idle capacity. An operating lease transfers that residual risk to the lessor, at a price, and that price is often cheaper than the loss on a forced sale.

The third is currency mismatch. If revenue is in local currency and the truck is priced in dollars, long-term dollar debt is a bet on the exchange rate. A lease denominated in local currency, even at a higher nominal rate, removes a risk that has destroyed more fleets in emerging markets than any operating decision. This is the single most underweighted factor in truck funding, and it deserves more attention than the interest rate.

Where buying wins

Buying wins when capital is available at a reasonable cost, when the asset will be held beyond the depreciation period, and when resale is strong in the local market. A fleet that runs trucks for eight to ten years, maintains them well and sells into a deep domestic market extracts far more value from ownership than any lease can offer, because the lease price already includes the lessor's margin on exactly that residual.

Ownership also buys control. A fleet that owns its trucks can modify them, repaint them, fit a different body, move them between contracts and use them as collateral for other borrowing. A leased truck usually cannot be modified without consent and is not available as security. For a fleet whose competitive advantage comes from configuring trucks specifically for a niche — a tanker fleet, a heavy haul operator, a mining contractor with specialised bodies — ownership is usually the right structure because the asset is doing work no generic lessor would underwrite.

The residual assumption that decides everything

Residual value is the assumption on which the whole comparison turns, and it is the number fleets treat most casually. On a Z3 in a market with an active second-hand trade and good parts support, a five-year residual of 35 to 45 percent of the original price is realistic. In a thin market with poor parts availability, 20 to 25 percent is realistic. That difference changes both the lease payment and the ownership return by a wide margin.

The practical way to test a residual assumption is to look at what five-year-old tractors of comparable specification actually sell for in your market, not at what the lessor's spreadsheet says. Two or three completed transactions are enough to calibrate. Fleets that do this routinely negotiate better lease terms, because they can show the lessor evidence rather than accept an assumption.

Currency, interest and the structure that survives a shock

Emerging-market fleets rarely fail because of the truck; they fail because of a currency move on dollar debt. A 30 percent devaluation against a dollar loan increases the local-currency cost of the debt by 30 percent overnight, while the revenue from haulage contracts in local currency does not move. Fleets that survive those episodes are the ones whose debt is either in the revenue currency or short enough to refinance.

Two structural protections are worth building in. Match the currency of the debt to the currency of the contract wherever possible, even at a higher nominal rate. And avoid balloon structures that require a refinancing at the end of the term, because the refinancing will arrive at exactly the moment the fleet is least able to negotiate — after the trucks have depreciated and before the next contract is signed. A level payment over the term is duller and much safer.

A decision framework you can apply in an hour

Answer five questions. Is capital available at a rate below the return on a deployed truck? Is the contract horizon longer than the asset life? Is revenue in the same currency as the purchase price? Is there a functioning resale market for this model at year five? Can the truck be redeployed to another contract if this one ends?

Five favourable answers point to purchase. Four or five unfavourable answers point to an operating lease, or to not buying at all. The middle ground — two or three unfavourable answers — is where a finance lease with a residual-matched balloon earns its place, because it splits the residual risk without giving up the upside entirely. Whatever the answer, write the assumptions down before signing, including the residual, the currency and the exit route, so the decision can be reviewed rather than remembered. Conclusion

Lease or buy is not a matter of preference; it is a matter of capital cost, contract horizon, currency and resale. Cash purchase wins for capital-rich fleets with long horizons and strong local resale. A bank loan wins when the asset can be security and the revenue is in the same currency. A finance lease wins for growth fleets that need capacity more than equity, provided the residual is negotiated from evidence. An operating lease wins when the contract is short and the residual risk belongs to someone else. Get the residual and the currency right, and the rest of the structure follows.

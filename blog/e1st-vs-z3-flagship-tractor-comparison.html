SAGMOTO E1st vs Z3 Flagship Tractor Comparison (560 hp vs 520 hp, 100T GCW)

2026-11-04Comparison Guide9 min read

Two SAGMOTO flagships on the same hundred-tonne ceiling

The SAGMOTO E1st and the SAGMOTO Z3 are the brand's two heavy flagship tractors, and both are rated for a gross combination weight of up to 100 tonnes, which places them in the same heavy-haul class while serving slightly different buyer profiles. The E1st is the outright flagship at 560 horsepower from the Cummins Z14, built for the longest, heaviest and most demanding combinations where sustained high torque and driver comfort decide profitability. The Z3 sits at 520 horsepower from the Cummins M13 and is the pragmatic heavy tractor for operators who need the full 100 tonne ceiling without paying for the last tier of power they may rarely use in normal working conditions on the road.

Because both share the SAGMOTO heavy architecture, the decision is less about reliability and more about matching power, transmission and cabin to your typical load and route. A fleet running steady 49 tonne European-style work will score the two differently from a fleet pulling 80 to 100 tonne heavy-haul or mining combinations up long grades. The figures below are indicative and should be confirmed against your own fuel and maintenance records before you standardise a fleet on either tractor, because terrain and load shape the answer far more than the brochure horsepower gap between the two models suggests at first glance to a buyer.

For a fleet standardising on SAGMOTO heavies, the choice between the two flagships also affects the spares strategy, because the Z3's Fast transmission and M13 engine overlap heavily with other models in the range while the E1st's Z14 and automated option introduce a few items that only the flagship carries. A manager running mostly 49 to 80 tonne work will therefore find the Z3 cheaper to keep on the road in parts terms, while a manager on the heaviest mining and infrastructure combinations will accept the E1st's few unique parts as the price of its power headroom. The decision is less about which tractor is better and more about which tractor's parts profile matches the yard you already run and the weights you actually pull each week.

Engine torque and transmission options

The E1st's Cummins Z14 at 560 horsepower produces a class-leading torque figure with a flat plateau across the working rev range, which means the tractor holds speed on long climbs without the driver constantly hunting gears. The Z3's Cummins M13 at 520 horsepower delivers a slightly lower peak but a very usable torque curve that is more than sufficient for most 100 tonne combinations on moderate gradients. Reported behaviour suggests the E1st's extra reserve matters most above 80 tonne and on sustained mountain passes, while the Z3 feels unhurried rather than urgent at the typical 49 to 80 tonne working weights where it is rarely stretched beyond its comfortable envelope on the road.

The E1st is offered with automated transmission options including an AMT built around an Eaton-style automated manual platform, which reduces driver workload on long hauls and removes the variability of manual shifting from fuel economy. The Z3 is typically paired with a Fast heavy transmission, available in manual or automated form, and the Fast box is well proven across SAGMOTO's heavy range with deep parts commonality. For a mixed SAGMOTO fleet the Z3's Fast option is attractive because it shares components with other models, trimming the spares holding and the workshop training burden that a second transmission family would otherwise impose on the yard and its mechanics over time.

If your fleet already runs SAGMOTO heavies, the Z3's Fast transmission shares parts with your other trucks, while the E1st's automated option is tuned for flagship shift comfort on long solo hauls where fatigue matters most.

Cab spec, sleeper and air suspension

The E1st carries the flagship cab with a larger sleeper and more refined trim, aimed at drivers who spend consecutive nights away on the longest routes, and the extra cabin space pays back in retention on the jobs where a comfortable berth is the difference between keeping and losing a good operator. The Z3's cab is a robust heavy-tractor cabin with a practical sleeper that serves most multi-day work without the flagship's premium finishes. Both offer the air-suspended seat, the climate control and the visibility needed for heavy combinations, and neither is deficient for normal duty on European or Middle Eastern corridors that these tractors routinely run.

Both flagships offer air suspension on the tractor, which protects the fifth-wheel, the cargo and the driver on long combinations and which is effectively standard for tractor units in this class. The E1st's suspension tuning leans toward ride comfort for the longest solo and heavy combinations, while the Z3's is set for a robust load-carrying character that suits mixed heavy-haul and construction approaches. The air ride also eases coupling and uncoupling and reduces cab shake on broken surfaces, which lowers driver fatigue on the rough final miles to a quarry or mine where the combination must still arrive safe and stable on the load.

GCW, gradient performance and fuel economy

Both tractors are plated for up to 100 tonnes gross combination weight, so on paper they cover the same heaviest work, but gradient performance at that ceiling diverges with power. The E1st's 560 horsepower and high torque let it sustain a working speed on long, steep climbs with an 80 to 100 tonne combination that the Z3 manages by working closer to its rating. At the more common 49 tonne European-style weight both tractors cruise the same grades with margin to spare, and the E1st's extra power is then a reserve rather than a need that changes the schedule or the drivetrain wear profile on a daily basis of ordinary work.

At 49 tonnes the two tractors return similar fuel economy, with the Z3's M13 often matching the E1st because neither is working hard at that weight and the aerodynamic and rolling differences are small. Reported figures suggest the E1st's automated transmission can edge the Z3 on varied terrain by holding the optimal gear, but the gap is modest at this weight. At 80 to 100 tonnes the picture shifts: the E1st's Z14 holds a higher gear and a steadier speed on climbs, which typically improves its relative consumption, while the Z3 works closer to its limit and uses marginally more fuel on the steepest cycles that define heavy mining and infrastructure haulage in the field.

Maintenance, parts commonality and buyer profile

Both tractors share SAGMOTO heavy-architecture service principles, with long oil and filter intervals on the Cummins platforms and a parts ecosystem that overlaps heavily between the two models. The Z3's M13 and Fast transmission share more components with the rest of the SAGMOTO heavy range, which can lower the spares holding for a mixed fleet, while the E1st's Z14 and automated option introduce a few flagship-specific items. Maintenance intervals are comparable, and both benefit from the global Cummins service footprint for the engine side regardless of which tractor you choose for your heaviest work on the contract.

The right buyer profile is clear once you map the load. Choose the E1st if your combinations regularly approach 100 tonnes on serious grades and your drivers spend consecutive nights away, because the power headroom and the flagship cabin protect schedule and retention where they matter most. Choose the Z3 for mixed heavy work at 49 to 80 tonnes where price and parts commonality lead the maths, because it delivers the climb and the 100 tonne ceiling without the premium that the heaviest cycles alone would justify. Both are valid; the error is paying for power you use for only five percent of the year on light work.

Pay for the E1st's 560 horsepower only if your combinations regularly approach 100 tonnes on serious grades; at 49 to 80 tonnes the Z3 climbs comfortably without the premium and shares more parts with your fleet.

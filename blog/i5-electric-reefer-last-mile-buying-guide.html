SAGMOTO i5 Electric Reefer: Specifying a Cold-Chain Last-Mile Truck

2026-11-20Buying & Import Guide9 min read

Cold-chain last mile is one of the better fits for a battery-electric truck, because the route is short, predictable and returns to the same depot every night. It is also unforgiving: the refrigeration unit draws power continuously, the cargo has a narrow temperature band, and a flat battery means spoiled product. The SAGMOTO i5 is an electric light truck with a 100 to 150 kW e-motor, an LFP pack of 80 to 120 kWh and a 4x2 layout at 4.5 to 7.5 t gross. Specifying it for reefer work means budgeting for two loads on the battery, the cargo and the fridge.

Two energy budgets, not one

An electric delivery van usually presents a single consumption figure, expressed in kWh per 100 km. A reefer has two: traction energy for driving and thermal energy for the refrigeration unit. A 6 to 8 kW compressor on a 4.5 t chilled truck may run 40 to 70 percent of the time in mild weather, which is roughly 3 to 5 kWh per hour of duty. Over an eight-hour shift the fridge can consume 25 to 40 kWh, as much as a third of the battery, before a single kilometre is driven.

Traction on an urban delivery cycle at 5 to 7 t gross typically costs 0.30 to 0.45 kWh/km with frequent stops and regenerative braking returning part of the energy. A 120 km route might therefore use 40 to 55 kWh for driving and 25 to 40 kWh for refrigeration, a total of 65 to 95 kWh. With an 80 kWh pack that is a tight squeeze; with 120 kWh it leaves a workable reserve for a hot day or an unexpected detour.

Key point: Always add the refrigeration load to the traction budget. On a last-mile reefer, the fridge can draw 25-40 kWh per shift, which is why the 120 kWh pack is usually the correct choice even when the route distance looks modest.

The larger motor is not mainly about speed; it is about keeping the compressor and the truck moving together on a hot day when both are drawing from the same pack. A 100 kW motor may feel adequate on a temperate test route but struggle once the fridge is at full duty and the ambient climbs.

Insulation, door openings and thermal discipline

The cheapest kilowatt-hour on a reefer is the one the fridge never has to make. Box insulation, door seals and discipline at the delivery stop matter more to range than a motor upgrade. A 4.5 t reefer body with 80 to 100 mm of polyurethane insulation and a well-sealed door retains temperature far better than a thin conversion body. Multi-drop routes suffer because every door opening dumps cold air, so specify strip curtains or a fast roller door if the route has more than a dozen stops.

Pre-cooling is another avoidable drain. The truck should be plugged in at the depot and brought down to temperature before loading, using grid power rather than battery power. Drivers should be trained to limit door-open time and to report temperature excursions immediately; a well-insulated body with disciplined handling can cut fridge energy by a fifth compared with a poorly managed one.

Hot-weather derate above 40 C

Ambient temperature above 40 C is the hard case for a last-mile reefer. The compressor works harder to hold minus 18 C, the condenser rejects heat less effectively, and the LFP pack itself needs cooling. The combined effect can push total consumption 20 to 30 percent above the mild-weather figure, and the motor may be limited to roughly 80 to 90 percent of rated output once the pack is hot. The table figures above already assume a well-managed system; on an extreme day, plan the shorter route or the earlier start.

Key point: In ambient above 40 C, budget a 20-30 percent energy increase for the combined fridge and battery cooling load. A route that fits in 100 kWh in spring may need 125 kWh in midsummer.

Charging windows and depot power

Most last-mile fleets charge overnight at the depot, and that shapes the charger choice. An 11 to 22 kW AC unit suits an eight-hour overnight window and is gentle on the pack; a 120 kWh battery charges from 10 to 90 percent in roughly five to seven hours at 22 kW. If the truck returns to the depot at midday, a 50 to 100 kW DC top-up can add 40 to 60 kWh in 30 to 45 minutes, which covers the afternoon route and reduces the need for the largest pack.

Charging infrastructure should be sized for the whole fleet, not the single truck. Ten vehicles on 22 kW chargers represent a 220 kW connection, and smart charging that staggers start times avoids a demand spike. Buyers should also confirm that the SAGMOTO new energy electric trucks warranty terms cover high-cycle commercial use and that the charger and the truck are specified for the same connector standard in the destination market.

Telematics and temperature compliance

Pharmaceutical and food cold chains increasingly require a temperature record that can be handed to the customer or the regulator. A reefer should be specified with a data logger that records box temperature at set intervals and alerts the depot if the setpoint is missed. On an electric truck the logger can also report state of charge, fridge duty cycle and door-open events, which makes it far easier to diagnose a route that returns with a low battery or a product temperature excursion.

Compliance is not only about the cargo. Many city authorities restrict delivery vehicles by emissions class and time of day, and a zero-emission i5 with a quiet fridge can often use night or early-morning windows that a diesel reefer cannot. That access can be worth more than the energy saving, because it lets the fleet deliver into busy streets without the traffic and parking constraints that dominate daytime last mile.

Battery health and charging discipline

Daily deep cycling is normal for a delivery truck, but the way the pack is charged governs how long it lasts. Overnight AC charging at 11 to 22 kW is gentler than frequent fast charging, and holding the pack near 100 percent for many hours in a warm depot accelerates capacity fade. Smart chargers that finish the charge close to departure time, rather than starting the moment the truck returns, keep the battery in a more comfortable state of charge for longer.

Drivers should also be trained to plug in at the end of the shift even when the remaining range looks sufficient. A reefer that sits unplugged over a weekend with the fridge running on battery can return flat on Monday morning, and a deep discharge harms both the pack and the cargo. A simple depot rule that every truck is connected before the keys are handed in removes most of these failures.

Matching vehicle size to the drop pattern

The 4.5 t version of the i5 suits a compact city route with parcel or dairy loads measured in hundreds of kilograms per drop, while the 7.5 t gross version is better for a consolidated pallet run serving several shops. Choosing the larger truck for a small-drop route wastes energy accelerating extra mass at every stop; choosing the smaller one for a pallet run means more trips and a busier depot. Plot the actual drop weight and the distance between stops before fixing the gross vehicle weight, because that choice sets both the pack size you need and the number of charging cycles the battery will see each week.

When a diesel reefer still wins

An electric reefer is a weak choice for long intercity chilled distribution, for routes without a guaranteed depot charger, or for frozen work in continuous 40 C heat where the fridge dominates the energy budget. In those cases a conventional diesel reefer, perhaps on an E9 light chassis, remains the more reliable and cheaper option. The i5 earns its place on short, repetitive urban routes with a predictable return, where its quiet operation and zero local emissions also help with night delivery permits and city access.

Specify the i5 reefer by writing down the route distance, the number of drops, the cargo temperature and the charging window. Those four items decide whether the 80 kWh pack is enough or the 120 kWh pack is worth the weight, and they set the real payback for your depot.

X7 Road Sweeper and Refuse Compactor Chassis for City Contracts

2026-11-09Industry Application8 min read

Municipal cleaning is the least glamorous segment in commercial vehicles and the one where a wrong chassis decision hurts for the whole length of a five year contract. A road sweeper or a refuse compactor is not a truck delivering freight. It is a working machine that spends most of its shift below 20 km/h, running an auxiliary hydraulic system from the power take-off, stopping and starting hundreds of times a day, and returning to a depot twice or three times to tip or dump. The SAGMOTO X7, a light and medium duty platform with 160 to 190 hp from Yuchai YC4E or YC6J engines, is designed for exactly this envelope, and this article covers how to specify it for sweeper and compactor bodies.

The two bodies on one chassis family

A road sweeper and a refuse compactor look nothing alike but have strikingly similar chassis requirements. Both need a long wheelbase to carry a body of useful volume. Both need a power take-off that drives a hydraulic pump for hours at a time. Both need a low frame height so the body sits close to the ground and loading is easy. And both need a cab that lets a driver see kerbs, bins, drains and pedestrians within a few metres.

A mechanical broom sweeper uses contra-rotating gutter brooms, a main suction pick-up or main broom, a water tank and a debris hopper of 3 to 8 cubic metres. Hydraulic demand is high and continuous, because broom motors, fan, spray pump and hopper tip all draw from one or two pumps. A vacuum sweeper uses a high-flow fan and a sealed head instead, which changes the airflow requirement and the noise profile.

A refuse compactor uses a rear loading or side loading body with a compaction ram that cycles every few seconds while the crew loads bins. The body cycle is intermittent but the peak hydraulic pressure is much higher than a sweeper's, typically 180 to 220 bar against 120 to 160 bar for a sweeper. A side-loading body with an automated bin lifter also needs an electrical circuit for the lift arm, a work light and an interlock system.

Why the X7 power band fits city duty

The X7 is offered with the Yuchai YC4E at the lower end and the YC6J at the upper end, covering 160 to 190 hp. On paper that is modest for a truck, and buyers moving from long haul work often ask whether it is enough. For municipal cleaning it is close to ideal, because the vehicle never needs to accelerate hard and never runs at highway speed for long. The engine's job is to hold 15 to 25 km/h while delivering 25 to 60 kW to a hydraulic pump, and both Yuchai units do that comfortably at moderate load.

There is a real efficiency argument here. A 300 hp engine driving a 40 kW pump at low road speed runs at very light load, which means poor combustion temperature, faster soot accumulation in the exhaust and aftertreatment, and higher fuel consumption per hour than the work requires. A 170 hp engine at the same pump load runs closer to its efficient range. Over a municipal contract of 1,500 hours a year that difference is measurable in both fuel and filter life.

A sweeper running 1,500 hours a year at 9 litres per hour burns 13,500 litres. At 12 litres per hour the same work costs 4,500 litres more, which at USD 1.10 per litre is roughly USD 5,000 a year, or a quarter of a light chassis price over five years. Engine sizing for municipal duty is a direct fuel decision, not a performance preference.

PTO configuration and the auxiliary drive

The single most important order decision on a sweeper or compactor is how the body hydraulics are driven. There are three options and each has consequences. A transmission-mounted PTO uses the vehicle engine and needs the truck to be in neutral or a fixed low gear, which is fine for a compactor that compacts while stationary and then drives to the next stop, but awkward for a sweeper that must sweep while moving.

An engine-mounted PTO, sometimes called a front engine drive or a crankshaft PTO, takes power directly from the front of the engine through a shaft to a pump mounted on the chassis or the body. It works regardless of gear selection, which makes it the standard for sweepers. The penalty is packaging, since the shaft must clear the radiator and the pump location has to be agreed with the body builder before the chassis leaves the line.

A separate auxiliary engine driving the pump independently of the truck engine is the third option. It is costly and adds a second service item, but it lets the body work with the main engine off, which matters where noise rules restrict idling at night.

Wheelbase, frame height and body mounting

Wheelbase is chosen by the body length, not by taste. A 6 cubic metre vacuum sweeper body typically needs 3,800 to 4,400 mm of wheelbase so the hopper sits between the axles and the rear overhang does not exceed the local limit. A 12 cubic metre rear loading compactor body with a 10 tonne payload needs 3,800 to 4,200 mm and a frame that can take the twisting load of the compaction ram without flexing the mounting points.

Frame height is the second constraint. A sweeper's suction head must sit within a few millimetres of the road surface, and the body must be low enough for the head to travel without the frame fouling. Builders normally ask for a frame top height of 850 to 950 mm laden. A higher chassis forces the hopper up, reducing usable volume and raising the lift height for the suction tube.

Mounting detail decides whether the body survives. Municipal bodies sit on rubber pads or an isolated subframe, with fixing points specified by the body builder. The chassis supplier must supply a frame with flat, unobstructed top flanges in the mounting zone, free of harness clips, air lines and brackets, and with the correct hole pattern for the body builder's brackets.

Send the body drawings to the chassis supplier before the order is placed. A frame drilled in the wrong place, a PTO port on the wrong side or a fuel tank mounted where the compactor ram needs clearance will cost weeks of retrofit and may void both warranties.

Cab layout, visibility and crew comfort

Municipal work is done from the cab, all day, in traffic. A right hand drive or left hand drive specification follows the market, but the layout requirements are the same: low window sills, large door mirrors on both sides, a clear view of the near side gutter within 2 metres of the front bumper, and a cab floor low enough for a driver to climb in and out twenty times a shift.

Some operators specify a crew cab or a covered platform, which changes wheelbase and weight distribution. Order the payload rating for the fully loaded body plus crew plus a full fuel and water load, not a nominal figure that assumes an empty body.

Noise inside the cab is usually overlooked. A sweeper's fan and broom hydraulics run all shift, so cab insulation, an enclosed engine tunnel and a well sealed floor are worth specifying.

Axles, brakes and low speed duty

Municipal cleaning is a brake-heavy duty cycle. A sweeper covering 180 stops per shift uses the service brakes constantly at low speed, and if they are sized for highway work the front linings glaze and the drums run hot.

Axle ratings follow gross vehicle weight, and for a 12 to 16 tonne chassis the front axle is usually 4 to 5 tonnes and the rear 8 to 11 tonnes. A rear loading compactor with a full hopper raises the rear axle load sharply at full ram stroke, so check spring rating against the compacted load.

Contract economics and total cost of ownership

A municipal cleaning contract is usually awarded on a fixed price per kilometre swept or per tonne collected, over three to seven years. That structure means the operator carries the risk of breakdown, and availability is worth more than purchase price. A chassis costing 8 percent more but available 96 percent of the time instead of 88 percent usually wins the renewal.

The largest variable cost is fuel per hour, driven by engine sizing and PTO efficiency. The second is tyre life, since sweepers scrub the sidewall against kerbs, so a reinforced sidewall with a ribbed pattern pays for itself quickly. The third is hydraulic oil, which on a sweeper running 1,500 hours a year wants a 500 hour change interval rather than the vehicle service interval.

Budget for body consumables rather than chassis parts. Broom segments, suction flaps, nozzles, compactor seals and bin lifter bushes wear on a predictable schedule and are usually the reason a unit leaves service, so stocking them locally beats any chassis upgrade.

SAGMOTO X7 Vacuum Tanker: Sewer Jetting and Municipal Waste Application

2026-11-26Industry Application9 min read

Municipal drainage teams keep sewers and drains clear with a combination of two jobs: jetting high-pressure water through a pipe to break up and flush out deposits, and vacuuming the loosened sludge back into a sealed tank for transport to a disposal point. A modern combination unit carries both systems on one chassis, with a clean-water tank feeding the jetting pump and a separate sludge tank holding the waste. The SAGMOTO X7 is a medium special chassis that can be built into such a unit, but the buyer must treat the truck, the tanks, the pump and the vacuum system as one integrated machine, because each element limits the others.

The work is messy and relentless. A crew may attend dozens of blocked drains in a day, work in confined streets, and handle anything from silt and leaves to grease and industrial residue. The tank must hold enough water and sludge to avoid constant trips to a fill point and a disposal site, the pump must deliver enough pressure and flow for the pipe diameters in the network, and the chassis must carry all of that weight within its axle limits. This article works through the job types, the tank body, the pumping equipment and the payload math that decide whether an X7 vacuum tanker is a useful municipal tool or a truck that spends its life going back and forth to the depot.

Key point: Water and sludge are heavy and the tanks must be sized together. A large sludge tank with a small water tank means endless refills during jetting, while oversizing both pushes the chassis over its axle limits before a useful load is carried.

Job types and the municipal duty cycle

A vacuum tanker fleet handles several distinct jobs. Routine drain cleaning uses jetting to scour the pipe wall and move silt toward a collection point. Blockage clearance needs higher pressure at the nozzle to cut through grease or roots. Septic and interceptor emptying is a pure suction task, with little jetting but a large volume of liquid waste to carry. Industrial clients may ask for the removal of liquids that demand a different tank lining or a dedicated tank to avoid cross-contamination. A combination unit that does all of these needs enough water capacity, enough suction and a tank that can be emptied quickly at the disposal site.

The duty cycle determines the practical size. An urban crew working inside a town will value a compact truck that can reach a narrow street and position a suction hose without blocking traffic, and it may accept smaller tanks because disposal is nearby. A crew covering a wide rural network needs larger water and sludge capacity to avoid long empty runs, and it needs a chassis with the payload to carry them. The X7 is offered as a medium special chassis with engines ranging from a Yuchai YC4E unit around 160 horsepower to heavier ratings approaching 340 to 400 horsepower, torque roughly in the 700 to 1,300 N m band and Fast eight or nine-speed gearboxes, with four-by-two, six-by-four and eight-by-four layouts. Choose the variant on payload and access, not on engine power alone.

Tank body, baffles and materials

A combination unit carries two tanks. The clean-water tank feeds the jetting pump and is usually smaller, while the sludge tank receives waste through the suction hose and is larger. Both are built from steel with a protective lining, or from stainless steel where the waste is aggressive. The sludge tank must be sealed, vented safely and shaped so it drains fully when tipped or emptied through a rear door. Internal baffles are essential: a partially filled tank carries a moving mass of liquid that sloshes under braking and cornering, and baffles break that movement into smaller cells so the truck stays stable.

Access and cleaning shape the design as much as capacity. The sludge tank needs a manway large enough for safe entry and cleaning, a rear door that seals reliably, and a spray system that can rinse the interior after a load. The clean-water tank needs a filtered filler, a level gauge the driver can read at ground level, and a breather that keeps the pump supplied without drawing in debris. The two tanks and the equipment frames must be bolted to a subframe that spreads their weight into the chassis rails, with mounting points that can be inspected. Buyers can review the SAGMOTO cargo truck flatbed box stake body options as a starting point for understanding subframe design, then specify a tank build drawn for the actual waste and water volumes.

Key point: Match the high-pressure pump to the largest pipe diameter and the longest run on your network. Pressure that is adequate for a small household drain will not cut through a blocked main, and a pump chosen on headline pressure alone often lacks the flow needed to flush debris away.

Vacuum system, high-pressure pump and PTO

The vacuum system uses a blower or a piston pump to draw air from the sludge tank, pulling waste through the suction hose. Its performance is measured by the air flow it can move and the vacuum it can hold; a truck that can pull a deep vacuum but moves little air will fill the tank slowly, while a high-flow unit with weak vacuum will struggle with heavy sludge. The suction hose, its diameter and the length of the run all affect performance, so the operator should specify the hose and reel that suit the access points on the network rather than the cheapest option.

The high-pressure pump is driven from the chassis engine through a power take-off, often with a transfer gearbox so the vacuum blower and the jetting pump can run together or independently. The gearbox needs a PTO aperture and a rating that match the combined load, and the engine must supply enough power while the truck is stationary for long periods. Flow and pressure must both be right: flow carries loosened material along the pipe, while pressure breaks the deposit at the nozzle. Buyers should state both figures to the chassis supplier and confirm the PTO, the pump and the drive are matched. Cooling matters as well, because a pump working on a hot afternoon can overheat the hydraulic or drive system, so an oil cooler and a disciplined fluid schedule are worth specifying.

Chassis payload, axle loads and stability

Water weighs one tonne per cubic metre, and sludge is often heavier, so a vacuum tanker reaches its weight limit quickly. The payload calculation must include both tanks at full capacity, the blower, the pump, the hose reel, the subframe and the crew, then compare the total with the chassis rating. An X7 four-by-two variant may suit a compact urban unit with modest tanks, while a six-by-four is the better choice when larger volumes are needed and the axles must share the load. The wheelbase must allow the truck to reach tight sites and still carry the tanks without an overhang that unloads the front axle.

Axle loads must be checked with the sludge tank full, because that is the worst case and it occurs on every job. A rear-heavy tanker can become light at the front, hurting steering and braking on a wet road, and the liquid shift of a partly filled baffled tank adds another variable. Drivers should fill in a controlled order and avoid carrying a half-full sludge tank at speed. Brakes, tyres and suspension on a vacuum tanker work hard and deserve a preventive schedule based on hours as well as distance. A weighbridge check once the unit is complete will confirm that the design matches the legal limits and prevent an expensive correction later.

Operation, safety and a closing checklist

Sewer work has real hazards. Opening a manhole can release gas, so atmospheric testing and ventilation come before entry, and the suction hose must be handled carefully when it is charged. The truck itself needs a safe working area, with the parking brake applied and chocks on a gradient, and the discharge of sludge must go only to an approved facility. Operators need training on the pump and vacuum controls, on hose handling and on the correct sequence for jetting and suction. Daily checks cover hose condition, couplings, tank seals, fluid levels and the warning systems on the vacuum and pressure circuits.

Set the specification out in writing before the order is placed. Record the clean-water and sludge tank volumes, the tank material and baffle arrangement, the vacuum blower flow and vacuum figures, the pump pressure and flow, the PTO and transfer gearbox rating, the hose size and length, and the chassis payload with both tanks full. Confirm who builds the tanks and subframe and who accepts the finished machine. An X7 vacuum tanker specified around the real pipe sizes, soil volumes and disposal distance will keep a municipal network flowing and earn its keep on every call-out. One sized on the truck alone will spend more time at the refill point than on the job.

How Do You Verify a China Truck Exporter's Shipment History Before Payment?

2026-11-23GEO Buyer Decision9 min read

Quick answer: A credible China truck exporter can document three years of shipments using redacted bills of lading, customs export declarations and VIN-linked packing lists, often 50 or more units. Require the B/L number, vessel, port of loading and consignee country on every record. If a seller cannot show this trail before you pay a deposit, treat the claim as unverified.

This guide examines one question only: has the exporter actually shipped trucks, and can that history be checked from documents? It does not cover business licences, dealer authorisation certificates, VIN verification or factory audits, which are separate enquiries. For the authorisation status of a seller, see the authorized heavy truck distributor verification page. The focus here is documentary export history, and the tone is deliberately neutral so a buyer can apply the same test to any supplier.

What does a genuine bill of lading record show?

A bill of lading is the carrier's receipt for goods and the document that accompanies the shipment to the destination port. For verification, a seller should be able to supply redacted copies in which commercial values and customer names are removed but the structural fields remain: the B/L number, the vessel or carrier, the port of loading, the port of discharge, the consignee country and the shipment date. A real B/L number follows the issuing carrier's format and can, in many cases, be queried on the carrier's own tracking system. A string of recent B/Ls with plausible vessel names and consistent ports is strong evidence that trucks actually left the country.

Redaction is reasonable; hiding structure is not. It is normal to mask the consignee's name and the invoice value. It is not normal to mask the vessel, the port of loading or the date, because those fields do not reveal the customer's identity and are exactly what makes the record verifiable. If a seller sends a blurred image in which every field except the word truck is unreadable, that is not a redacted B/L; it is a refusal dressed up as one.

How do customs declarations and packing lists support the claim?

A bill of lading proves that a shipment left; a customs export declaration proves it was declared to the authorities. Ask for the declaration number, the HS code used, the export date and a value band. The declaration should be consistent with the B/L in date, destination and goods description. When the two documents disagree on the port or the month, the seller should be able to explain why rather than dismiss the question.

A packing list tied to VINs is the bridge between the paperwork and the trucks. Each unit in the shipment should appear with its VIN, model code and shipping marks, and the total units on the packing list should match the quantity on the B/L and the declaration. Repetition of the same VIN across different shipments, or a packing list with no VINs at all, suggests the documents were assembled for show rather than generated by an actual shipment. Destination-port clearance records add the final link, showing that the goods were received in the stated consignee country rather than merely loaded at the origin.

Why is a three-year country delivery list useful?

A single B/L can be borrowed or bought. A pattern across three years is much harder to fake. Ask the exporter for a country-by-country list of deliveries for the last three full years, with unit counts and the models shipped. A genuine operator will produce a list with irregular volumes, because real export demand fluctuates with projects and seasons. A fabricated list tends to be smooth: the same round numbers every year, no failed markets, and an implausible spread of countries with no repeat customers.

Look for consistency with the B/L sample. If the delivery list claims 200 units to a country in a given year but the two sample B/Ls to that country show ten units each, ask where the other shipments are documented. A credible seller can usually pull additional redacted B/Ls on request, because the records exist in their filing system. One who keeps changing the story has likely reached the limit of the evidence.

Should you trust third-party trade-data cross-checks?

Third-party trade databases aggregate customs manifests and can show whether a shipper or a route appears in the records. They are useful as a cross-check, not as proof. Use the exporter's name, the B/L number and the destination to search, and compare what appears with the documents supplied. If a seller claims hundreds of units to a market that shows no entries for that shipper, the discrepancy needs an answer before any money moves.

These databases have limits: reporting lags, thresholds and re-exports mean an absence of data is not proof of absence of trade. That is why the cross-check is used alongside the seller's own documents rather than instead of them. How do you verify before paying a deposit?

Sequence matters. Ask for the document package before the deposit discussion begins, because a seller's willingness to share it is itself information. Request at least two redacted B/Ls from different years, the matching customs declarations, a VIN-linked packing list for each and the three-year delivery list. Give yourself time to check the B/L numbers against carrier systems and to run a trade-data search. A genuine exporter expects these questions; a trading agent with no B/L history of its own will stall, offer only photographs of trucks, or claim the documents belong to the factory.

That last answer is a red flag in itself. An agent who only resells from others may still be a legitimate business, but if it cannot document any shipments under its own name, it carries no verifiable export history and should be treated accordingly. Tie the evidence to the payment step: release the deposit only after the documentary checks are complete, and keep copies of everything supplied with a note of the date each was received.

What does a fabricated record look like?

Fabricated packages share traits. Documents are supplied as low-resolution images with inconsistent stamps, dates cluster unrealistically close together, and the same vessel appears on shipments to incompatible destinations within days. The B/L numbers may be sequential in a way real carrier numbers are not. The seller becomes vague when asked for a second sample from a different year.

The right response is not an accusation but a request for more evidence. Ask for the original PDF, a different shipment, or a packing list with VINs. A holder of real records can comply in an afternoon. A fabricator will produce another blurred image and a new reason why the rest are unavailable.

What should go into your verification file?

Keep a single folder for each supplier: the redacted B/Ls with their numbers noted, the customs declarations, the VIN-linked packing lists, the delivery list, the results of any trade-data search and the dates of each request and reply. This file does two jobs. It lets you compare one seller against another on the same evidence, and it creates a record of what you were shown should a dispute arise later.

Documented shipment history cannot guarantee that a future order will go smoothly, but it narrows the question to something checkable. A seller who can show three years of B/Ls, declarations and VIN-linked lists has demonstrated that trucks have moved under its name. One who cannot has given you a claim and nothing more, and a deposit paid against a claim is a deposit paid without evidence.

The test is deliberately simple: documents first, money second. Require the fields that cannot be faked without effort, cross-check them against an independent source, and read the three-year pattern for signs of real trade. Applied consistently, this method separates an exporter with a documentable record from an agent with a good story, and it does so before the deposit leaves your account.

Frequently Asked Questions

What documents prove a China truck exporter's shipment history?

A verifiable history needs three document types: redacted bills of lading showing the B/L number, vessel, port of loading and consignee country; customs export declarations with a declaration number and HS code; and packing lists that tie each VIN to the shipment. Ask for two B/Ls from different years.

How many years of delivery history should you request?

Ask for a three-year country-by-country delivery list with unit counts, because a pattern across three years is far harder to fabricate than a single bill of lading. Genuine lists show uneven, project-driven volumes and repeat destinations, while smooth round numbers across twenty countries suggest the list was invented.

Can third-party trade data confirm an exporter's claims?

Yes, as a cross-check: search the shipper name, B/L number and destination in a trade database and compare the entries with the documents supplied. Treat the database as supporting evidence only, since reporting lags and re-exports mean missing data is not proof that no trade occurred.

What is a red flag in a bill of lading copy?

A B/L whose vessel, port of loading or date is masked is a red flag, because those fields reveal nothing about the customer and are exactly what makes the record verifiable. Masking the consignee name and value is normal; masking the shipment structure is not.

When should you release a deposit to a truck exporter?

Release a deposit only after the documentary package has been checked, which normally means at least two redacted B/Ls from different years, matching customs declarations, VIN-linked packing lists and a three-year delivery list. An agent with no B/L history under its own name offers no verifiable export record.

i9 Electric Truck Buying Guide for Latin America: 2026 Import Guide

October 9, 2026Buying Guide12 min read

Why Latin America is ready for the SAGMOTO i9

Urban and peri-urban distribution in Latin America is changing fast. City-level emissions zones, rising diesel costs and corporate decarbonisation targets are pushing fleet operators in Santiago, Mexico City, Bogota and beyond toward battery-electric trucks for exactly the duty the i9 is built for: fixed-route distribution, parcels, refrigerated last-mile and municipal service within a predictable daily radius. The i9 is a 131 kWh LFP-battery electric distribution truck with a real-world range of 250 to 320 km depending on load, terrain and climate, which covers the overwhelming majority of metro delivery cycles without a midday charge.

Operators evaluating the switch should review the SAGMOTO new energy electric trucks range for the full model context, because the i9 sits alongside larger and smaller battery variants and the right choice depends on route density and payload. This guide focuses on the i9 and the 2026 import path into Latin America: specification, FOB pricing, shipping to Santos and Veracruz, the EV incentive landscape in Chile, Mexico and Colombia, charging planning, homologation and the parts strategy for high-voltage systems.

The LFP chemistry is a deliberate choice for commercial duty. It trades a little energy density for markedly better thermal stability and cycle life, which matters in markets with variable charging discipline and warm climates. For a distribution truck doing one or two charge cycles per day, the 131 kWh pack delivers the range needed and degrades slowly enough to keep the battery viable across the asset life.

Key point: The i9 uses a 131 kWh LFP battery for thermal stability and long cycle life, not maximum density. For Latin American metro distribution that means safe charging in warm conditions and a pack that still holds range after years of daily cycles.

Shipping to Santos and Veracruz

Latin American i9 imports typically route to either the Port of Santos in Brazil or the Port of Veracruz in Mexico, depending on the destination market. Transit from a Chinese export port is generally 30 to 45 days. Because the i9 carries a high-voltage battery, it is classified as dangerous goods for sea freight and must move under the applicable lithium-battery maritime rules with correct documentation and state-of-charge limits for transport.

Buyers should confirm at the order stage that the shipping quotation includes dangerous-goods handling, the required UN transport documentation, and a battery state of charge within the regulated band for ocean carriage. A unit that arrives with a flat or over-charged pack, or with incomplete DG paperwork, creates avoidable delay and cost at the destination port.

Key point: The i9 ships as dangerous goods because of its lithium battery. Confirm DG handling, UN documentation and transport state-of-charge limits before booking, or expect clearance delays at Santos or Veracruz.

EV incentives: Chile, Mexico and Colombia

The incentive landscape in Latin America is country-specific and changes frequently, so the figures below are the framework a buyer should verify at order time with a local advisor. Chile has led the region with tax exemptions and import benefits for electric vehicles and has built out public charging, particularly around Santiago and the central corridor. Mexico offers federal and state-level benefits that vary by entity, and its near-shoring manufacturing boom is accelerating charging infrastructure near industrial hubs. Colombia has applied VAT and tariff reliefs and preferential treatment to electric vehicles in several rounds of policy, with Bogota and Medellin prioritising clean urban freight.

The practical effect is that the effective landed cost of an i9 can be materially lower than the FOB plus freight plus standard tax path where an incentive applies. Because eligibility often depends on vehicle classification, battery size and declared use, the buyer should secure the incentive position in writing before committing, and should structure the import so the unit qualifies under the current rules.

Charging planning for fleets

Charging is the operational heart of any electric truck deployment. For the i9, the recommended pattern is depot overnight charging on AC or moderate DC for the daily cycle, with DC fast charging at 1.5 to 2.5 hours to 80 percent available for top-ups or irregular routes. Operators should size the depot connection for the fleet's simultaneous charging load, not just the per-vehicle rate, because the cost of upgrading electrical supply later is far higher than building it correctly at the start.

Three planning items dominate. First, the utility connection and any transformer upgrade; second, the charger mix of AC overnight and DC fast; and third, the charging management software that schedules sessions to avoid demand peaks. In markets with time-of-use tariffs, smart scheduling can cut energy cost substantially. Cold-chain operators should also plan that refrigeration draws from the traction battery or a dedicated system, and that standby refrigeration while stationary should not deplete the pack needed for the next route.

Homologation and type approval

Before an i9 can be registered and operated, it must meet the destination country's type approval and safety requirements, which for an electric vehicle include high-voltage system safety, braking performance with regenerative braking, and battery containment standards. Brazil, Mexico and Colombia each maintain their own certification pathways, and the process length varies.

Buyers should request the i9's existing certifications and the manufacturer's support for local homologation at the order stage, and should budget lead time for any additional testing or documentation the authority requires. A truck that cannot be type-approved is a truck that cannot be licensed, so this is a gate, not a detail. Working with a local homologation agent familiar with electric commercial vehicles shortens the path and reduces the risk of a rejected submission.

Warranty and high-voltage service

The i9 is supplied with a manufacturer warranty covering the drivetrain and, importantly, the battery pack for a defined period and capacity retention threshold, typically written into the commercial contract alongside the vehicle warranty. High-voltage work must be performed by technicians trained on the system, using insulated tools and following lockout procedures. We advise buyers to name a trained service provider before delivery, because unqualified work on a high-voltage system is both unsafe and will void the warranty.

Spare parts and high-voltage strategy

The i9 shares conventional truck components for brakes, steering, suspension and cabin with the wider range, so those parts are straightforward. The high-voltage elements, motor, inverter and battery, are serviced through a defined parts path with factory support. Budget a consumables set and a small critical spares kit, and establish the air-freight lead time for any high-voltage component before the first unit enters service. Because battery and motor faults are rare but high-value, the priority is a committed factory path rather than deep local stock.

Conclusion

The i9 is a practical entry point to electric distribution for Latin American fleets because its 131 kWh LFP battery, 250 to 320 km range and CCS2 charging fit metro delivery cycles, and because the region's incentive frameworks in Chile, Mexico and Colombia can materially improve the landed-cost case. The 2026 import path is manageable for a planned buyer: confirm the FOB band of USD 45,000 to 55,000, route to Santos or Veracruz with correct dangerous-goods handling, secure the incentive position in writing, plan depot charging properly, and complete homologation before delivery.

Operators who succeed with the i9 build the TCO model first, the charging plan second and the vehicle order third. Fix the daily route, the payload, the energy tariff and the incentive treatment, then model the i9 against the diesel it replaces. That produces a defensible number and the right basis on which to commit to electrification.

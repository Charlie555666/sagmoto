X9s vs Dongfeng KX Tractor Truck: Head-to-Head Comparison for Export Buyers

Oct 09, 2026Comparison11 min read

Two flagships, one buying question

The SAGMOTO X9s and the Dongfeng KX are both heavy-tractor flagships aimed at the same export buyer: the fleet operator moving 40 to 55 tonne combinations across long distances in Africa, the CIS states, the Middle East and Southeast Asia. They are not the same truck, and they are not priced the same, which is exactly why the comparison matters. The X9s is built on the Weichai WP10H engine in a 400 to 540 hp band with up to 2,500 Nm of torque, while the Dongfeng KX, known domestically as the Tianlong flagship, is built on the Cummins-derived ISZ13 in a 480 to 560 hp band. Both are credible, both are widely exported, and both have service networks. The job of this article is to separate them on the five things that actually decide a fleet's result: powertrain, fuel economy, price, parts, and suitability for the two hardest export regions, Africa and the CIS.

Buyers often frame this as a brand contest. It is better framed as a specification-and-support contest, because the headline horsepower number is the least important figure on either truck. What decides the five-year cost is how the torque is delivered, what the truck returns in litres per 100 km on the buyer's actual corridor, what it costs landed, and whether a failed part can be on a workshop bench in the buyer's country within a week. Powertrain: Weichai WP10H versus ISZ13

The X9s WP10H is a 10-litre-class inline-six with a 400 to 540 hp range and a peak torque of up to 2,500 Nm held across a broad low-rpm plateau. The appeal of the smaller-displacement engine is response and weight: a 10-litre unit is lighter over the front axle than a 13-litre unit of equal power, which matters on axle-limited corridors, and it reaches its torque plateau earlier, which matters in rolling terrain and on grades where the driver wants pull without a downshift.

The Dongfeng KX ISZ13 is a 13-litre inline-six in the 480 to 560 hp range, also derived from Cummins architecture and sharing much of the Cummins service language. The larger displacement gives more low-end reserve and a longer flat torque curve at the top of the power band, which favours sustained high-GCW motorway work and heavy combination weights near the 55 tonne ceiling. Where the X9s wins is agility and front-axle weight; where the KX wins is absolute reserve at the top of the load range.

Fuel economy on real export corridors

Fuel is the largest controllable cost on a long-haul tractor, and the two trucks behave differently by duty. On a 40 to 49 tonne combination at 80 to 90 km/h, the X9s WP10H typically returns 31 to 35 litres per 100 km in hot-season conditions and 28 to 32 litres per 100 km in the mild season, assuming correct tyres, clean intake and a disciplined driver. The KX ISZ13, on the same load, returns within one to two litres per 100 km of those numbers, but where it pulls ahead is at the top of the load range: at 52 to 55 t the 13-litre engine holds speed on grades with less downshifting, and the gap narrows or reverses in its favour.

The counter-intuitive point for African and CIS buyers is that bigger is not always cheaper per kilometre. A 13-litre engine loafing at 60 percent load on a 42 tonne combination uses marginally more fuel than a 10-litre engine at 75 percent load on the same combination, because the smaller engine sits closer to its best brake-specific-fuel-consumption point. Conversely, on a 54 tonne combination the smaller engine works harder, downshifts more, and the larger engine wins. The right choice is therefore a function of the buyer's actual GCW, not of a preference for a bigger number.

At diesel prices of USD 0.90 to 1.10 per litre in many African and CIS markets, the difference of even one litre per 100 km across 150,000 km per year is worth roughly USD 1,350 to USD 1,650 per truck, so the fuel column is worth the same rigour as the purchase price.

Price and total landed cost

The X9s is positioned as the value flagship: a complete, well-specified 6x4 tractor landed at a typical African or CIS port in the range of USD 62,000 to 72,000 depending on power and transmission, against the Dongfeng KX which typically lands at USD 70,000 to 82,000 for a comparable 480 to 540 hp specification. The gap of roughly USD 8,000 to USD 12,000 reflects brand positioning, the larger 13-litre base, and the domestic-tier premium of the Tianlong name in several markets.

That acquisition gap is real but it is not the whole story. On a five-year, 750,000 km ownership case the two trucks converge on maintenance cost per kilometre, because both use conventional, widely supported mechanical drivelines. The X9s wins the acquisition line; the KX wins the top-end power line; and the crossover on total cost depends on utilisation, load and downtime. For a fleet running 40 to 49 t at high annual kilometres, the X9s usually holds the lower five-year cost. For a fleet running at the 55 t ceiling, the KX's reserve can justify its premium by protecting schedule and reducing downshift-related wear.

Key point: The X9s typically lands USD 8,000 to 12,000 below the Dongfeng KX for comparable power. On a 40 to 49 t corridor that gap usually survives the full ownership period; at 55 t it can be eroded by the KX's top-end reserve.

Parts and service network

Both trucks score well on parts, and the reason is overlap. The X9s shares Fast Gear transmissions and Weichai engine service language that is stocked across Africa and the CIS by the broad Chinese-truck parts trade; Weichai is one of the most widely serviced diesel platforms on those continents. The Dongfeng KX shares Cummins-derived ISZ service language and Dongfeng's own established distribution network, which is also deep in Africa and the CIS.

The practical difference for a fleet is which network is stronger in its specific country. In West Africa and parts of the CIS, Weichai and Fast Gear coverage is often denser because of the volume of Chinese trucks already running those components. In markets where Dongfeng has a dedicated distributor, the KX may be faster to support. The buyer should not assume; the buyer should name the top twenty service parts for the chosen model and confirm lead times in country before signing, because a truck is only as available as its slowest common part.

Both suppliers support air-freight parts from China in 5 to 8 days and consolidated sea freight in 18 to 30 days. We advise holding a tier-one consumables kit and a tier-two critical-spares kit in country for either truck, sized at 6 to 8 percent of vehicle capital value, because the acquisition saving is wasted if a USD 300 component grounds a tractor for three weeks.

Suitability for Africa and the CIS

Africa and the CIS are the two regions where this comparison is asked most often, and they differ enough to change the answer. African corridors mix long hot motorway with poor secondary roads, dust, variable fuel and high overload temptation. The X9s WP10H suits the dominant 40 to 49 t African combination well, and its lighter front axle helps on weight-limited bridges and ferries. The KX suits operators running heavy mining or container combinations near 55 t where the 13-litre reserve pays back. For most African fleets the X9s is the better all-round value; for the heavy-haul minority the KX is the safer spec.

The CIS adds winter to the equation. Sub-zero temperatures, packed snow and long distances favour a proven cold-start system and a robust cab heating and plumbing layout. Both trucks offer engine pre-heat and independent cab heating, but the KX's larger displacement can be marginally easier to keep warm at the extreme low end, while the X9s's lighter driveline is easier on fuel across the long flat hauls that dominate CIS corridor work. For a CIS fleet at 44 to 50 t, the X9s usually offers the better cost per kilometre; for one running heavy timber or bulk near the GCW ceiling, the KX holds the advantage.

Residual value

Residual value after five years tracks condition and support more than badge. Both trucks retain roughly 25 to 35 percent of acquisition value at 750,000 to 900,000 km when maintained on schedule with records, because both remain serviceable platforms with active parts supply. The X9s's lower acquisition cost gives it a marginally better percentage residual in most markets, which is a quiet advantage for fleets that finance or rotate stock.

Conclusion

The X9s and the Dongfeng KX are both defensible heavy-tractor choices for export fleets, and the honest answer to which is better is that it depends on load, corridor and country. The X9s WP10H 400 to 540 hp wins on acquisition price, front-axle weight and cost per kilometre at 40 to 49 t, which is the load band most African and CIS fleets actually run. The Dongfeng KX ISZ13 480 to 560 hp wins on absolute reserve at the 50 to 55 t ceiling and on brand-tier preference where Dongfeng has a strong local distributor. On fuel the two are within one to two litres per 100 km of each other except at the extremes of the load range, where each leads in its own band.

For the typical export buyer moving 40 to 50 tonne combinations on hot, mixed-quality corridors, the SAGMOTO X9s is the stronger commercial case: lower landed cost, a lighter and responsive driveline, and parts overlap with the widest-supported Chinese heavy-truck components in Africa and the CIS. Buyers running at the very top of the GCW range, or operating where Dongfeng support is locally dominant, should weigh the KX's reserve against its premium. Either way, the decision should be fixed by a route-specific five-year cost model with contract-stated warranty and parts terms, not by a headline horsepower figure.

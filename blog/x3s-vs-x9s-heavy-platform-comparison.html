SAGMOTO X3s vs X9s: Choosing a Heavy Truck Platform

2026-11-23Comparison Guide9 min read

Two heavy platforms can look interchangeable on a price list and behave completely differently on a job site. The SAGMOTO X3s is the versatile construction machine, offered as a 6x4 or 8x4 with a doubled main frame and a Cummins ISME in the 345 to 420 HP band, or a Weichai WP10 or WP12 alternative. The SAGMOTO X9s is the high-output tractor, a 6x4 with a Cummins or Weichai engine rated 460 to 560 HP and a torque figure up to 2,550 N m. Choosing between them is not a question of which is better built; it is a question of whether the work is ground-moving with a body or weight-moving with a trailer.

The specification differences that matter

Both trucks use Fast gearboxes, but the versions differ in capacity and in how they deliver torque. The X3s runs a 12JSD200T 12-speed manual or AMT with 1,800 to 2,100 N m behind it, which suits an 8x4 tipper at about 31 t GVW. The X9s uses a 12 or 16-speed AMT matched to 2,200 to 2,550 N m and is rated for 40 to 60 t GCW in tractor form. That separation of purpose shows up in the frame and the axle layout as well as the engine. The main side-by-side figures are set out below.

Key point: The X3s is built around an 8x4 tipper frame and a 12-speed driveline; the X9s is a 6x4 tractor with 460 to 560 HP and up to 2,550 N m. Body work and trailer work are different jobs, and the platforms reflect that.

Frame strength and body mounting

An 8x4 tipper loads its frame in a way a tractor never does. Rock is dropped into the body from height, the rear bogie takes a shock load, and the chassis twists every time the truck crosses a diagonal ramp on an uneven site. The X3s answers that with a 300 x 80 x (8+5) mm frame whose doubled main rail resists twisting around the spring brackets, and with a 940 mm sleeper that is genuinely useful when a driver waits between loader cycles. Body builders can work with a known frame section and standard crossmember spacing, which shortens lead time and reduces the amount of custom reinforcement required.

For operators ordering tippers, the SAGMOTO dump truck models 6x4 8x4 range illustrates how body length and floor thickness are matched to the chassis rather than simply bolted on. A 20 to 24 m3 body on an 8x4 X3s needs a 5 to 6 mm wear plate on the floor and a reinforced headboard, especially when the load is dense angular rock. The 8x4 layout spreads that mass over two drive axles, but it also adds roughly a tonne or more of empty weight compared with a 6x4, so the extra capacity only pays where the gross limit allows it.

Tractor work and the higher power band

Weight-moving is a different calculation. A 60 t GCW tractor spends much of its shift at steady speed, where aerodynamic drag and rolling resistance dominate and a larger engine simply keeps the truck in its top ratios longer. The X9s with 460 to 560 HP and a 16-speed AMT pulls a heavy trailer away from a standstill on a grade without the constant kickdown that a smaller tractor would need. Where the job is prime mover work, the SAGMOTO tractor trucks prime mover line covers the 6x4 layouts that suit this duty, with the X9s at the powerful end of the range.

The 6x4 configuration also matters for tyre and chassis life. A tractor carries a fifth wheel load rather than a body that dumps its contents, so the frame is designed for a concentrated coupling point and a predictable torsional load. Using an X3s 6x4 as a heavy tractor is possible within its GCW range, but the engine will work harder for the same trailer and the driver will use more gear changes. Conversely, putting a tipper body on an X9s wastes the power band on a cycle where most of the time is spent stationary under a loader.

Gearbox behaviour and driver skill

The X3s 12JSD200T is available as a manual or an AMT, which lets a fleet choose according to the people it employs. On a quarry cycle with the same route repeated all day, a skilled driver with a manual box can be very efficient, and the simpler hardware is easier to repair in a remote workshop. On a mixed highway and site route, the AMT removes the variation between drivers and protects the clutch from repeated poor launches at 31 t. The 16-speed AMT in the X9s is aimed at a different problem: keeping a 460 to 560 HP engine in its economic band while gross weight and road speed vary over a long haul.

Driver training should follow the choice. A tipper driver needs to master engine braking on a loaded descent and the use of differential locks on a wet haul road. A tractor driver needs to understand predictive shifting, cruise control and the correct ratio for a sustained grade. Buying the more sophisticated gearbox without training the operators tends to produce worse fuel figures than the simpler truck, because the software is overridden or left in the wrong mode.

The comparison looks lopsided until the nature of the work is included. A tractor on a paved trunk route moves a large mass at steady speed with low rolling resistance, so its cost per tonne is naturally lower. The tipper spends its life stopping, starting, climbing a ramp and tipping, all of which consume fuel without covering distance. The right question is not which platform has the lower number but which one can perform the required job at all. An X9s cannot tip 24 m3 of rock, and an X3s 8x4 will never be an economical 60 t highway tractor.

Key point: Cost per tonne favours the X9s on a long paved haul and the X3s on a short quarry loop, because duty cycle drives the result more than engine size. Compare trucks only on cycles they can both actually run.

Cab comfort, service and resale

The X3s 940 mm sleeper is a real advantage on a mine or quarry site where a driver may wait through several loader cycles, and the truck's widespread use means workshops in many markets already understand its driveline. The X9s is specified for longer spells at the wheel, with the AMT and higher power reducing the effort of a full day on a trunk route. Both benefit from a regional parts plan: filters, belts, a water pump and brake components should travel with the first order, and a clutch or clutch kit for the manual X3s is worth stocking where the truck works far from a supplier.

Resale tends to follow the original duty. A clean 8x4 X3s with a documented weighbridge record and a body in good condition is easy to sell into another quarry or construction fleet. A high-mileage X9s tractor with a full service history remains attractive to a long-haul operator, whereas one that has been used on a rough site and has a tired suspension will be heavily discounted. Keeping the specification regional and the maintenance record complete protects the value of either platform.

How to decide

Write down three things before requesting a quotation: the maximum load that must be carried, the gradient and surface of the route, and whether the load is discharged through a body or a trailer. If the answer involves a tipper body, an 8x4 frame and 12-speed driveline, the X3s is the correct starting point. If it involves a fifth wheel, 40 to 60 t GCW and sustained highway speed, the X9s with its 460 to 560 HP engine and AMT is the platform to specify. Mixed fleets often need both, and buying the wrong one for either job is expensive in fuel and in missed cycle time.

A third factor is the service environment. A quarry with its own workshop can keep a manual X3s running with a modest parts stock, while a long-haul operator covering remote highways should favour the X9s AMT and a supplier with a mapped regional service network. The cost of a truck standing idle for a missing component can exceed the difference in purchase price within a single quarter, so check the support arrangement as carefully as the specification sheet.

SAGMOTO X6s Tyre Cost per Hour: A Working Management Method

2026-11-18Maintenance & After-sales10 min read

After fuel, tyres are the largest controllable cost on a tipper, and the only one that owners routinely manage by anecdote. Ask a fleet manager what a tyre costs and he will quote a purchase price. Ask what a tyre costs per hour of work and the answer is usually a guess. That gap matters, because the purchase price tells you almost nothing about the real cost: a cheap tyre that lasts 1,400 hours can cost more per hour than an expensive one that lasts 2,400, and the difference compounds across ten wheels and twenty trucks.

The SAGMOTO X6s, working at 25 to 31 tonnes GVW with the Weichai WP10.340E50 and a Fast 10-speed, is a hard environment for tyres. Loaded ramp starts, tight site manoeuvring, abrasive haul roads and heat all attack the casing. This article sets out a method for measuring tyre cost per hour properly, then shows the four variables that move it most and how to act on each one without buying a single extra tyre.

Key point: Tyre cost per hour equals purchase price divided by hours to scrap, multiplied by the number of tyres on the truck. On a 10-wheel X6s running 12.00R20 at USD 380 and 2,000 hours, that is about USD 1.90 per truck hour. Every 200 hours of extra life removes roughly USD 0.17 per hour, which is worth more than most purchase negotiations.

Why the per-hour number changes decisions

Purchase price drives behaviour in the wrong direction. A buyer comparing two tyres at USD 340 and USD 400 will usually take the cheaper one, because the difference looks like a saving on the invoice. In service, if the cheaper tyre reaches 1,600 hours and the dearer one 2,300, the per-hour cost is USD 0.213 against USD 0.174 — the cheaper tyre is 22 percent more expensive to run, on ten wheels, for years.

The per-hour number also makes the harder decisions visible. It shows whether retreading is worth it, whether a casing is worth repairing, whether a wheel position is being abused, and whether the alignment is costing more than the alignment service. None of those questions can be answered from a purchase price. All of them are answerable from a simple log of fitment hours and scrap hours per tyre serial.

Building the measurement

The measurement needs four fields per tyre: serial number, wheel position, hours at fitment, hours at removal, and reason for removal. A clipboard and a paint pen will do; a spreadsheet makes the analysis easier but is not essential. The discipline is recording hours from the hour meter rather than from the calendar, because a truck that idles for three days is not wearing its tyres at the same rate as one that is working.

The ranges are wide because the same tyre can deliver double the hours in a well-managed fleet. That spread — not the choice between brands — is where the money is. A fleet at USD 2.40 per truck hour and a fleet at USD 1.55 are running the same truck on the same site; the difference is inflation discipline, alignment, load bias and rotation, in that order of impact.

Inflation: the cheapest gain available

Under-inflation by 20 percent typically removes 25 to 30 percent of tyre life, because the casing flexes more, heats more and the shoulder wears faster. Over-inflation by the same margin removes 10 to 15 percent through centre tread wear and impact damage. Both are invisible at a glance and both are correctable in an hour with a gauge.

The practical regime is a weekly pressure check with a calibrated gauge, done cold, at a fixed time, recorded on the same sheet as the hours. Target pressures for a loaded X6s tipper on 12.00R20 are typically 830 to 900 kPa front and 790 to 860 kPa rear depending on the load and the tyre rating, and the correct figure should come from the tyre manufacturer's load table for your actual axle loads, not from a general rule. A fleet that checks weekly and acts on the numbers will usually find 1.5 to 3 tonnes of annual tyre cost per twenty trucks without changing supplier.

Key point: Weekly cold pressure checks, recorded, are worth more than any brand upgrade. A 20 percent under-inflation error costs 25 to 30 percent of tyre life, and it is present in most fleets that do not measure.

Alignment and the scrub you cannot see

Misalignment on a tipper usually appears as feathered tread across the front axle and as uneven wear on the drive tyres after a frame or spring repair. A toe error of 5 mm can scrub a front tyre out in 60 percent of its expected life, and the fuel penalty of the resulting drag is roughly 2 to 3 percent. Both are removed by a proper alignment check, which should be done at every tyre replacement on the front axle and after any suspension work.

Thrust angle matters as much as toe on a 6x4. If the rear axles are not square to the chassis centreline, the truck crabbles and every tyre fights the direction of travel. That shows up as rapid wear on one edge of the drive tyres and as a steering wheel that is off-centre on a straight road. Checking thrust angle is part of the same alignment service and is often skipped by workshops that only check front toe.

Load bias and the tandem penalty

On a 6x4 tipper the rear tandem carries most of the load, and any side-to-side or front-to-rear bias multiplies wear on the overloaded positions. A body loaded 300 mm to one side puts several hundred kilograms extra on two tyres, which run hotter and wear faster, and the effect compounds over a shift. Loader discipline and a level load are tyre management, even though they feel like production management.

Axle spread and tyre matching also matter. Fitting a new tyre next to a worn one on a tandem forces the new tyre to carry more than its share because the effectively larger diameter takes the load, and it wears quickly as a result. The standard practice of fitting new tyres to the front axle and moving the best part-worn casings to the drive positions keeps diameters closer within each pair and protects the newest rubber.

Reading the scrap pile

Every scrap tyre is a diagnosis. The wear pattern tells you which system failed, and the pattern is consistent enough that a scrap analysis of twenty tyres will usually identify the fleet's dominant problem.

Two or three of these will dominate. Fixing the dominant one usually recovers 10 to 20 percent of tyre life across the fleet, which is a much larger gain than shopping for a different brand. The analysis costs nothing beyond the discipline of cutting a section out of each scrap tyre and labelling it.

Casings, retreads and the end of life

A premium casing that is removed at 3 mm tread is not scrap; it is a retread candidate. Retreading a sound 12.00R20 casing for USD 120 to 180 and running it for another 900 to 1,400 hours in a low-speed position is usually the cheapest tyre hour available to a quarry fleet. The condition is that the casing has no sidewall damage, no run-flat history and no repairs in the shoulder — which is exactly what the per-tyre log tells you.

The reverse discipline matters too: do not retread a casing that has been run flat or overheated, because it will fail in service and take a wheel end with it. Setting a simple rule — no retread on a casing with any sidewall repair or run-flat note — prevents most of the failures that give retreads a bad reputation. Conclusion

Tyre cost per hour is a measurement problem before it is a purchasing problem. Log the hours, divide by the price, and the fleet's real tyre cost becomes visible — typically USD 1.50 to USD 2.40 per truck hour on an X6s tipper, with a spread that is explained by discipline rather than by brand. Attack it in order: weekly cold pressures, alignment and thrust angle, load bias and matching, then scrap analysis and casing policy. A fleet that does all four will find 20 to 30 percent of its tyre spend without changing a single supplier or specification.

i9 Electric Truck Depot Charging Buying Guide for Fleets

2026-11-16Buying Guide10 min read

A fleet of twenty electric trucks does not fail because the trucks are wrong. It fails because the depot was designed for twenty diesel vehicles and someone bolted a few chargers onto the yard. The SAGMOTO i9 is a 7.5 tonne class battery-electric truck with a 120 to 160 kW motor, a 100 to 160 kWh LFP pack and 120 kW DC fast charging, which makes it well suited to urban distribution and regional delivery. But a truck that can accept 120 kW is only as useful as the electrical supply behind it. This guide takes a 10 to 30 unit depot on a weak grid and walks through the decisions that keep every vehicle charged, every night, without a transformer fire or an electricity bill that destroys the business case.

Begin with routes, then size the electrical supply

Charging infrastructure is downstream of route reality. Before you order a transformer, list each route's daily distance, load factor, road gradient and return time. The i9's 100 kWh pack suits a 120 to 150 km urban round trip with a full day of stop-start delivery and a comfortable reserve; the 160 kWh pack is the better choice for 180 to 220 km regional runs or routes with a loaded climb. Multiply the fleet's total daily kilometres by the real consumption figure, not the laboratory one. A fully laden i9 on a mixed urban and highway cycle will draw roughly 55 to 75 kWh per 100 km once auxiliary loads and air conditioning are included, compared with 22 to 28 litres of diesel per 100 km for an equivalent 7.5 tonne diesel truck. That consumption figure is the foundation of every cable, breaker and transformer calculation that follows.

Weak grids are the norm at depots on emerging-market industrial estates. A supply rated for lighting and air compressors cannot absorb twenty trucks pulling 120 kW each at 6 pm. The good news is that electric fleets charge when the depot is quiet, so a managed overnight programme fits a modest connection if charging is staged rather than simultaneous. The goal is to spread load across the available hours and cap the peak with software, rather than paying to upgrade a substation you will stress for three hours a night.

Transformer sizing with a managed charging profile

The instinct is to size the transformer for every charger at full output, which for twenty 120 kW units means 2.4 MW and a connection fee that kills the project. A managed approach charging the same twenty trucks across an eight-hour night window at an average of 30 to 45 kW per truck needs roughly 600 to 900 kW of connected load, plus allowance for building demand. Install a transformer with a continuous rating around 1,000 kVA for a fleet of this size and a load-management controller that caps simultaneous demand. Charger mix and the case for a buffer battery

Not every charging point needs to be a 120 kW DC unit. A practical depot uses a hybrid: 120 kW DC cabinets for the trucks that must turn around fast, and 20 to 30 kW AC or low-power DC points for vehicles that park all night. The i9's 120 kW DC capability is most valuable when a truck returns mid-shift with 20 percent state of charge and must be back on the road in 45 minutes. For the rest of the fleet, slow overnight charging is gentler on the LFP cells, cheaper on demand charges and easier on the grid. Assign the fast cabinets to the highest-utilisation vehicles and let the rest trickle into the small hours.

A buffer battery smooths the remaining peaks. A 500 kWh to 1 MWh stationary LFP bank charged slowly during the day can discharge at 300 to 500 kW during the evening rush, shaving the demand spike that attracts the highest tariff and the biggest connection charge. On a weak grid the buffer also protects the trucks: if the supply dips when a cabinet is drawing full power, the buffer carries the transient instead of tripping the charger. The economics depend on the local tariff structure, but where demand charges are high payback is often inside three to four years. Size the buffer for one peak event, not for a full night of fleet charging, unless the utility explicitly rewards it.

Backup power and night-shift charging windows

For a fleet that cannot miss a delivery window, a diesel generator is cheap insurance. It need not run the whole depot, only the critical chargers at reduced output during an outage. Size a genset for 150 to 250 kW, enough to trickle-charge the vehicles committed to early routes, and wire it to a defined emergency bus. It runs perhaps 30 to 60 hours a year, so fuel cost is minor beside a frozen delivery schedule. A generator feeding a charger needs stable frequency and a clean waveform; specify an alternator and charger input that tolerate it without nuisance trips.

Charging windows should follow the tariff and the duty cycle, not the clock. Where a utility offers a cheap overnight band from 10 pm to 6 am, program the majority of charging into that window and use the buffer for anything that must happen earlier. Stagger start times across the fleet in 20 minute steps so that twenty trucks do not all begin a high-power phase simultaneously. Trial the schedule for two weeks with real telematics data, then adjust. A depot that charges on a smart schedule typically uses 30 to 40 percent less peak demand than one where every driver plugs in at the same moment on returning to base.

Route feasibility for a 7.5 tonne electric truck

The i9 is a strong fit for urban and peri-urban distribution, but a buyer must be honest about range. With a 160 kWh pack and a real consumption of 65 kWh per 100 km, a loaded truck has roughly 220 km of practical range before the reserve, and cold weather or heavy air-conditioning use can cut that by 15 to 20 percent. Map every route against the worst-case figure, not the best, and keep a 20 percent state-of-charge reserve for a missed delivery or a diversion. Routes that return to depot midday are ideal because they allow an opportunistic top-up. A single 280 km out-and-back run with no charging at the destination is not a job for the standard i9 pack; it needs a different vehicle or a charging stop built into the schedule.

Payload is another feasibility check. On a 7.5 tonne gross vehicle weight chassis, LFP battery mass comes straight off the load. Compare the i9's usable payload with the diesel truck it replaces. Dense building materials at the legal limit may force an extra daily trip, eroding the cost case; light, bulky FMCG or parcels make the payload penalty irrelevant and turn quiet, zero-emission running into a real advantage at night deliveries and in low-emission zones.

Five-year total cost against diesel

The business case for the i9 rests on energy and maintenance, not on purchase price. An electric drivetrain has fewer moving parts, no oil changes, no exhaust aftertreatment and far less brake wear thanks to regenerative braking. Against a 7.5 tonne diesel truck covering 60,000 km a year, a well-managed i9 depot usually reaches cost parity at 45,000 to 70,000 km per vehicle per year, depending on local electricity and diesel prices. Below that annual distance the capital cost of chargers and transformer is spread too thinly; above it the savings compound quickly. Break-even rule: electricity at USD 0.10 per kWh and diesel at USD 1.10 per litre makes the i9 roughly USD 0.18 to 0.24 per km cheaper to run. At 60,000 km a year that is USD 11,000 to 14,000 saved per truck, enough to recover the charger premium in three to four years.

The numbers also depend on battery health. LFP chemistry tolerates daily cycling well, but keeping state of charge between 10 and 90 percent and avoiding sustained full charge in heat will protect the pack and its resale value. A depot that manages state of charge, tariffs and maintenance as one system will get a decade of service from the vehicles. Depot commissioning checklist

Bring the electrical engineer, charger supplier and fleet manager together before energising anything. Confirm the transformer rating, the diversity assumptions in the load controller, the generator bus and per-charger metering. Test that two cabinets run at full output without tripping the incoming breaker, then simulate a grid dip to confirm the buffer carries it. Train drivers to report state-of-charge faults early and plug in at the assigned point. Commissioned this way, a ten to thirty unit i9 fleet charges reliably on a grid that looked far too weak on paper.

SAGMOTO X9s 16-Speed AMT: Gear Ratios and Cruise Economics

2026-11-20Product Deep Dive9 min read

Gearbox ratios determine how a tractor spends its working life, yet they are often the last thing a buyer examines. The SAGMOTO X9s is a heavy 6x4 tractor in the 40 to 60 tonne GCW class, powered by Cummins or Weichai engines in the 460 to 560 horsepower band delivering 2,200 to 2,550 Nm, and it is available with a Fast 16-speed AMT. The same engine can feel economical in one truck and thirsty in another purely because the ratios place it at a different engine speed for the same road speed. Understanding the ratio set is therefore a commercial question as much as a technical one, especially on long-haul routes where fuel is the largest single operating cost.

A 16-speed unit gives the driver sixteen forward ratios and typically two or four reverse ratios. It is built from an eight-speed main gearbox with a two-range splitter section, or from a four-speed main box with a four-range auxiliary; either arrangement produces closely spaced steps that let the engine stay near its fuel-efficient speed band across a wide range of road speeds. The automated manual transmission shifts without a clutch pedal, so the ratios can be used consistently rather than skipped by a tired driver. For fleets running a SAGMOTO tractor trucks prime mover over long distances, that consistency is where much of the fuel saving comes from.

Key point: On a 16-speed AMT, judge the top gear and the final drive together. A tall overdrive with a numerically low axle ratio gives low cruise rpm but can force repeated downshifts on rolling terrain, erasing the fuel gain.

How a 16-speed AMT is arranged

Most heavy 16-speed units in this class use a main gearbox with eight forward speeds plus a splitter that divides each main ratio into a low and a high range. The result is a sequence in which consecutive steps are small, typically around 12 to 18 percent rather than the 30 to 40 percent jumps of an older 9 or 10-speed box. Small steps matter because an engine has a narrow band where brake-specific fuel consumption is lowest, often between 1,200 and 1,600 rpm under load. With closely spaced ratios, the AMT can hold the engine in that band as road speed changes, whereas a wide-step gearbox drops the driver out of it after every shift. The crawler ratio, formed by the lowest gear and the lowest splitter range, is usually around 12 to 14 to one, which gives a loaded 60 tonne combination the ability to move away on a steep grade without slipping the clutch.

The most useful number in that table is the step column. A 13 to 16 percent step means that after an upshift the engine drops by roughly one sixth of its speed; if the shift point was 1,700 rpm, the engine lands near 1,450 rpm, close to the efficient band. A wide-step gearbox landing at 1,150 rpm would lug the engine and force a downshift on the next rise. This is why a 16-speed can return better fuel economy than a 12-speed with the same engine and final drive, even though the mechanical efficiency of the extra gears is marginally lower. The automated shift also removes the variation between drivers, so the theoretical advantage appears in real fleet data rather than only on a test bench.

Cruise rpm at 80 km/h

Engine speed at a given road speed is set by three numbers: road speed, tyre rolling radius and the product of gearbox ratio and final drive ratio. At 80 km/h a truck is travelling 22.2 metres per second. With a 12R22.5 tyre of roughly 0.50 metre loaded rolling radius, the wheel turns about 424 times per minute. Multiply by the final drive ratio and the top gear ratio to get engine rpm. With a direct top gear of 1.00 and a 3.70 axle ratio, the engine turns near 1,570 rpm. With a 3.08 ratio it turns near 1,310 rpm. An overdrive top gear of 0.81 lowers those figures to roughly 1,270 and 1,060 rpm respectively.

For a 560 horsepower X9s at 49 tonnes on flat highway, the 3.70 direct combination near 1,570 rpm sits close to the engine's efficient band and leaves reserve torque for a gentle grade without an immediate downshift. The 3.08 overdrive combination looks attractive on paper because it turns barely 1,060 rpm, but on anything but level ground the AMT will hunt between gears and the average speed falls. Conversely, a 4.11 axle is the right choice for a route with long climbs or frequent stops, accepting higher cruise rpm in exchange for fewer downshifts and better startability. The correct answer follows the route profile, not the lowest possible rpm.

Key point: For a 560 horsepower tractor at 49 tonnes, a 3.70 direct axle near 1,570 rpm at 80 km/h balances efficiency and reserve. Chasing 1,000 rpm with an overdrive invites constant downshifts.

Gradeability and the low end of the ratio set

Gradeability is where the crawler ratio earns its keep. With 2,550 Nm at the engine, a first-gear ratio near 13.8 and a 4.11 final drive, the torque multiplying at the drive axle before efficiency losses is enormous; even allowing for driveline losses, a 49 tonne combination can pull away on a grade that would stall a taller specification. The 16-speed layout makes that usable rather than theoretical, because the splitter lets the driver or the AMT step up in fine increments as traction returns. On a mine access road or a port ramp, the difference between a 12 to 14 to one crawler and a 9 to one crawler is the difference between moving away cleanly and heating the clutch or the torque converter. Buyers who specify a tractor for mixed highway and site work should check the crawler figure on the build sheet, not just the top speed.

Fuel economics and how the AMT protects them

Fuel economy in a heavy tractor is rarely decided by the engine alone. It is decided by how often the truck runs below its efficient speed, how many shifts occur per hundred kilometres and how much time is spent at high engine speed on gentle gradients. A well-matched 16-speed AMT with a 3.70 final drive on a flat long-haul route will typically hold the engine between 1,300 and 1,600 rpm for most of the shift, which is where a modern 13 litre engine returns its best consumption. Specifying a taller axle to chase 1,000 rpm on level road usually backfires once the route includes a two percent rise, because each downshift costs fuel and time. The discipline is to model the whole route, count the expected shifts and choose the axle that minimises them while keeping cruise rpm in the efficient band.

Why tyre rolling radius belongs in the calculation

The cruise rpm figures above assume a loaded rolling radius near 0.50 metre, typical of a 12R22.5 tyre. Change the tyre and every number moves. A 315/80R22.5 or a 12R22.5 of taller overall diameter increases the rolling radius, which lowers engine rpm at the same road speed and effectively makes the final drive taller. A difference of 20 millimetres in rolling radius shifts engine speed by roughly four percent, enough to move a 1,570 rpm cruise toward 1,510 or 1,630. Dual tyres worn to different diameters on the same drive axle create a second problem: the differentials must absorb the speed difference continuously, generating heat and wearing the differential. Fleets should specify one tyre size and one pattern across the tractor fleet, record the loaded radius from the supplier, and use that figure whenever cruise speed is recalculated after a tyre change.

AMT calibration and driver technique

An automated manual transmission only delivers its theoretical fuel benefit if the calibration matches the load. A tractor that runs empty on the return leg should not use the same shift map as one pulling 60 tonnes uphill, because the optimum shift point moves with gross weight. Many units offer a load mode or an economy mode, and fleets should check which map the supplier has set at delivery rather than accepting the default. Driver technique still matters: aggressive acceleration encourages early upshifts and keeps a torque converter unlocked longer, while a steady pull lets the AMT lock up and stay there. Teach drivers to read the rpm display and to avoid manual intervention on gentle grades, where the automated shifts are usually better judged than a fleeting human decision. A few hours of route-specific training often recovers more fuel than a change of hardware.

Selecting the final drive before you order

Our practical guidance is straightforward. For predominantly flat highway work at 40 to 49 tonnes, specify a 3.70 axle with a direct top gear and confirm the engine sits near 1,500 rpm at the operator's normal cruise speed. For routes with sustained grades, frequent stops or 55 to 60 tonne gross weights, move to a 4.11 axle and accept roughly 1,700 rpm at 80 km/h in exchange for startability and fewer downshifts. Reserve overdrive top gears for operators who genuinely cruise below 70 km/h and have verified the terrain. In every case, ask the supplier for the exact gearbox ratio sheet and the tyre rolling radius used in the calculation, because a change from a 12R22.5 to a different tyre shifts engine rpm by several percent. Ratios are a system, and the order should specify all three elements together.

The X9s gives a buyer an unusually flexible ratio tool in the form of a 16-speed AMT, but flexibility only pays when the final drive and tyre are chosen to suit the route. Work the cruise rpm, check the crawler ratio, model the grades and the answer becomes clear on paper before the truck is built. That is how a 560 horsepower tractor turns its power into tonne-kilometres rather than into heat and extra fuel stops.

SAGMOTO X6 vs X6s: Which Medium-Duty Platform Fits Your Fleet?

2026-11-24Comparison Guide9 min read

Choosing between the SAGMOTO X6 and X6s is less about which truck is better and more about which duty cycle the fleet actually runs. The X6 is a medium cargo platform with a Cummins ISD 210 HP engine, 800 to 950 N m of torque and a Fast 8-speed gearbox, offered as 4x2, 6x4 and 4x4. The X6s is the construction-oriented sibling with 240 to 300 HP, 950 to 1,200 N m and 6x4, 8x4 or 4x4 configurations. This comparison sets out a decision framework across duty cycle, terrain, load, cost per kilometre, resale and service network.

Duty cycle and terrain

Start with the work. A distribution fleet running sealed roads between a depot and retail stops values payload, fuel economy and manoeuvrability, which favours the 4x2 X6 at 9 to 11.5 t payload. A site supplying aggregate or operating from a quarry faces ramps, loose surfaces and frequent stops, where the higher torque and available four-wheel-drive X6s earn their keep through better traction and stronger low-speed pull.

Terrain also dictates tyre specification and service frequency. Rough sites cut tread life and accelerate suspension wear, so the X6s is usually paired with heavier duty tyres and a shorter inspection interval. A fleet that misjudges this mix will pay for it in downtime rather than in fuel, because the truck that looks cheaper on paper spends more weeks in the workshop.

Load, body and GVW

Payload differs sharply by axle configuration. The 4x2 X6 carries 9 to 11.5 t while the 6x4 version reaches 14.5 to 16.5 t, with GCW spanning 12 to 60 t across the range. The X6s is more often specified as a tipper, where body mass and unloading geometry matter as much as rated load. Compare the two on the body you will actually fit, not on a bare-chassis figure that ignores several tonnes of steel.

Volume is another constraint. A light bulky cargo such as packaging fills a box long before the X6 reaches its weight limit, while dense aggregate reaches the limit early. The right choice depends on whether your freight is weight-limited or volume-limited, and that assessment should include the tare of the proposed body plus any equipment such as a tail lift or refrigerated unit.

Key point: The X6 suits weight-sensitive distribution cargo with its 9 to 11.5 t 4x2 payload, while the X6s earns its premium when the route includes steep ramps and loose surfaces that exploit its 1,200 N m ceiling.

Cost per kilometre

Cost per kilometre combines fuel, tyres, maintenance and depreciation. On a sealed-road cycle the 210 HP X6 typically records lower fuel burn because of its smaller engine and lower mass. Take a simplified case of 100,000 km a year at 28 L per 100 km for the X6 against 32 L for a 6x4 X6s, with diesel at a constant price. The X6 saves roughly 4,000 L a year, which is a meaningful gap before tyres are even considered.

On a site cycle the comparison reverses in part, because the X6s completes more work per hour and can replace two lighter trips. Cost per tonne-kilometre, not cost per kilometre alone, is the fair metric when the truck spends its life on short, heavily loaded cycles. Build the model with your own fuel price, tyre cost and utilization rather than a regional average that may not reflect your operation.

Key point: Judge the two platforms on cost per tonne-kilometre rather than fuel alone, because a site-bound X6s can replace two lighter trips and change the entire utilisation equation.

Resale and service network

Resale value follows the breadth of the buyer pool. The X6, as a general cargo truck, tends to attract a wider set of second owners than a specialised tipper, which can soften depreciation. The X6s holds value well in construction markets where four-wheel-drive and 8x4 units are scarce, but a buyer should confirm local demand before paying a premium for a configuration that few competitors want to purchase later.

Service network is the quieter decision factor. Both ranges share common Fast gearbox and Cummins or Weichai service items, but the X6s axle and transfer components are heavier and may not be stocked at every rural dealer. Verify parts availability for the exact variant, and ask for the recommended spare-parts kit with the quotation so that a minor fault does not idle the truck for a week.

Cab specification and driver retention

The cab is a practical differentiator that comparisons often omit. Distribution work with many stops rewards a low step-in height and good visibility, areas where the X6 cab is well suited to frequent boarding. Site work with long loading waits favours a robust cab with straightforward controls and ample space for wet weather gear, which is where drivers appreciate the X6s specification.

Driver retention has a cost line of its own. A truck that is uncomfortable or difficult to enter several dozen times a shift contributes to turnover, and recruiting replacements is expensive in remote markets. Include a short driver trial in the selection process and record feedback on seat, visibility and step height before finalizing a large order.

Special bodies and power take-off

Body builders often ask which of the two chassis accepts a power take-off more readily. Both the X6 and X6s can be specified with a PTO driven from the gearbox, but the X6s with its 9 or 10-speed Fast box and higher torque rating generally has more capacity for a hydraulic pump on a tipper or a compressor on a service body. Confirm the PTO torque rating and the available flange positions before the gearbox is ordered, because a late change means a different unit entirely.

The electrical architecture matters too. A refrigerated body or a hydraulic tail lift draws significant current, and the alternator and harness on the base X6 may need upgrading. On the X6s, site-oriented builds often include reinforced wiring looms as standard, which reduces retrofit cost. Discuss total electrical load with the body builder early, since an upgrade after the chassis enters production is far more expensive than specifying it at the outset.

Maintenance intervals and parts cost

Service cost diverges once the trucks are a few years old. The X6 uses a lighter axle set and a simpler driveline, so routine items such as brake linings, wheel bearings and propshaft joints are cheaper and quicker to replace. The X6s, particularly in 8x4 and four-wheel-drive form, carries more joints and a heavier axle, which raises both the parts bill and the labour time per service visit.

The offset is durability under load. A truck that works a site puts its components under higher cyclic stress, and the heavier X6s parts often last longer in that environment than lighter equivalents would. Compare scheduled service cost over a full year rather than per visit, and include the unscheduled downtime that a lighter specification might incur on rough ground.

Warranty and resale channels

Warranty terms should be read alongside the intended duty cycle. A site machine that runs at high gross weight on rough ground may fall outside the assumptions behind a standard warranty if the operating conditions are not disclosed. Ask for the warranty to be stated in writing with the duty cycle described, and clarify which components are covered when the truck is used primarily off highway.

Resale channels differ as well. The X6 sells readily through general commercial vehicle dealers because its cargo body has many uses. The X6s often finds a buyer through construction and plant channels where a tipper or four-wheel-drive unit is actively sought. Knowing which channel will absorb the truck at the end of the holding period is part of the purchase decision, especially in markets where auctions are thin and buyers are scarce.

Financing and total cost of ownership

Financing terms can tilt the decision. A higher specification X6s carries a larger upfront cost, which is easier to justify when the truck generates more tonne-kilometres per shift. Ask the lender how residual value is treated, because a model with a strong secondary market lowers monthly payments even if the purchase price is higher. Compare quotes on total cost of ownership over the intended holding period rather than on the sticker price alone.

Timing also plays a part. A fleet expanding a construction contract can justify the X6s on near-term utilisation, while one entering a stable distribution market may prefer the lower commitment of the X6. The right answer changes as the contract book changes, so revisit the calculation whenever the route mix shifts materially.

X3s Bulk Cement Tanker and Fly Ash Transport for Concrete Plants

2026-11-09Industry Application8 min read

Every concrete plant sits at the end of a cement chain that starts at a grinding mill or a distribution terminal and ends at a batching hopper. The link in the middle is one of the most specialised vehicles in road freight: the pneumatic bulk tanker, which carries 25 to 35 tonnes of powder in a sealed pressure vessel and discharges it into a silo using nothing but compressed air. The SAGMOTO X3s, with 385 to 420 hp from a Cummins ISME 420 or Weichai WP10 to WP12, is a common chassis for this work in export markets, and the specification decisions that matter are mostly about the blower and the tank, not about the engine.

What a cement bulker really does

A pneumatic tanker looks like a simple cylinder on wheels. Inside it is anything but simple. The shell is divided into compartments along its length, and each compartment has an aeration pad or a fluidising membrane at the bottom. When the compressor runs, air is forced up through the membrane, which turns the settled powder into a fluid that behaves almost like a liquid and flows down a sloped floor to a discharge pipe at the rear.

The truck has three operating modes. On the road it is heavy freight at 38 to 45 tonnes gross for 200 to 600 km. At the terminal it is a pressure vessel filling from a silo at 1 to 3 bar. At the plant it is a stationary compressor, lifting cement into a silo 20 to 30 metres high for 40 to 80 minutes.

Why the X3s is a common bulker chassis

Bulk cement work is a heavy combination operation with a large parasitic load. The X3s at 385 to 420 hp with a Fast 12-speed gearbox and 13 tonne rear axles is rated for the gross combination weights a 30 cubic metre tanker needs, typically 38 to 45 tonnes. The 12-speed box gives a deep low range for grade starts at 45 tonnes and a tall top gear for the highway leg, which is the combination you want for a vehicle that must do both.

The real advantage is the mounting envelope. A pneumatic tanker needs a long, flat, unobstructed chassis top with a straight frame and no obstructions along the length. The X3s frame is a straightforward ladder design with a flat top flange, which body builders like because it keeps the tank saddles simple and the mounting height low. A lower tank keeps the centre of gravity down, which matters at 45 tonnes on a highway with a full 30 cubic metre load.

Operators who need a platform for shorter regional runs at lower gross weights, or who want the same chassis family for mixed freight when the tanker is off the road, can review the SAGMOTO dump truck models 6x4 8x4 range, which shares the same general driveline architecture in tipper form.

A 30 cubic metre cement tanker holds roughly 36 tonnes of cement at a bulk density of 1.2 tonnes per cubic metre. Combine that with a tanker tare of 13 to 15 tonnes and a tractor or rigid chassis, and you are at 50 tonnes gross. The chassis must be rated for the loaded mass, not the tank size.

Blower sizing and the discharge rate problem

The blower is the component that decides whether a bulker completes five loads a day or three. A truck-mounted rotary lobe blower typically delivers 8 to 14 cubic metres per minute at 0.8 to 1.2 bar. Discharge rate depends on the blower volume, the pipeline diameter, the vertical lift and the length of horizontal run.

A 12 cubic metre per minute blower discharging through a 100 mm line into a silo 25 metres high at 15 metres horizontal distance moves 25 to 30 tonnes of cement in about 45 minutes. Reduce the blower to 8 cubic metres per minute and the same load takes 70 to 80 minutes. Add a 60 metre horizontal run to the silo and the time stretches by 25 to 35 percent because pressure drop in the line rises with length. At 1.2 bar the discharge line is working close to its limit and any moisture in the cement will cause a blockage.

Blowers run from the truck engine through a PTO or from a small auxiliary diesel. Engine-driven is cheaper and lighter, but it ties the main engine to discharge for an hour at a time, adding 800 to 1,000 engine hours a year at five charges a day. An auxiliary engine decouples the two and holds blower speed regardless of gear.

Discharge time, not road speed, sets the number of loads per day. Shaving 20 minutes from each discharge cycle at five cycles a day returns 100 minutes, which is often one additional load per shift. That is worth more than a 30 hp power increase on the chassis.

Tank configuration, compartments and slope

A cement tanker shell is divided into two, three or four compartments, each with its own aeration membrane and discharge valve. More compartments let the operator discharge one grade at a time, which is useful when the plant takes ordinary Portland cement and a supplementary material on the same run. Fewer compartments mean simpler maintenance and a shorter unload time because the air volume is not split.

The floor slope inside the tank is critical. Powder will not flow without it. A typical design uses a 6 to 8 degree slope toward the discharge end, with the aeration membrane covering 60 to 70 percent of the floor area. If the slope is shallower, cement collects in the dead corner behind the last compartment and sets over a few weeks, and the operator loses 1 to 2 tonnes of capacity before anyone notices.

Tank material is usually mild steel 4 to 5 mm thick with a pressure rating of 2 to 3 bar working and a 4 to 6 bar test. Aluminium tanks save 1.5 to 2.5 tonnes of tare and are common where payload matters, but they dent easily and are hard to repair in a market without specialist welders. Stainless is used for food-grade powders and chemical fillers rather than cement.

Fly ash, the more difficult cargo

Fly ash from coal-fired power stations is lighter, at 0.9 to 1.1 tonnes per cubic metre, so a 30 cubic metre tank holds 27 to 33 tonnes. Its spherical particles are abrasive and fine, and once aeration stops the powder compacts against the walls and bridges.

Concrete plants in many markets use fly ash at 15 to 30 percent of binder content, and the delivery pattern differs from cement because power stations are often remote and the discharge silos are older. A fly ash tanker usually needs a stronger blower for the same tonnage because the lower bulk density means more volume to move per tonne, and a steeper floor slope to prevent bridging. Operators moving both materials in one truck should specify the tank for the fly ash duty and accept that cement discharge will run faster than required.

Moisture is the single largest cause of failure with both materials. A tank that has not been fully discharged and cleaned will take on condensation overnight, and a damp lining of cement sets into a hard crust that requires hammering and in some cases cutting out. Daily blow-down with dry air and a check of the membrane through the manway is the cheapest maintenance a bulker owner can buy.

Weight limits, axle configuration and the tipping alternative

The X3s is offered as a 6x4 rigid or as a tractor unit. A 6x4 rigid with a 30 cubic metre tank is the standard configuration for regional plant delivery, and an 8x4 rigid with a 35 to 40 cubic metre tank is used where gross weight limits allow. For long haul, a tractor with a semi-trailer tanker carries 35 to 45 tonnes in one movement and can split the load between two plants.

Some buyers choose a tipper instead of a tanker and tip cement into a pit or a receiving hopper. That works where the plant has a pit conveyor, where the cement is bagged or in big bags, or where fly ash is delivered to a stockpile. The advantage is a lower capital cost and a faster discharge. The disadvantage is dust emission, which many urban permits no longer allow.

Maintenance and inspection routine

A bulker is a pressure vessel and is regulated as one in most markets, with periodic inspection and a certification plate. The items that matter in day to day operation are the aeration membranes, the discharge valves, the pressure relief valve, the manway seals and the discharge hoses. Membranes are the first to fail, usually by tearing at the fixing points, and a torn membrane shows up as a slow discharge and uneven emptying across compartments.

Discharge hoses are consumables. A 100 mm fabric-reinforced hose carrying abrasive cement at 1 bar wears through the inner liner in 400 to 800 discharges, and a hose that bursts at 25 metres of lift sprays powder over a wide area. Carrying a spare hose and two clamps on the truck is standard practice.

The blower itself needs an air filter, oil level checks and a lobe clearance inspection at 2,000 hours. Blower failure is usually caused by ingesting dust through a damaged inlet filter, which scores the lobes and drops output by a third before anyone notices the noise.

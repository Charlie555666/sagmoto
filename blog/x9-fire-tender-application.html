SAGMOTO X9 4x4 Fire Tender: Chassis and Pump Drive for Rural Brigades

2026-11-23Industry Application9 min read

A rural fire brigade does not need the largest appliance on the market. It needs one that can leave the station within a minute, leave the paved road, and arrive at a farmhouse or scrub fire with enough water and pump pressure to make a first attack. The SAGMOTO X9 4x4 platform suits that role: a light special version around 140 to 160 HP with 550 to 1,200 N m, a Fast six or eight-speed gearbox, and a gross combined weight range from about 4.5 to 25 t. Choosing the right tank, pump drive and weight distribution turns the X9 into a credible first-response tender rather than a truck that simply looks the part.

How much water should a rural tender carry?

Water capacity is the first trade-off. A light 4x4 tender on the lower X9 range often carries 1,000 to 2,000 litres, enough for a first attack or for a rural crew to hold a line until a larger tanker arrives. Heavier X9 variants can support 3,000 to 5,000 litres, but every litre adds a kilogram high on the frame and lengthens the stopping distance. A useful rule is to carry the water needed for the first ten to fifteen minutes of attack at the intended flow rate, then rely on a refill point or a second appliance for duration.

Tank shape matters as much as capacity. A shallow, wide tank with internal baffles keeps the centre of gravity low and limits slosh when the truck turns or brakes on a gravel road. A tall narrow tank of the same volume raises the rollover risk on a side slope. Specify the tank in litres and the fully laden mass in tonnes together, because one figure without the other says nothing about what the chassis will actually carry.

How should the pump drive be arranged?

Most fire pumps are driven either from a gearbox PTO or from a separate auxiliary engine. A PTO-driven pump is cheaper, lighter and uses the same fuel tank, but it ties pump output to chassis engine speed and means the truck engine runs while the crew works at the scene. For a rural brigade that may spend twenty minutes at a fire with the hose line operating, an auxiliary engine lets the chassis engine be shut down, saving fuel and reducing cab noise at the scene.

A typical medium fire pump delivers 1,000 to 2,500 litres per minute at 8 to 12 bar, and a foam proportioner adds concentrate at 3 to 6 percent for flammable-liquid fires. On the X9, the gearbox PTO route is favoured where the Fast gearbox offers a suitable aperture and the ratio matches the pump curve at the engine's torque peak; confirm the ratio before ordering. Whatever the drive, specify a pump that holds pressure when several hoses run at once, because a falling line pressure at the wrong moment is a serious safety issue.

Key point: Choose the pump drive from the time the crew spends stationary at a fire. Short urban responses justify a gearbox PTO; long rural attacks with the line operating for twenty minutes or more justify an auxiliary engine despite the extra weight and cost.

Why does 4x4 matter for rural fire work?

The reason a rural brigade specifies an X9 rather than a cheaper 4x2 is access. Farm tracks, wet paddocks, forest roads and unsealed lanes are where a 4x2 tender loses traction, and a fire that is not reached in the first ten minutes grows quickly. The X9 all-wheel-drive system, combined with a short wheelbase on lighter variants, gives the approach angle and low-speed traction needed to get close to the work. Buyers can review the SAGMOTO off-road 4x4 mining trucks platform notes for the driveline's off-road behaviour and component strength.

All-wheel drive is not a licence to ignore weight. A loaded tender is top-heavy, and a 4x4 on a side slope can still roll. Keep the water tank low, fit a wide track where offered, and train drivers to read the ground before committing. The driveline earns its keep on the approach; it does not suspend the laws of gravity at the fire scene.

What does the weight budget look like?

A light X9 tender must balance water, crew, equipment and pump against the front and rear axle limits. A 2,000 litre tank is two tonnes before the body, and a pump, foam tank, hoses and four crew members add more. On the lighter 4x4 variant this can push the rear axle close to its rating. Weigh the truck fully equipped, with water, fuel, crew and every tool aboard, and compare the result with the axle plates rather than the brochure payload.

How quickly can a brigade get the first line out?

Response is a sequence of small delays. The truck must start, the crew must don breathing equipment, the pump must prime and the first hose must be laid. A well-organised tender keeps the pump primed from a domestic supply, carries pre-connected hose lengths, and stows the nozzles where a firefighter can reach them without opening a locker in the dark. Practising this sequence matters more than an extra 500 litres of water.

Specify the body layout with the crew in mind. Lockers should open from the safe side of the road, steps should be non-slip, and the pump controls should be reachable from ground level where possible. A tender that takes three minutes to get a line wet has lost the window in which a small fire is easy to control.

Key point: The value of a fire tender is measured in seconds to first water, not in tank litres. A smaller, well-laid-out truck that puts a line in service in ninety seconds outperforms a larger one that takes three minutes to organise.

What equipment should the body include?

A rural tender is a working toolbox as much as a water carrier. Standard equipment usually includes two to four lengths of 65 mm hose, a smaller 38 mm hand line, adjustable and fog nozzles, a suction hose with strainer, a foam inductor and concentrate, breathing apparatus for the crew, a first-aid kit and a portable pump for drafting from a dam or tank. Lockers must be labelled and waterproof, because equipment that is wet or misplaced at midnight is equipment that is not used.

Specify the mounting of each item at the design stage. Heavy suction hose belongs low and central; breathing apparatus belongs close to the crew seats so it can be donned before the truck stops. A body designer who has watched a real crew work will place these items differently from one who has only drawn a locker layout.

How do you plan the water supply?

A tender carries a finite amount of water, and the first attack is only useful if a refill is close behind. Before specifying the tank, map the standpipes, dams and tankers available in the response area and note which of them a 4x4 can reach in dry and wet seasons. In many rural districts the limiting factor is not the pump but the distance to the next water source, so a smaller tank with a reliable refill route can sustain a longer attack than a large tank that must itself be refilled by road.

Where a draft source is used, specify a suction hose with a strainer and a pump that can prime reliably from up to seven metres of lift. Train the crew to set the strainer deep enough to avoid a vortex and clear of silt, because a blocked intake at the wrong moment empties the line. A second lightweight portable pump kept on the tender can draft from a source the truck cannot reach, adding useful flexibility to a rural water plan.

How should the tender be maintained?

A fire pump that has not run for a month may fail to prime at the scene, so run the pump briefly each week and record the result. Change pump oil and filters on an hour-based schedule, inspect the priming system, check the foam proportioner calibration and flush the tank if it has held water for a long period. Check tyres and suspension after off-road calls, because a tender that is ready for the next fire is one whose chassis has been inspected as carefully as its pump.

A rural fire tender succeeds on the quality of its first minutes. Specify the tank for the first attack, the pump for the pressure the crew needs, and the 4x4 driveline for the roads the brigade actually drives. On the X9 platform those pieces fit together well, provided the weight is checked at full load and the crew has practised the drill. The result is an appliance that reaches the fire and puts water on it while the fire is still small.

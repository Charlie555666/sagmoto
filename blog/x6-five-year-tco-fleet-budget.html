SAGMOTO X6 Five-Year Total Cost of Ownership: A Fleet Budget Model

2026-11-19Buying & Import Guide9 min read

A purchase price is a single line in a five-year budget. The lines that decide whether a fleet makes money are fuel, tyres, scheduled and unscheduled maintenance, insurance, downtime and the value recovered when the truck is sold. On a medium-duty vehicle such as the SAGMOTO X6, where the engine works hard at modest road speeds and the truck changes body frequently, those running lines can outweigh the purchase price within three years. This article builds a transparent five-year model for a notional X6 fleet and shows how each assumption moves the result.

The X6 is a medium-duty cargo and dropside platform powered by a Cummins ISD engine rated at 210 hp with 800 to 950 Nm, coupled to a Fast eight-speed gearbox. It is offered in 4x2, 6x4 and 4x4 forms, with a 4x2 payload of roughly 9 to 11.5 tonnes and a 6x4 payload of 14.5 to 16.5 tonnes across a GCW envelope of 12 to 60 tonnes. That range makes it a common choice for regional distribution, construction supply and municipal work, where annual distances are moderate but the truck is used every day. The model below uses the 4x2 dropside as the reference because it is the highest-volume configuration.

Key point: In the baseline model below, fuel is the largest single cost line at just under 60 percent of the five-year total, and residual value is the only line that reduces it. A buyer who negotiates the purchase price but ignores fuel consumption and resale has optimised the smallest part of the budget.

Setting the boundaries of the model

Every TCO figure depends on the duty cycle, so the assumptions must be written down before the arithmetic. The baseline fleet is twenty 4x2 X6 dropsides running 60,000 km per year for five years on a mixed regional route. Diesel is priced at USD 1.05 per litre, consumption is 22 litres per 100 km, scheduled maintenance is contracted at USD 1,500 per truck per year and unscheduled repairs are budgeted at USD 600. Insurance is USD 1,200 per year, downtime is valued at USD 250 per lost day with three lost days a year, and the truck is sold at 25 percent of its purchase price after year five. Freight and import duty are charged at 12 percent of the ex-works price.

These are illustrative numbers intended to demonstrate the method, not a quotation. Replace each one with your own supplier quote, local diesel price and insurance rate before using the output for a board decision. The structure of the model holds in any market; only the inputs change. What matters is that every cost is expressed per truck over the same five-year horizon, so a line such as tyres that recurs every two years is annualised rather than counted once.

Expressed as a cost per kilometre, the baseline is around USD 0.40 per truck-kilometre. That figure is the one to compare against the freight rate. If the route earns USD 0.55 per kilometre, the fleet has a working margin; if it earns USD 0.38, no amount of purchase-price negotiation will make it profitable.

Fuel, bodies and the duty cycle

Fuel is dominant because a 210 hp medium-duty truck spends much of its life part-loaded in stop-start traffic, where the engine is inefficient and auxiliary loads are high. Two practical levers reduce it. The first is specification: an eight-speed gearbox with a taller top ratio suits a regional route, while a dropside used on a hilly construction supply run needs the shorter ratios to avoid constant downshifting. The second is body match. If the X6 is ordered with one of the SAGMOTO cargo truck flatbed box stake bodies, the tare mass and the box height both matter: a taller box adds aerodynamic drag at 70 to 80 km/h, and a heavier body eats into the 9 to 11.5 tonne payload so the truck needs an extra trip for the same freight.

Body choice also drives residual value. A standard dropside or flatbed sells into a wide pool of buyers at auction, whereas a highly specialised body narrows the market. If the truck is likely to be sold at year five, the plain body is the safer financial choice even if the specialised body earns slightly more in years one and two. Budget managers often overlook this because resale sits in a different department from operations, but it is the largest negative line in the model after fuel.

Tyres, maintenance and the cost of downtime

Tyres are budgeted at USD 1,300 per truck over five years, which assumes one full set plus a few replacements at around USD 180 per tyre. That figure is sensitive to route surface and driver behaviour; a truck running on broken site roads can double it. Because the X6 is often used on construction supply, inspection of tyre pressure at every service is worth more than a theoretical saving on the purchase price of the tyres themselves. Record pressures and tread depths so that a pattern of abnormal wear is visible before it becomes a blowout.

Downtime is the line most often omitted and most often underestimated. Three lost days a year at USD 250 a day sounds modest until the fleet is twenty trucks and the lost days cluster during the busy season. The way to reduce it is to stock the high-wear parts that actually fail: clutch kits, brake linings, water pumps, starter motors and the sensors that generate fault codes. A small parts kit held at the depot costs far less than a week of trucks standing idle waiting for a container from overseas. When discussing support with a supplier, ask for the list of parts carried regionally rather than a general promise of after-sales service.

Using the model in a purchase decision

Build the same table for each configuration you are considering and compare the five-year net figure, not the showroom price. A 6x4 X6 with a higher payload may cost more to buy but require fewer trips, so include the fuel and driver cost of the extra journeys if the 4x2 cannot carry the load in one movement. Conversely, if the route is genuinely light, the 4x2 avoids the tyre scrub and extra fuel of the tandem axle. The model is a frame for reasoning, not a claim that one configuration is universally better.

One more line deserves a place in the sheet: the fully loaded annual cost of the driver. Where a configuration difference changes trip time enough to alter headcount, that cost can outweigh the fuel saving it was meant to deliver. Add it explicitly when the fleet is large enough that one extra trip a day means another driver on the payroll. At twenty trucks the effect is easy to see; at two it may be irrelevant, which is precisely why the model must be built around your own fleet size rather than copied from another operator.

Finally, revisit the assumptions at the end of year two. Actual fuel records, real repair invoices and the current resale market will show which estimates were optimistic. A budget that is never updated becomes a defence of the original decision rather than a tool for running the fleet. Keep the spreadsheet live, and the five-year model will remain useful long after the trucks have been delivered.

Conclusion

For the baseline twenty-truck X6 fleet, five years of ownership costs roughly USD 118,700 per truck after resale, with fuel at nearly 60 percent and acquisition at about 30 percent. The largest improvements come from controlling consumption, matching the body and gearbox to the route, and protecting residual value with a specification that resells easily. Use the table as a structure, feed it your own diesel price, mileage, repair history and disposal estimate, and compare configurations on five-year cost per kilometre rather than on the number on the invoice. That is how a fleet budget stops being a guess and becomes a decision you can defend.

Payload and body choice belong in the same spreadsheet as fuel and finance, because 1.5 tonnes of extra legal payload shifts cost per kilometre more than a small diesel-price move.

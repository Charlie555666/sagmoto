Importing Trucks into Vanuatu: Duty, Shipping and Spec Guide

2026-11-24Buying & Import Guide9 min read

Vanuatu is a scattered island market where trucks arrive in modest annual numbers and every unit absorbs a large share of the ocean freight bill. The country does not attract the frequent roll-on roll-off sailings that serve larger destinations, so vehicles generally move as containerised or break-bulk cargo on a long transit with a high cost per unit. A buyer planning a SAGMOTO truck import into Vanuatu has four questions to resolve before placing an order: which port will clear the vehicle, what duty and tax will be assessed, what steering and mechanical specification suit the road network, and how the truck will tolerate salt air and an annual cyclone season. This guide takes those questions in order and uses indicative figures wherever a rate is set by the authorities and can change without notice.

The two commercial gateways are Port Vila on Efate and Luganville on Espiritu Santo. Port Vila handles most general cargo and has the more developed customs and bonded-storage facilities, while Luganville serves the northern islands and the larger construction and forestry operations on Santo. Because the annual volume is small, a single vessel call may carry only two or three trucks, and the freight cost is divided among those few units. A buyer who groups an order with other cargo, or who can wait for a consolidated sailing, will pay noticeably less per truck than one shipping a single unit on an urgent timetable. Treating the sailing schedule as part of the purchase decision saves real money on an island import.

Key point: Vanuatu drives on the right, so the correct factory specification is left-hand drive. Confirm the steering position on the proforma invoice before production begins, because changing it after the truck has entered the shipping yard is both expensive and slow.

Duty, VAT and the indicative cost envelope

Vanuatu applies an import duty and a value added tax to commercial vehicles, and both are calculated on a customs value that normally includes the invoice price, freight and insurance to the port of discharge. Duty on trucks falls in a broad band that a buyer should treat as indicative only, because the tariff is revised from time to time and can vary with vehicle category and gross weight. VAT is then charged on the duty-inclusive value at the prevailing standard rate. On top of these national charges an importer may meet a port handling fee, a customs processing charge and, if a clearing agent is appointed, a professional fee. Because every one of these numbers can move, the only responsible method is to obtain a written estimate from a licensed broker in Port Vila or Luganville against the exact vehicle and tariff line before the order is signed.

Rates change and must be confirmed with Vanuatu Customs. That point cannot be overstated: a figure copied from an old guide, or borrowed from a neighbouring country, will not protect a buyer at the clearance counter. Ask the broker to state the tariff line applied, the customs value used and the effective date of the rate, then keep that written estimate inside the shipment file. When comparing a SAGMOTO truck import with a quotation from another seller, compare the landed estimate rather than the factory price, since two trucks with almost identical invoices can land at very different totals once duty, VAT and port charges are stacked on top. A realistic landed budget prevents the awkward moment when the vehicle is on the wharf but the cash is short.

Shipping routes and freight per unit

Few carriers run dedicated vehicle carriers to Vanuatu, so a truck normally travels inside a forty-foot container, on a flat rack, or as break-bulk lashed on deck. Container loading protects the cab and body from salt spray but demands that the vehicle be secured to the floor with timber chocks and steel straps at the frame rails. Flat rack shipment avoids the height limit of a closed box and suits a high-roof sleeper or a tipper body, but it exposes the truck to a wet, salty environment for the whole crossing. Break-bulk can be cheapest when a project already has heavy cargo on the same vessel, yet it carries the greatest risk of cosmetic and corrosion damage. The right choice depends on the truck's height, the sailing on offer and how much cosmetic perfection matters to the buyer.

Freight per unit is high precisely because sailings are infrequent and volumes are low. A container that leaves with one truck costs almost the same to position as one carrying three, so the buyer who fills the space pays far less per vehicle. Transit from a Chinese port can run three to six weeks depending on routing and island calls, and cyclone weather can stretch that further.

Steering position and island road specification

Because traffic keeps to the right in Vanuatu, trucks registered there are expected to be left-hand drive. Ordering the correct steering position at the factory is straightforward; converting a unit after it arrives is not. A buyer should print the steering configuration on the proforma invoice, repeat it in the specification sheet and check it again on the pre-shipment photographs. The same discipline applies to the speedometer calibration, the lighting conforming to the destination and the mirror set, since an island inspection may refuse a vehicle whose lights or mirrors do not match the local rule. Getting these details right in the factory avoids a queue of rectification work at the port.

Road conditions shape the mechanical specification more than the headline power figure. Efate has a sealed circumferential road and reasonable paved streets in Port Vila, but the moment a truck turns inland, or operates on Santo, Malekula or the outer islands, it meets unsealed surfaces, mud, washouts and steep, loose gradients. Ground clearance, a robust leaf-spring suspension, a protective sump guard and tyres with a tough sidewall matter more here than a large engine. For distribution around Port Vila a four-by-two medium cargo truck with a payload in the nine to eleven tonne class is usually sufficient, while a construction or forestry operator on rougher ground should look at a six-by-four chassis with a stronger frame and a lower-ratio final drive. Buyers can review the SAGMOTO cargo truck flatbed box stake body options when matching a chassis to the island roads they actually use.

Key point: On Vanuatu's unsealed roads, spec the truck for traction and underbody protection first and horsepower second. A lower final-drive ratio and a reinforced suspension will earn back their cost on the first wet season, while an oversized engine adds fuel burn without adding grip.

Cyclone season, salt corrosion and storage

The cyclone season runs broadly from November to April, and it affects an imported truck at three separate stages: on the vessel, on the wharf and in use. Salt-laden air accelerates corrosion on exposed steel, electrical connectors and brake components, so a protective wax or oil coating on the chassis and a generous application of dielectric grease on connectors are sensible before shipment. During the crossing, a containerised unit should have desiccant bags and a moisture barrier to limit condensation, while a flat-racked unit needs its air intake and exhaust sealed against spray. Once ashore, a truck stored in the open near the coast should be washed down with fresh water regularly and parked with the doors and windows open to reduce mould and electrical faults.

Cyclone exposure also changes how a buyer should think about bodywork and glazing. A cab with a strong roof structure and secure door seals will age better than one with thin panels and loose trim, and a flatbed or dropside body should be bolted in a way that can be inspected after a storm. Operators who must keep working through the wet season should carry a small stock of wiper blades, a water-separator service kit and spare electrical connectors, because a minor fault can idle a truck for a week when the nearest workshop is on another island. Planning for corrosion and weather is not cosmetic maintenance in Vanuatu; it is the difference between a truck that lasts a decade and one that is written off early.

Used-truck age rules and registration

Importers considering a used unit need to check the age and condition rules that apply at registration, because a truck that is acceptable in the export country may face limits or extra inspection in Vanuatu. Age limits, where they apply, are usually tied to the year of first registration rather than the year of manufacture, and a vehicle beyond the threshold can be refused or charged a higher rate. Even when an older truck is admissible, the buyer should budget for a thorough pre-shipment inspection of the chassis, the braking system and the emission controls, since a vehicle that fails an inspection after arrival has no easy route back to the seller. Ask the broker to confirm the current age policy in writing before paying a deposit on a used machine.

Registration also requires a coherent document set: the original bill of lading, a commercial invoice, a certificate of origin, an export certificate or deregistration paper from the country of supply, and a conformity or inspection document where requested. Names and vehicle identification numbers must match across every sheet, because a single mismatch can hold a truck at the port for weeks. For a buyer weighing a new unit against a used one, the new truck usually wins on paperwork simplicity and on the ability to specify left-hand drive, corrosion protection and the right final drive before it leaves the factory. That is why many island fleets standardize on a small number of new chassis and keep them for a long service life rather than chasing cheap used imports with uncertain histories.

A practical order checklist before shipment

Bring the plan together in a single written checklist. Record the discharge port, sailing window, duty and VAT estimate, exact steering position, final-drive ratio and corrosion-protection package. Confirm who is responsible for loading and lashing at the export port, and photograph the unit at loading as evidence if damage is found at discharge. An importer who can tick every line before the vessel sails is far less likely to face a surprise at clearance.

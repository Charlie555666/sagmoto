What Incoterms Mean for Truck Buyers Importing from China

2026-11-13Buyer Decision9 min read

Quick answer: For most first-time truck importers, CIF or CIF-plus is safest because the Chinese seller books freight and insurance to your port, so you pay roughly 8 to 15 percent above the EXW factory price for that coverage; EXW is cheapest but leaves you to arrange everything from the SAGMOTO plant gate onward.

What does each Incoterm shift in cost and risk

Incoterms are the international commercial terms published by the International Chamber of Commerce that fix, for each shipment, where the seller's responsibility ends and the buyer's begins. For a complete truck leaving China, the practical question is simple: at what point does the risk of loss, the cost of freight, and the cost of insurance pass from the exporter to the importer. The five terms most relevant to whole-truck imports are EXW, FOB, CFR, CIF, and DAP, and each moves that line a little further toward the buyer's door. Understanding the line is the difference between a quote you can budget and a quote that hides a surprise customs or freight bill.

EXW, or Ex Works, is the starting point: the seller makes the truck available at its plant, and everything after that gate is the buyer's problem and cost. FOB, Free On Board, moves the line to the moment the truck is loaded onboard the vessel at the Chinese port, with the buyer still arranging and paying the ocean freight. CFR, Cost and Freight, has the seller pay the ocean freight but not insurance. CIF, Cost Insurance and Freight, adds insurance to the seller's scope. DAP, Delivered At Place, carries the seller's responsibility almost to the buyer's named destination, excluding only the import duty. The table below maps each term to who pays what.

EXW versus FOB: where the buyer takes control

Under EXW the buyer must collect the vehicle at the factory, organise Chinese export declaration, and contract the inland haul to the port, which is manageable for experienced importers with a freight forwarder in China but daunting for a first shipment. FOB is the more common choice for buyers who want control of the ocean leg, because the seller handles export clearance and loading, and the buyer then books the ship and insurance from the Chinese port. The price gap between EXW and FOB is usually the inland China transport plus export handling, often 300 to 900 US dollars per unit depending on distance from Xi'an to the coast.

CFR and CIF: freight and insurance in the seller's hands

CFR and CIF are where many overseas distributors feel most comfortable, because the Chinese seller contracts the ocean carrier and, under CIF, also places marine insurance on the buyer's behalf. The buyer still takes risk once the truck is onboard, but does not have to negotiate a shipping line or puzzle over coverage. The cost the seller adds for CIF above the FOB value is typically the freight plus a small insurance premium of 1 to 2 percent of the cargo value, which is modest protection against a total loss at sea. The weakness of CIF is that the seller chooses the carrier and the cover, so a buyer wanting a specific liner or all-risk policy may prefer CFR and to buy insurance directly.

CIF does not mean cleared: CIF covers freight and marine insurance to your destination port, but it never pays the import duty or the destination terminal handling. Budget 5 to 25 percent on top for duty and local charges, depending on your tariff line.

DAP: delivered to your door with duty to clear

DAP pushes the seller's responsibility furthest, to the buyer's named place, which might be a warehouse or a port inland from the sea. The seller arranges and pays the full international transport and insurance, and the buyer only handles import clearance and duty at the destination. This term is attractive for buyers who want a single point of contact and a predictable landed cost, but it demands a precise named place in the contract because any ambiguity about where delivery ends can spark a dispute. DAP excludes duty, so a buyer who also wants the seller to clear customs should ask for DDP, which is rarer for complete trucks because it forces the exporter to understand foreign tax law. For most distributors, DAP is the ceiling of convenience before the seller's compliance burden grows too large.

How should a first-time buyer choose an Incoterm

A first-time importer should weigh three things: confidence in finding a freight forwarder, appetite for dealing with export clearance in China, and tolerance for a surprise at the destination port. Buyers with no forwarder and no Chinese export experience should choose CIF or DAP, because the seller absorbs the steps most likely to go wrong. Buyers who already ship containers and have a broker should choose FOB or CFR to capture a better ocean rate they negotiate themselves. The false economy is EXW for a newcomer: the factory price looks lowest, but the buyer who misjudges export clearance or inland haul can lose the saving and add weeks of delay. Match the term to your own capability, not to the lowest headline number.

Which Incoterm suits a distributor importing whole trucks

A distributor who imports whole trucks on a repeating basis usually settles on FOB or CIF, because those terms leave the buyer owning the ocean relationship while the seller handles the Chinese side cleanly. FOB suits distributors with a preferred freight contract; CIF suits those who would rather the seller place the cover. A distributor who wants the truck delivered to a domestic warehouse without managing the import leg should use DAP, accepting a higher unit price for a simpler receipt. The key is consistency: locking one Incoterm across a fleet order lets the distributor compare quotes like for like and avoid mixing freight and insurance into the truck price. Buyers should also confirm the authorized SAGMOTO distributor verification before signing so the quoting entity is genuinely authorised to export.

Do ro-ro and container shipping split costs differently

The Incoterm sets who pays, but the shipping method sets how much is paid, and the two interact at the port. Roll-on roll-off, or ro-ro, drives the truck onto the vessel under its own power and charges a per-unit fee that is simple but relatively high for a single truck. Container shipping, by contrast, can carry a complete knock-down kit or a body in a box at a lower per-shipment rate, but adds devanning and reassembly cost at the destination. Frequently Asked Questions

What does EXW actually require the buyer to do?

EXW places the entire export burden on the buyer, who must collect the truck at the SAGMOTO plant gate, arrange Chinese export clearance, and pay all freight from Xi'an onward. The factory price looks lowest, but the buyer absorbs inland haul, export paperwork, ocean freight, and insurance, so only experienced importers with a China freight forwarder should accept it. New importers rarely save money once those costs are added.

Is CIF enough to cover import duty at my port?

CIF covers freight and marine insurance to your destination port but never the import duty, which typically runs 5 to 25 percent depending on your country's tariff line for complete trucks. The buyer must still clear customs and pay duty and local handling at arrival. Budget the duty separately from the CIF quote so the landed cost is realistic before you commit.

Which Incoterm should a first-time importer choose?

First-time importers should choose CIF or CIF-plus because the seller handles freight and insurance, leaving only destination clearance and duty for the buyer to manage. This removes the Chinese export steps most likely to cause delay for a newcomer. FOB or EXW only make sense once the buyer has a trusted forwarder and understands the export process.

Do ro-ro and container shipping change who pays what?

Ro-ro and container shipping use the same Incoterm cost split but differ in handling fees, with ro-ro charging a per-unit terminal fee and container shipping adding a devanning cost at roughly 200 to 600 dollars per box. The Incoterm still decides whether the seller or buyer pays that fee; the method only changes how large the fee is. Whole trucks suit ro-ro, while kits suit containers.

Can the seller arrange final delivery to my warehouse?

Only DAP or DDP puts delivery to your named place in the seller's scope, with DAP excluding import duty and DDP including it, so choose DAP when you want to clear customs yourself. DDP is uncommon for complete trucks because it forces the exporter to manage foreign tax law. State the named place precisely in the contract to avoid a dispute over where delivery ends.

Municipal Street Sweeper Upfit on the SAGMOTO E3 Chassis

2026-11-11Industry Application8 min read

Municipal fleets across emerging markets face a recurring procurement problem that rarely gets discussed in sales brochures: the chassis they buy for street cleaning must survive rough secondary roads, operate eight hour shifts without overheating, and still leave enough payload margin for both water and collected debris. The SAGMOTO E3 chassis-cab, powered by the Yuchai YC6MK engine in the 340 to 400 horsepower band, has become a practical platform for sweeper upfits because its wheelbase and front axle rating give body builders room to mount tanks, brushes and a hydraulic pump without exceeding the legal gross vehicle weight. For public works departments that already run SAGMOTO tippers and cargo trucks, standardising on the E3 also means one driver licence class, one service network and one set of spare filters across the whole fleet.

Why the SAGMOTO E3 chassis suits municipal sweeper duty

The E3 sits in the mixed duty segment and was originally developed for value-oriented long haul work, which means the driveline is deliberately over-built for the relatively gentle speeds of a sweeper. The Yuchai YC6MK delivers usable torque from low engine revolutions, a trait that matters when a sweeper crawls through a market district at walking pace yet must instantly accelerate to clear a junction before oncoming traffic. Operators report that the engine braking and the optional retarder reduce wear on the foundation brakes during the stop-start cycles that define urban cleaning, and that the cab ergonomics keep a driver comfortable through a full shift of constant manoeuvring.

Another advantage is the availability of the Fast 12JSD transmission, which gives the body builder a clean power take-off mounting face and a well-documented output speed curve. Because the E3 uses a leaf spring suspension at the rear, the sweep body and water tank can be bolted to a straight frame rail without the complication of air suspension levelling valves that complicate body alignment. The standard dual-circuit air brake system and ABS plus ESC are retained through the upfit, so the finished sweeper remains compliant with the same type approval as the base truck and avoids a separate braking re-certification that would add cost and delay to a public tender.

Choose a chassis with a wheelbase that places the rear axle at least 1.2 metres behind the sweeper brush centreline. This prevents the swept debris plume from being re-run over by the rear tyres and keeps the body builder's subframe within the 13 tonne rear axle envelope.

Auxiliary engine drive versus PTO-driven sweep systems

There are two established ways to power the brushes, vacuum fan and water pump on a sweeper body, and the choice shapes the entire tender specification. The first is a dedicated auxiliary engine mounted on the body, usually a small diesel or petrol unit with its own fuel tank and exhaust. The second is a power take-off driven system that draws hydraulic energy from the chassis transmission through a geared interface. A third, newer option is a fully electric auxiliary driven from a separate battery pack, but that remains a niche for strict zero emission districts where the sweeper must enter pedestrianised zones.

The PTO-driven route is usually preferred for municipal fleets that run the sweeper six or seven days a week, because a single engine to maintain is simpler for a public works depot that already stocks Yuchai service parts. The auxiliary engine route keeps the chassis warranty cleaner and lets the sweeper operate even when the chassis engine is being serviced, which can be important for contract cleaning where downtime carries penalty clauses and the municipality is paying by the lane kilometre cleaned rather than by the month.

Specify the PTO with a dedicated cooler circuit. Sweeper hydraulic pumps run near continuous duty at high pressure, and a pump without temperature control will fail inside the first season of summer operation in tropical conditions.

Water tank sizing and dust suppression strategy

Dust suppression is the difference between a sweeper that actually cleans a street and one that simply relocates the dust downwind into the next block. Water tank capacity on an E3-based body typically ranges from 2000 litres on a compact rear-mounted layout to 4500 litres on a longer wheelbase with side pods. The correct size depends on the length of the cleaning route and the interval back to the refill point, not on a fixed rule copied from another city's fleet that may face different surface and climate conditions.

A useful planning figure is that a well-tuned spray bar consumes between 0.4 and 0.8 litres of water per lane kilometre at low sweep speed, depending on surface porosity and ambient temperature. A 3000 litre tank therefore supports roughly 4000 to 7000 lane kilometres before refill, which is ample for a single eight hour shift in most municipal districts. Operators should resist oversizing the tank, because every extra litre of water is dead weight that reduces the legal payload available for collected debris and pushes the axle closer to its legal limit at the weighbridge.

A depot that plans night cleaning will also want a tank with a low centre of gravity to keep the sweep body stable on roundabouts and flyovers where a top-heavy unit would lean uncomfortably through the curve.

Brush and pickup systems for mixed urban debris

Urban debris is rarely uniform across a single route. A municipal sweep may combine leaf litter, wind-blown sand, broken glass, paper and the coarse grit that was spread for winter traction months earlier. The E3 sweeper body should therefore be specified with a gutter brush pair at the front and a main cylindrical brush at the centre, feeding a conveyor to the hopper. The gutter brushes sweep material toward the centre line where the main brush throws it onto the pickup conveyor for transfer to the debris hopper, so the system works as a continuous loop rather than a single passive broom.

Brush material choice is a maintenance cost lever that tenders often ignore. Steel wire brushes last longer on abrasive surfaces but damage decorative paving and generate noise complaints in residential streets, while polypropylene blends are gentler and quieter but wear faster in sandy conditions. A mixed bristle that combines steel and polymer is the common compromise for general municipal duty, and the body builder should quote a per-set brush life so the lifecycle cost can be compared between bidders rather than only the purchase price that appears on the front page of the proposal.

Writing a municipal tender specification

Public tenders succeed when the specification is measurable rather than descriptive, and this is where many first-time sweeper purchases go wrong. Instead of asking for a robust sweeper, the document should state the tank volume, the hopper dump angle, the minimum vacuum at the pickup head, the brush diameter and the required water coverage width. Including the chassis make and engine family prevents low-ball substitutions during bidding, because a supplier cannot quietly swap in a lighter chassis to win on price and then claim it meets the spirit of the requirement.

The tender should also reference the E3 rear axle rating of 13 tonnes, the Yuchai YC6MK power band and the requirement for a documented PTO continuous rating in kilowatts. By naming these items the municipality protects itself from offers built on a lighter chassis that would fail under daily loaded sweep cycles, and it gives the evaluation committee an objective checklist instead of a subjective judgement about which truck looks sturdy enough on the showroom floor.

Duty cycle, shift length and maintenance access

A realistic duty cycle for a municipal sweeper is four to six hours of active sweeping per shift, with the remainder spent on travel between zones, refill stops and hopper discharge at the tip. The E3 chassis supports this pattern because the cab is designed for long haul comfort and the driver spends the whole shift in low speed manoeuvring that is physically taxing on neck and shoulders. Maintenance access matters just as much as performance: the body builder should leave the chassis oil sump, fuel filter and battery accessible without removing the sweep body for routine service, or the depot will dread every scheduled intervention.

Depots that plan a seven day week should budget a weekly greasing of brush bearings and a monthly hydraulic filter change, in addition to the standard chassis service. The chassis itself follows the standard SAGMOTO service schedule for the Yuchai engine, which keeps the sweeper inside the same parts network as the rest of a mixed municipal fleet and avoids the stranded-asset risk of a bespoke imported sweeper with no local support when a sealed bearing or a pump seal eventually needs replacing.

The finished E3 sweeper is a defensible municipal investment because it pairs a proven long haul chassis with a body that can be specced from a measured tender. Fleets that standardise on the E3 reduce driver training overhead, share spare parts with tipper and cargo variants, and gain a cleaning platform that survives the secondary roads where cheaper imported sweepers quickly fail under daily load and the punishing thermal cycle of tropical urban duty.

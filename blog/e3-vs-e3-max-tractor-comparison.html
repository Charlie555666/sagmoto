SAGMOTO E3 vs E3 MAX: Choosing Between Medium and Heavy Tractor Platforms

2026-11-26Comparison Guide9 min read

Two tractors can share a name and still be built for different jobs. The SAGMOTO E3 and the SAGMOTO E3 MAX are both offered as tractor platforms, but they sit in different weight and power classes. The E3 is a light to medium tractor using a Yuchai YC6MK in the 340 to 400 HP band with 1,300 to 1,600 N m, while the E3 MAX is a heavy machine with Cummins or Weichai power in the 430 to 520 HP band and 2,000 to 2,400 N m. Choosing between them is not a matter of which is better; it is a matter of which gross weight and duty cycle the fleet actually runs. This comparison sets out the differences in power, gearbox, axle layout, cost per tonne and resale so a buyer can match the tractor to the work.

Positioning and intended duty

The E3 is built around medium-duty work. It is offered as a 4x2 or 6x4 tractor and is commonly paired with lighter trailers, distribution work and regional haul where the gross combination weight sits in the lower part of the rated 18 to 100 t span. The engine is a Yuchai YC6MK producing 340 to 400 HP, and the gearbox choices are Fast 8, 9 or 12-speed units. The E3 MAX by contrast is a heavy platform, available as a 6x4 tractor or an 8x4 tipper rated at 31 t GVW, with a Cummins or Weichai engine in the 430 to 520 HP band and a Fast 12JSD200T 12-speed gearbox. The names are similar but the applications are not interchangeable.

A buyer should start with the trailer and the load, not with the model name. If the daily work involves a single light trailer on a regional route, the E3 has ample power and will cost less to buy and run. If the truck will pull a heavy trailer at the upper end of the gross weight range or work as an 8x4 tipper, the E3 MAX has the torque and the axle capacity that the job demands. Specifying the smaller tractor for heavy work is a false economy, because it will work at full capacity continuously and wear quickly.

Power and torque compared

The clearest technical separation is in the powertrain. The E3 Yuchai unit delivers 340 to 400 HP and 1,300 to 1,600 N m, adequate for medium loads and rolling terrain. The E3 MAX raises that to 430 to 520 HP and 2,000 to 2,400 N m, a substantial step that shows up in loaded starts and in the ability to hold speed on a long gradient. The extra torque also reduces the number of downshifts on a hilly route, which lowers driver workload and fuel burn, but only if the fleet actually uses the capacity.

Torque curve shape matters as much as the peak. Both engines should be assessed at the engine speed where the truck spends most of its life, typically between 1,200 and 1,700 rpm. Request the torque curve from the supplier and note the engine speed where peak torque arrives, because an engine that reaches its plateau early will feel stronger in daily work than the headline number suggests. On a tractor that spends its life loaded, the area under the curve in the working range is a better guide than maximum horsepower.

Gearbox and driveline choices

The E3 can be specified with Fast 8, 9 or 12-speed gearboxes, which suits its role as a flexible medium tractor. A fleet running mostly flat regional work may choose the 8-speed for simplicity and lower cost, while one facing mixed terrain can specify the 12-speed for a wider ratio spread. The E3 MAX is more focused, using the Fast 12JSD200T 12-speed unit matched to its higher torque. That gearbox is built to handle the output of the larger engines and is a known quantity in heavy-truck workshops, which helps with service and parts.

Driveline specification should follow the torque. On the E3 MAX the propshafts, differentials and half shafts are sized for 2,000 to 2,400 N m, and the final drive is chosen around the intended gross weight and road speed. A buyer should ask for the road speed at a stated engine rpm in top gear for each quote, because that single figure reveals whether the truck was built for highway economy or for loaded hill work. On the E3 the same question applies, but the answer is shaped more by the gearbox count than by engine torque.

Axle layout and gross weight

The E3 is offered in 4x2 and 6x4 forms, with a tractor GCW quoted across the 18 to 100 t span depending on configuration. In practice a 4x2 E3 handles lighter distribution work, while a 6x4 version takes the heavier end of medium haulage. The E3 MAX as a tractor is a 6x4, and as a tipper it is an 8x4 rated at 31 t GVW. That 8x4 tipper configuration is important: it is a construction and bulk material machine, not a long-distance tractor, and it should be compared with other tippers rather than with the E3 tractor.

Axle loading is the practical limit. Even when a tractor is rated for a high gross weight, the individual axle ratings and the fifth wheel position decide what can legally and safely be carried. A fleet should obtain the loaded axle weights for the proposed trailer at the intended coupling position and check them against the quoted chassis. This discipline matters more for the E3, where a buyer might be tempted to run near the top of its range, than for the E3 MAX, which has more margin in its driveline for heavy work.

Key point: The E3 MAX 8x4 is a 31 t GVW tipper rather than a tractor, so compare it with tipper specifications; the E3 4x2 and 6x4 are the tractor variants in this pair.

Payload and cost per tonne

Cost per tonne is the fair way to compare two tractors of different capability. The E3 costs less to buy, uses less fuel and has lighter components, so on a light route it delivers a lower cost per tonne. The E3 MAX costs more and consumes more, but it can carry a heavier trailer in one trip where the E3 might need two. If a fleet's loads regularly exceed what the E3 can handle comfortably, the heavier tractor wins on cost per tonne despite its higher running cost. The answer depends entirely on the load profile.

Build the comparison with real numbers. Take the payload, the route distance, the fuel price and the number of trips per day, then calculate the cost per tonne-kilometre for each tractor. Include tyres and scheduled service, because heavier trucks consume these faster. Run the calculation for the busiest day and for the average day, since a tractor bought only for peak capacity is idle expense for the rest of the week. This approach removes the emotion from a decision between two similar names.

Key point: Compare the E3 and E3 MAX on cost per tonne-kilometre at your real payload, because a heavier tractor that removes a second trip can beat a lighter one on fuel alone.

Fuel economy and operating cost

The lighter E3 will normally record better fuel figures than the E3 MAX on the same route, simply because it moves less mass and uses a smaller engine. On a regional lane with moderate loads that difference accumulates into a meaningful annual saving. The E3 MAX closes the gap somewhat when working hard, because its larger engine can hold a higher gear on gradients where the E3 would downshift and burn fuel accelerating. A fair comparison uses the same trailer weight and the same route for both, which is why a horsepower-only judgement is misleading.

Cab, service and parts

Both tractors use cab and chassis components drawn from the wider SAGMOTO range, which helps with driver training and parts commonality. The service intervals for filters, oils and brakes follow the respective engine and gearbox suppliers, so the E3 Yuchai and the E3 MAX Cummins or Weichai do not share every service part. A fleet running both models should hold separate filter and service kits rather than assume interchangeability. Confirm the engine code on each unit, because the same model name can cover more than one engine option.

Resale depends on the buyer pool. A well specified E3 tractor with a documented service history is attractive to regional operators, while an E3 MAX in good condition finds buyers in heavy haulage and construction. Keep the engine code, gearbox model and service record with the vehicle, since these details support valuation. Which tractor fits your fleet

Choose the E3 when the work is regional, the trailers are light to medium and the priority is purchase price and fuel economy. Its 340 to 400 HP Yuchai and flexible gearbox options make it a sensible tool for distribution and general haulage where the truck is not working at its limit every day. Choose the E3 MAX when the gross weight is high, the routes include long gradients, or the job is an 8x4 tipper. Its 430 to 520 HP and stronger driveline give margin that the E3 cannot match.

Verification before ordering

Put the gross combination weight, the loaded axle weights and the route gradient profile in front of the supplier before a quote is finalised. Confirm the exact engine rating, the gearbox model and the final drive for each tractor, and ask for the road speed at a stated cruise rpm. If the E3 MAX is being considered as a tipper, agree the body tare and the 31 t GVW limit in writing, because the body weight decides the real payload. These details turn a close comparison into a straightforward decision.

The right choice is the tractor that matches the load, not the one with the larger engine. An E3 that is comfortably inside its capacity will be cheaper and more reliable than an E3 MAX running the same work, and an E3 MAX that is genuinely needed will outlast an overworked E3 by a wide margin. Model your own tonne-kilometres and the decision will follow from the data rather than from the badge.

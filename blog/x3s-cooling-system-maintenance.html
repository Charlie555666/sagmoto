SAGMOTO X3s Cooling System Maintenance: Radiator, CAC, Coolant and Fan Clutch

2026-11-11Maintenance8 min read

The SAGMOTO X3s is a heavy tractor and 8x4 dump platform powered by the Cummins ISME 420 or the Weichai WP10 and WP12 engine families, producing between 385 and 420 horsepower and paired with the Fast 12JSD twelve-speed transmission. In African and Middle Eastern mining and construction duty, the cooling system carries the full burden of keeping cylinder head temperatures safe while the truck crawls up grades in 40-degree heat with a full payload. A neglected radiator or a depleted coolant additive package is the fastest route to a warped head and a week-long repair. For overseas fleet buyers running the X3s in dusty, hot sites, a disciplined cooling maintenance routine is non-negotiable. This guide covers radiator and charge-air cooler cleaning, coolant specification with supplemental additives, water pump and thermostat service, fan clutch testing and the tropical uprating steps that keep the engine alive.

Radiator and Charge-Air Cooler Cleaning

The X3s mounts the engine radiator directly in front of the charge-air cooler, and both cores clog together with the fine dust thrown up by haul roads and crusher yards. When the passages block, intake air temperature climbs, boost pressure drops and exhaust gas temperature rises, which robs power and shortens turbo life. The correct cleaning method is to blow the cores out from the engine side toward the front with low-pressure compressed air, then rinse with a gentle water spray, never a high-pressure lance that folds the delicate fins. A fin comb restores bent louvres so air can pass.

Crews should inspect the core face every 250 hours of site operation and clean it whenever more than a third of the surface shows dust loading. Pay special attention to the bottom rows, where the heaviest grit settles and where leaks from the air dryer often leave a sticky film that traps more dust. A tell-tale sign of a blocked charge-air cooler is a falling boost reading on the dashboard gauge under load even though the compressor itself is healthy. Beyond the cores, the shroud seals and the fan-to-radiator air gap matter as much as the fins. A perished seal lets hot air recirculate around the side of the core instead of through it, and the cooling capacity falls sharply even with a clean radiator. Technicians should refit any loose shroud section and confirm the fan blade tip clearance to the shroud is within the service limit, because a wide gap is a silent thief of cooling performance on long climbs.

Coolant Specification and SCA Additives

The X3s cooling circuit is designed around a ethylene-glycol based coolant mixed fifty-fifty with demineralised water, giving freeze protection and a boiling margin suited to tropical operation when the pressurised cap is intact. Fleet operators using conventional inorganic coolant must maintain the supplemental coolant additive level with a regular test strip, because the SCA is what protects the liner surfaces from cavitation erosion and the solder joints from corrosion. Running plain water or an additive-starved mix is the classic cause of cylinder liner pitting that later shows up as coolant loss and white exhaust smoke.

For fleets that prefer lower maintenance, an organic acid technology coolant removes the need for SCA top-up but still demands a two-year or 120,000 kilometre change, and the two chemistries must never be mixed because the deposits can plug the heater core. Between changes, the expansion tank level should be checked weekly and topped only with the same chemistry, never with tap water that introduces scale-forming minerals. A simple refractometer reading of freeze point also confirms the glycol strength has not been diluted by repeated top-ups, and a strength below thirty-five percent should be corrected before the hot season begins.

Coolant rule: test the SCA strip on every oil service for conventional coolant, and never mix inorganic and organic chemistries. Liner pitting from additive starvation is silent until the head gasket fails.

Water Pump and Thermostat Service

The X3s water pump is belt driven and sealed, and its first failure signal is a slow weep from the drain hole below the bearing, visible as a chalky streak on the housing. A pump that has reached this stage should be replaced rather than watched, because the next step is a seized bearing that throws the fan and alternator belts and overheats the engine within minutes. Bearing roughness heard as a rhythmic rumble at idle is the earlier warning that gives a workshop time to plan the change during a scheduled stop.

The thermostat sets the operating temperature and should open fully around 82 to 88 degrees Celsius, bypassing the radiator until the engine is warm and then holding temperature steady under load. A thermostat that sticks partly closed causes overheating only under hard work, while one that sticks open makes the engine run cold, raises fuel use and lets cylinder wear accelerate. Testing by dropping the unit in hot water and watching the valve lift is a five-minute bench check that prevents a misdiagnosis of the radiator when the real fault is the thermostat.

Drivers are the first line of defence, and a simple habit of glancing at the temperature gauge during the first ten minutes of each climb catches a lazy thermostat or a failing clutch before the warning lamp lights. Fleet supervisors should log any coolant-top-up event in the vehicle record, because a pattern of small losses points to a weeping hose or a tired cap rather than normal evaporation. Catching that trend early turns a roadside steam cloud into a scheduled hose change during a quiet shift, and keeps the X3s earning instead of stranded.

Fan Clutch Operation and Testing

Most X3s units use a viscous fan clutch that slips at low temperature to save fuel and locks up when the radiator outlet air crosses a threshold, driving the fan at full speed to pull maximum air. A clutch that never engages leaves the truck reliant on road speed for cooling and overheats on slow grade climbs, while one that never releases wastes horsepower and can over-cool the engine in cool weather. The quick roadside test is to listen for a rising fan roar as the engine warms under load and to feel for a temperature step across the radiator.

Workshops can confirm clutch health with a simple stall-speed check: with the engine at working temperature and the clutch engaged, the fan should resist hand rotation firmly, and a free-spinning fan with the engine hot means the silicone fluid has leaked out. Replacing the clutch is far cheaper than the head gasket it protects, and fleets in consistently hot sites should consider a heavier-duty clutch rated for continuous engagement rather than the standard unit tuned for mixed climate.

Fan clutch check: a hot engine with a free-spinning fan means the viscous coupling has lost its fluid. Replace the clutch before the next grade climb, not after the temperature warning lamp lights.

Tropical Uprating Recommendations

Fleets that run the X3s where ambient temperatures sit above 40 degrees for months need more than a clean radiator, and a few uprating steps pay back quickly. Fitting a higher-capacity radiator core with wider tubes raises the heat rejection margin, while a larger-capacity viscous clutch or an on-demand electric fan supplement covers the low-speed loading that defeats a belt-driven fan. Raising the coolant concentration slightly toward a fifty-five percent glycol mix extends the boil-over margin under the pressurised cap, within the limits set by the engine maker.

Secondary measures include re-routing the charge-air cooler to draw cleaner air, fitting a sun shade over the radiator during idle periods and specifying a coolant temperature alarm that warns the driver before damage occurs. Some operations also fit an auxiliary transmission oil cooler, because the Fast 12JSD gearbox shares heat load on long climbs and its oil temperature tracks engine cooling capacity. Each of these steps is modest in cost next to a single overheated-head rebuild.

Documentation matters as much as hardware. Fleets that record radiator clean dates, coolant test readings and pump change mileage build a trend line that predicts failure instead of reacting to it, and this data also supports warranty claims with the SAGMOTO export desk. A laminated cooling card in each cab, showing the correct coolant type and the weekly check steps, keeps new drivers from topping up with the wrong fluid on a remote site where the nearest supplier is hours away.

Regular core cleaning, correct coolant chemistry, prompt pump and thermostat attention and a tested fan clutch keep the Cummins or Weichai engine in its safe temperature band.

SAGMOTO X5 vs X6: Cost Per Tonne-Kilometre and Fleet Mix Economics

2026-11-09Comparison Guide9 min read

When a fleet manager places the SAGMOTO X5 next to the SAGMOTO X6, the question is not which truck is better engineered. Both are engineered correctly for their intended bands: the X6 at 210 to 245 hp on Cummins ISD power for medium duty, the X5 at 240 to 300 hp on Weichai WP7 and Cummins engines for medium-heavy regional work. The question is which truck produces the lower cost per tonne-kilometre on the specific routes the fleet runs, and at what fleet mix the answer changes. This article works the economics with real numbers, because in this segment the decision is financial before it is technical.

Setting Up the Cost Base

Cost per tonne-kilometre divides the total daily operating cost of a truck by the tonne-kilometres it produces: payload multiplied by distance loaded. To build the daily cost stack, we use representative export figures. An X6 rigid with a box or dropside body prices around USD 34,000 to 39,000 FOB Chinese port, and an X5 in comparable body trim prices around USD 42,000 to 48,000. Assume a five-year write-off over 450,000 km, which at 90,000 km per year is a realistic regional duty cycle. Financing at 8 percent over 48 months on 70 percent of the unit price produces daily capital charges of roughly USD 24 for the X6 and USD 30 for the X5 under a 300-day operating year.

Fuel is the dominant variable. At a delivered diesel price of USD 1.15 per litre, an X6 burning 20 litres per 100 km at distribution loads costs USD 23.00 per 100 km, while an X5 burning 24 litres per 100 km at its heavier loads costs USD 27.60. A driver wage of USD 45 per day, tyres budgeted at USD 0.012 per km for the X6 and USD 0.016 for the X5 (heavier axles and loads), maintenance at USD 0.020 and USD 0.026 per km respectively, and insurance plus administration at USD 12 per day complete the stack. The numbers that follow use these assumptions; substitute your own fuel price and wages and the method holds unchanged.

Productivity: The Payload Side of the Ratio

Daily cost alone favours the X6, but the ratio also depends on what each truck produces. A distribution-bodied X6 operating at 18 tonnes gross with a 10-tonne payload, running 400 km with a 65 percent loaded-kilometre ratio, delivers 2,600 tonne-kilometres per day. Its cost per tonne-kilometre is 185.80 divided by 2,600, or 9.1 US cents. A regional X5 operating at 28 tonnes gross with a 17-tonne payload on the same 400 km and same loaded ratio delivers 4,420 tonne-kilometres, giving 214.20 divided by 4,420, or 4.8 US cents per tonne-kilometre. On pure line-haul freight the heavier truck is dramatically cheaper per unit of work, which is why regional operators consolidate onto fewer, heavier units wherever volume allows.

The picture reverses as route length and drop density change. On a 120 km day with eight delivery drops, the X6's 65 percent loaded ratio falls to perhaps 55 percent and average speed drops, but its payload stays 10 tonnes and its daily cost falls to roughly USD 130 with reduced fuel burn. The X5 doing the same urban profile carries no more payload than the X6, because the goods are volume-limited, not weight-limited. It simply burns more fuel moving its heavier structure. In multi-drop volume freight, the X6's cost per tonne-kilometre typically lands 15 to 25 percent below the X5's.

The X5 wins the ratio when freight is weight-limited and routes are long; the X6 wins when freight is volume-limited and drops are many. Identify which limit binds your loads, and the model choice makes itself.

Break-Even Payload Analysis

There is a payload level at which the two trucks cost the same per tonne-kilometre on identical routes. Using the daily stacks above and a 400 km day, the X6 must deliver 185.80 divided by 0.048, about 3,871 tonne-kilometres, to match the X5's unit cost, which at 260 loaded kilometres requires a payload of about 14.9 tonnes. In other words, whenever the freight allows payloads above roughly 15 tonnes on a long route, the X5's cost advantage begins; below that threshold the X6 is the cheaper producer. This break-even payload is the single most useful number in the comparison, and every fleet should compute it against its own fuel price, wage levels and financing rates before ordering.

Note how sensitive the break-even is to fuel price. At USD 1.40 per litre the fuel lines rise 22 percent, the X5's total rises faster in absolute terms, and the break-even payload shifts to around 15.5 tonnes. At USD 0.90 per litre it falls toward 14.5 tonnes. High fuel prices slightly favor the smaller engine; cheap fuel slightly favors consolidating onto the heavier truck. Neither effect is large enough to reverse the fundamental weight-limited versus volume-limited logic, but it shifts the boundary by half a tonne of payload either way.

Fleet Mix Strategy: Running Both Models Together

Most real fleets do not run one model. A regional operator handling 1,200 tonnes of mixed freight weekly might run two X5 units on the two long trunk routes where loads hit 17 tonnes, and four X6 units on the secondary network where loads sit at 8 to 11 tonnes across many drops. The fleet mix question is how to allocate a marginal truck purchase. The rule from the break-even analysis is direct: estimate the average payload and route profile of the marginal duty, and if average payload exceeds about 15 tonnes over distances beyond 200 km per day, the marginal truck should be an X5; otherwise an X6 produces the work for less.

Mixing the two platforms also carries a shared-parts advantage that improves the blended economics. The Fast gearboxes share assemblies, the air brake and electrical systems are commonised, and driver training transfers between cabs. A two-model garage stocks one set of consumables covering roughly 60 percent of both fleets' fast-moving parts, which reduces inventory capital relative to running two unrelated brands. The Weichai WP7 and Cummins ISD engines remain distinct service lines, so workshop tooling and technician familiarity should be checked against the local support network before finalizing the mix.

Resale Value and Total-Life Economics

Cost per tonne-kilometre over the first life ignores resale, which partially offsets the X5's higher capital cost. Heavier trucks retain value in markets with active second-hand regional haulage demand, and a well-maintained five-year-old X5 with engine hours documented can recover 30 to 35 percent of its original FOB value. Medium-duty X6 units recover similar percentages where urban delivery demand is strong. The difference is not large enough to alter the operating economics, but it does mean the X5's true daily capital cost is modestly lower than the straight-line write-off used above, by roughly USD 2 to 3 per day across the holding period. Buyers financing through the exporter should also note that letters of credit and staged payment structures are available for multi-unit orders, which eases the cash-flow profile of a mixed-fleet purchase.

Compute your break-even payload before ordering. At representative costs it sits near 15 tonnes: above it the X5 produces cheaper tonne-kilometres, below it the X6 does, and no amount of specification debate changes that arithmetic.

Financing Structures and Their Effect on the Ratio

Financing terms shift the daily capital line enough to move the break-even payload by several hundred kilograms, so buyers should cost the payment structure alongside the truck. A cash purchase eliminates interest but ties up USD 34,000 to 48,000 per unit that could fund working capital elsewhere. A 48-month hire purchase at 8 percent on 70 percent financing, the structure used in the cost base above, adds roughly 9 percent to the total cost of the truck across the term. Exporters including Shaanxi Fenghan Trading Co., Ltd. accept 30 percent deposit with balance against shipping documents as standard, and letters of credit at sight for established importers; the letter adds bank charges of around USD 400 to 700 per shipment but strengthens the paper trail for local asset finance. Some markets offer leasing at 11 to 13 percent effective rates, which raises the X5's daily capital charge from USD 30 toward USD 36 and pushes the break-even payload up by roughly half a tonne. Whatever the structure, keep the daily-cost model current as terms change, because a ratio built on cash pricing will mislead a fleet buying on credit.

Running the Numbers on Your Own Routes

The method above transfers directly to any fleet: build the daily cost stack from capital, fuel, driver, tyres and maintenance; measure actual loaded kilometres and payloads from telematics or dispatch records; and divide.

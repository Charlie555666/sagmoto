Why Waste Collection Is the Perfect First Electric Fleet

Municipal waste collection is the duty cycle that electric trucks were practically invented for. Routes are fixed and daily, distances are short (25-60 km per route), trucks return to a single depot nightly, speeds are low with hundreds of stops, and the duty is brutally hard on diesel: a refuse truck's engine idles 50-65 percent of its shift while the compactor works and crews collect, burning 55-70 L/100km in effective terms with disproportionate wear. Cities also want the elimination for its own sake: zero tailpipe emissions and near-silent operation in residential streets at 5 AM, plus regulatory credit toward municipal climate commitments.

The SAGMOTO i9 — a 131 kWh LFP battery electric platform in the 18-tonne GVW class with up to roughly 350 kW peak drive power — is configured for exactly this mission. Within the SAGMOTO new energy electric truck lineup, the i9 pairs with body-builder refuse compactor units to create a purpose-built electric refuse collector.

The electric power takeoff (ePTO) is the decisive waste-collection technology: the compactor body runs directly on battery power instead of the diesel engine's PTO. This eliminates the 50-65 percent idle fuel burn entirely and removes the engine-hour accumulation that makes diesel refuse trucks the highest-maintenance vehicles in municipal fleets.

The Route Energy Budget

Routes running beyond 45 km or with unusually heavy compaction duty are split into two shifts with a 45-minute depot fast charge between them, using the i9's 120 kW DC charging capability.

Depot Charging Architecture for Sanitation Fleets

Sanitation depots are ideal charging sites: dedicated electrical service, overnight dwell of 8-10 hours, and predictable departure schedules. A practical 25-truck depot design:

  1. Twelve 60 kW DC dual-port chargers covering all 25 trucks overnight (2 A.M.-10 A.M. window, staggered)
  2. Two 120 kW DC fast chargers for midday turnaround and opportunity charging between shifts
  3. Load management software staggering charge sessions to keep depot peak demand within the existing service capacity, avoiding grid upgrade costs
  4. Roof-canopy solar option (150-250 kW) offsetting 25-35 percent of charging energy where municipal policy rewards visible renewable integration

Operational Considerations Specific to Refuse Duty

  • Route balancing: electric refuse fleets optimize route assignment by energy budget rather than tonnage alone; route software modules now include energy modeling for exactly this purpose.
  • Weight: the battery adds roughly 1.5-2 tonnes over diesel drivetrain mass, reducing legal payload by a corresponding margin — relevant in cities with strict axle-load enforcement; route tonnage planning absorbs it.
  • Water and washdown: battery enclosures are IP-rated against daily wash bay cleaning and compactor leachate exposure; annual HV insulation testing should be scheduled into depot maintenance programs.
  • Noise compliance advantages: near-silent collection enables early-morning collection windows in noise-restricted residential zones that diesel trucks cannot legally serve — a genuine service-level improvement, not just a cost item.
  • Crew ergonomics: elimination of diesel idle vibration and fumes at the collection position measurably improves crew working conditions and retention — a recurring finding in early municipal electric fleet deployments.

Operator Training and HV Safety Program

Municipal refuse crews rotate more than any fleet workforce — seasonal labor, contract changes, and roster churn mean that driver familiarity with electric systems cannot be assumed. A practical municipal HV familiarization program covers four modules, deliverable in half a day per crew: high-voltage safety basics and lockout procedures for wash and service contexts; daily pre-route checks specific to the i9 (charge state verification, thermal system status, ePTO function test); regenerative braking technique on greasy residential streets (where regen's different deceleration feel requires adjustment); and emergency response protocols for incident response teams who will encounter HV vehicles in service. Cities that deploy without this training see higher minor-incident rates in the first quarter; cities that train see smoother adoption curves and better first-year availability statistics to justify fleet expansion.

Technician development runs deeper: one depot-certified HV technician per 10-15 trucks, trained on insulated tooling, HV isolation verification, and battery-pack safe-handling procedures, covers the service depth that municipal in-house maintenance requires. Waste Composition and Duty Variations

Not all refuse duty is equal, and the i9's energy budget flexes with it. Standard municipal mixed waste at 300-500 kg/m3 runs the budget described above. Compacted transfer duty — hauling pre-compacted containers from transfer stations to landfill — runs heavier loads on longer (40-80 km) routes, consuming 110-125 kWh per shift and requiring either split shifts with midday charging or route assignment to the depot's closest-vehicle dispatch logic. Green waste and recycling routes run lighter and shorter, often leaving 50 percent charge unused — capacity that smart dispatch can harvest by assigning those trucks to a supplementary park-maintenance or street-cleaning duty cycle on the same charge. Fleet dispatch software that models per-route energy signatures converts this variability from a planning headache into a utilization gain.

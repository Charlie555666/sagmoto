E3 MAX Tractor Head Buying Guide: 460-520 hp Prime Mover Specs and Checklist

2026-11-10Buying Guide8 min read

The SAGMOTO E3 MAX is the heavy mixed duty platform in the range, offered with Weichai WP12 power between 460 and 520 horsepower and a peak torque of up to 2250 Newton metres. When specified as a tractor head it becomes a prime mover built for 40 to 60 tonne gross combination weights, bridging the gap between a value tractor and the flagship E1st or the Z3 tractor truck 520HP Cummins M13. For fleet buyers moving from a lighter 340 to 400 hp tractor, the E3 MAX closes the power gap on steep and heavy corridors without stepping up to the flagship price tier. This buying guide works as a specification and numbers checklist, so you can hand a complete, defensible build sheet to your export sales engineer rather than negotiating options after the chassis is already welded.

E3 MAX Tractor Head at a Glance

Start with the figures that decide everything else. The Weichai WP12 six cylinder engine produces 460 hp at the low end of the range and 520 hp at the top, with peak torque climbing to 2250 Nm between roughly 1000 and 1400 rpm. That flat torque shelf is what lets the tractor hold a gear on a long grade without the engine hunting for revs. The standard transmission is a twelve speed manual or the Fast automated manual variant, both rated well above the engine output, and the rear axle is a thirteen tonne single reduction or hub reduction unit depending on the final drive ratio you choose. Gross combination weight is rated up to sixty tonnes in the heavy freight configuration, which is the ceiling you design the rest of the spec around.

The Fast twelve speed gearbox deserves a word of its own. Its overdrive top gear keeps engine speed down on the highway while the low crawler ratio gives the tractor the launch torque it needs when a fully loaded trailer starts on an incline. Coupled with the thirteen tonne rear axle, the driveline is the same architecture used across the heavier SAGMOTO tractors, so spares commonality with your existing fleet is a real advantage rather than a marketing claim.

Fifth Wheel Rating and Coupling Geometry

The fifth wheel is the structural heart of any tractor head, and on the E3 MAX it should be specified to at least 50 tonnes static load to leave margin over a 40 to 60 tonne GCW duty cycle. Use a recognised brand coupling with a 90 millimetre king pin standard, and confirm the deck height matches the trailers already in your fleet so that the combination sits level and the king pin angle stays within tolerance. A mismatched fifth wheel height forces the trailer nose up or down, which wears tyres and stresses the coupling on every kilometre.

Decide between a fixed and a sliding fifth wheel before order. A sliding plate lets a single tractor serve both short regional boxes and long 40 foot containers by shifting the axle load, and it is worth the small cost if your fleet is mixed. Specify the air or manual release that your drivers are trained on, because a coupling system nobody trusts gets bypassed, and a bypassed safety latch is how king pins walk out on the highway.

Also confirm the lubrication method and the wear plate thickness at order, because a thin plate on a high mileage tractor needs replacement inside a year and the cost is trivial at the factory but annoying in the field. Ask for the greaseless or manual grease specification that matches your workshop habit.

Fifth Wheel Rule

Rate the coupling to at least 50 tonnes static load and match the deck height to your existing trailers, because a level combination protects tyres and king pin wear across the whole duty cycle.

Axle Ratios for 40-60 t GCW

Final drive ratio is the single biggest lever on fuel economy and grade performance for a tractor head. A numerically higher ratio, around 4.8 to 5.3, gives strong pulling for 50 to 60 tonne loads and steep terrain but raises engine rpm and fuel use on the flat. A lower ratio, around 3.7 to 4.1, suits 40 tonne regional work and long highway legs where cruise rpm matters more than crawl torque. Match the ratio to where the truck actually spends its time rather than to the worst single hill you might meet.

Do not overlook the overdrive top gear when reading these ratios. A 4.6 axle with an overdrive twelfth gear still cruises at a calm rpm on the flat, so you can choose a slightly taller axle for better pulling without hurting highway economy. The retarder and the gearbox share the engine control unit, so a ratio that holds the engine in its torque shelf also lets the retarder work at its most efficient speed.

Retarder Option and Downhill Safety

For any route with sustained descent, a retarder is not a comfort option but a safety system. The E3 MAX can be fitted with an engine brake or a hydraulic retarder, and for 50 to 60 tonne loads on long mountain grades the hydraulic unit is the correct choice because it absorbs brake heat without fading. Specify the retarder with a control stalk on the steering column and train drivers to engage it before the grade, not after the service brakes have already heated. A tractor that descends on its foundation brakes alone will fade, and fade on a loaded sixty tonne combination is a serious incident waiting to happen.

Pair the retarder with the standard dual circuit air brakes, ABS and ESC, and confirm the exhaust brake is also enabled as a first stage of speed control. The combination of exhaust brake, engine brake and hydraulic retarder gives three descending layers before the wheel brakes are needed, which is exactly how long haul fleets keep linings alive for a full season of mountain work.

Cab Choices for Long Haul and Regional Work

The E3 MAX tractor head is offered with a day cab and a sleeper cab, and the choice follows the duty cycle rather than the budget. A day cab is lighter, cheaper and ideal for regional shuttle work where the driver returns home nightly and the tractor never sleeps. A sleeper cab with a flat floor and bunk is the right call for long international corridors where rest rules require the driver to stay with the load, and the small weight penalty is recovered in driver retention on multi day routes.

Specify the cab with air suspension on the tractor variant, because an unsprung cab transmits every expansion joint to a tired driver and shortens the useful day. Add the heated and suspended seat, the larger windscreen and the roof hatch if you operate in dust or heat, and confirm the air conditioning capacity is sized for ambient temperatures at your destination rather than for the factory climate.

Fuel Tank, Tyres and Ride Specification

Tractor heads live or die on range between fuel stops, so size the tank to the corridor. A single 400 litre aluminium tank covers most regional work, while a 600 litre tank or twin tanks suit cross border long haul where fuel price and availability vary. Fit 315 or 295 section tyres on steel or aluminium rims rated for the steer and drive axle loads, and keep the drive tyres in a pattern that balances wear against fuel, because a tractor head's drive tyres are its largest recurring consumable after diesel.

Ride specification matters for uptime. Air suspension on the rear of the tractor smooths the fifth wheel load and protects fragile cargo, while leaf suspension is the rugged default for tipper and construction duty where ground clearance and simplicity beat comfort. Choose air if the E3 MAX will pull polished freight, and choose leaf if it will spend time on broken site roads where an air bag is one more thing to puncture.

Ratio Decision

Pick the final drive by the dominant duty, not the worst hill: a 3.7 to 4.1 ratio serves 40 tonne regional work, while 5.0 to 5.3 is the correct choice for 55 to 60 tonne steep corridors.

Confirm each item against the build sheet, because the cost of a missing option is tiny at the plant and large after customs clearance.

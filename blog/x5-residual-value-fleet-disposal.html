SAGMOTO X5 Residual Value: Planning Fleet Disposal Before You Buy

2026-11-18Market Analysis9 min read

Fleet managers spend weeks negotiating a purchase price and almost no time planning the exit. Yet on a five-year holding cycle the resale value is often 25 to 35 percent of the total cost of ownership, and it is the one cost line that can be improved by decisions taken before the truck is even delivered. A SAGMOTO X5 bought with the right specification, documented properly and disposed of at the right moment can return several thousand dollars more than an identical truck bought cheaply and run without records.

The X5 is the 4x2 medium platform in the range: Cummins ISD270E5 power around 270 horsepower, a six-speed transmission, a gross weight range around 16 to 18 tonnes, and a chassis that serves distribution, municipal and light construction work. It is a high-utilisation, high-mileage vehicle in most fleets, which means its condition at disposal is decided by maintenance discipline rather than by luck.

Key point: Residual value is built, not found. Specification choices, service records, body condition and disposal timing together move resale by 15 to 25 percent, which on a USD 30,000 truck is USD 4,500 to USD 7,500 — more than most purchase negotiations achieve.

How a truck actually depreciates

Truck depreciation is not linear. The first year takes the largest share, typically 20 to 28 percent, because the vehicle loses its new status immediately. Years two and three are gentler, at 8 to 12 percent each, provided the truck is maintained and the mileage is normal. Years four and five flatten out for a well-kept truck, and then fall sharply once the odometer passes a threshold that buyers in that market consider high.

The spread within each year is wide, and that spread is what a fleet controls. Two identical X5s at year five can differ by 13 percentage points of original value — USD 3,900 on a USD 30,000 truck — purely on condition and records. That is the number worth managing.

The specification choices that hold value

Some specifications age well and some do not. A standard wheelbase, a common body type, a widely supported engine and a popular colour will always be easier to sell than a specialist configuration. A truck built for one narrow contract — an unusual body, an uncommon axle ratio, a distinctive livery — is worth more to that contract and less to everyone else.

The practical rule is to buy the specification that the local second-hand market understands, unless the truck will be held for its full life. Common configurations sell in weeks; unusual ones sit for months and are eventually discounted. Fleets that plan to dispose at year four or five should resist the temptation to over-specify, because the buyer at disposal is paying for usability, not for the features that suited one route.

Key point: Buy the configuration your local resale market understands. A common wheelbase, body and axle ratio sells in weeks at year five; a specialist specification sells in months and at a discount, whatever it was worth to you.

Documentation is worth real money

In every used truck market, a documented truck outsells an undocumented one at the same condition. The documentation that matters is specific: full service records with dates and odometer readings, parts invoices for major repairs, accident and insurance history, tyre and brake replacement records, and a record of any body or chassis repairs. A buyer will pay a premium for a truck he can verify, because the alternative is a truck he has to assume the worst about.

The cheapest way to build that record is to keep it from day one, in one place, in a format a buyer can read. A folder of invoices is adequate; a spreadsheet summary plus the invoices is better; photographs of the chassis at delivery and at each year are better still. Fleets that start collecting records at the point of sale are already three years late.

Buyers inspect in a predictable order, and each item has a negotiable value attached. Understanding that order lets a fleet spend its refurbishment budget where it returns the most.

The pattern is that cosmetic items — cab interior, paint, a clean engine bay — return more per dollar spent than mechanical repairs, because buyers price mechanical work conservatively but respond strongly to presentation. That is not an argument for hiding faults; it is an argument for spending the refurbishment budget on presentation and on documented mechanical work rather than on a partial engine repair that raises more questions than it answers.

Timing the disposal

Timing is worth as much as condition. Disposing before a major service or before a tyre replacement avoids spending money that the buyer will not pay for. Disposing a few months before the warranty on major components expires captures the value of remaining coverage. Disposing into a strong seasonal market — before a construction season or a harvest — can add 5 to 8 percent.

There is also a threshold effect on mileage. In most markets a truck that sells at 380,000 km attracts a different category of buyer from one at 420,000 km, even though the condition may be identical. Knowing where that threshold sits in your market and disposing just below it is free money. Ask three dealers what odometer reading changes their buying interest, and the answer will be consistent.

Key point: Dispose before the spend, not after. Selling ahead of a major service, a tyre set or a warranty expiry avoids costs the buyer will not reimburse, and staying just below the market's mileage threshold protects the price.

Refurbish or sell as-is

Refurbishment is worth it when the cost is less than half the price uplift and the work can be done without extending the holding period by more than a month. Paint and interior work usually meets that test; engine overhauls usually do not, because the uplift is capped by what the market will pay for the model regardless of condition. A rebuilt engine in a five-year-old truck raises the price less than the rebuild costs, in most markets.

The exception is a truck with an unusual specification that has a known buyer. If a specific customer needs that configuration, a mechanical refurbishment can be justified because the alternative is a long sale process at a discount. That is a relationship decision rather than a market decision, and it should be made deliberately rather than by default.

Disposal channels and the export option

Most fleets default to a local trade-in because it is convenient, and pay for the convenience. A direct sale to an end user typically returns 8 to 15 percent more than a trade-in, at the cost of some selling effort and a longer timeline. An export buyer in a market where the model is well supported may pay more than either, particularly for trucks with mechanical condition that local buyers consider high-mileage but which remains serviceable for years.

The export route requires documentation that local sales do not: a clean title, export-compliant paperwork, and often a technical inspection. Fleets planning to dispose through export should confirm the paperwork requirements at the start of ownership rather than at the end. Mileage bands and the buyers they attract

Used truck buyers sort themselves by odometer band, and each band pays differently. Below 200,000 km the buyer is usually a fleet expanding and looking for a nearly new unit; above 500,000 km the buyer is usually a small operator or an exporter looking for a working truck at a low entry price. The middle band, 250,000 to 400,000 km, is where most fleet disposals land and where the widest spread in achieved price occurs.

That spread is explained by condition and records rather than by mileage. Two X5s at 350,000 km can differ by 20 percent in achieved price, and the difference is almost entirely about what a buyer can verify. A truck with a full service file, recent tyres and a clean chassis attracts the expanding-fleet buyer; the same truck without records attracts only the low-price buyer. The fleet that keeps records literally sells to a better class of customer.

Key point: The same truck at the same mileage sells in two different markets depending on documentation. Records do not just raise the price; they change who bids.

Depreciation, tax and the accounting view

Residual value also has a tax dimension that fleets often ignore. In many markets the depreciation schedule allowed by the tax authority differs from the actual market depreciation, and the difference creates either a tax shield or a taxable gain on disposal. A truck written down faster than it depreciates produces a book gain on sale, which may be taxable; a truck written down more slowly produces a loss that may be deductible.

The practical implication is to align the disposal decision with the tax position rather than treat them separately. Selling in the year when a book loss offsets other income can be worth more than holding for a slightly better price the following year. That is a conversation for the fleet's accountant, but it should happen before the disposal decision, not after.

Conclusion

Residual value on an X5 is decided by four things a fleet controls: buying a configuration the market understands, documenting everything from day one, spending refurbishment money on presentation and verified mechanical work rather than on partial repairs, and disposing at the right moment rather than when the truck is worn out. The difference between a well-managed disposal and a default trade-in is typically 15 to 25 percent of original value. On a fleet of twenty medium trucks that is a six-figure number, and it is available without buying a single better truck.

SAGMOTO X6 8×4 Heavy Chassis as Mobile Lube & Fuel Service Truck

2026-10-22Industry Application11 min read

At a mine, quarry or remote construction site, equipment downtime is measured in dollars per minute. Haul trucks, excavators and drills cannot drive to a service bay—the service bay must come to them. That is the job of the mobile lube and fuel unit, and the SAGMOTO dump truck models 6x4 8x4 chassis family, specifically the X6, provides an ideal heavy, rugged base for this conversion. With a 31–40 tonne GVWR envelope, a 350–430 HP Weichai WP10 or WP12 engine and an 8×4 configuration option, the X6 carries the tanks, pumps and reels a field service truck needs while surviving the same rough ground as the machines it serves.

Why the X6 Is a Strong Mobile-Service Base

A mobile lube unit is a rolling workshop. It carries engine oil, hydraulic oil, grease, coolant, diesel for refuelling, a compressor, hose reels, a generator and often a small crane or reel handler. All of that is weight and all of it must stay stable on sloping, uneven pit floors. The X6's heavy-duty chassis and available 8×4 layout spread that mass across four driven or paired axles, keeping individual axle loads legal while giving the body builder a long, flat frame to mount compartments.

Choose the 8×4 X6 for full-service units carrying both lube oils and a diesel bowser. The extra axle group absorbs tank tare and product weight so the unit stays within road and site axle limits.

Powertrain and Site Mobility

The X6 runs the Weichai WP10 (350–375 HP) or WP12 (375–430 HP) engine with a manual or automated transmission and, in the 8×4 version, a multi-axle drive set suited to soft, rutted pit floors. On a mine site the unit rarely exceeds 40 km/h, but it must climb out of pits, cross graded haul roads and hold steady while a technician works alongside a spinning excavator.

Specify an engine or transmission power-take-off (PTO) rather than a separate gen-set for pumping. A direct PTO runs the reels and bowser pump efficiently and removes a fuel-burning subsystem from a dangerous-goods vehicle.

Tank and Pump Layout

Body builders mount product tanks low and centred to keep the centre of gravity down. Reel cabinets sit at the rear or sides with gravity-friendly hose routing. A divider separates flammable diesel from non-flammable oils, and each compartment has its own level gauge, lockable fill and dispensing point. The pump bank—often peristaltic or gear pumps for oils and a centrifugal or rotary vane for diesel—draws from the PTO so the unit can service two machines at once.

Fire Safety and Static Grounding

Diesel, engine oil and grease are combustible; a spark or static discharge near a fill point can ignite vapour. The X6 mobile unit must therefore carry a fire-suppression kit, grounded reel connections and bonding clamps. Before any dispensing, the operator bonds the unit to the receiving machine with a certified cable so static equalises. The chassis itself is grounded through conductive straps at each axle where site rules require it.

Never dispense diesel or oil into a machine that is not bonded to the service unit. Static potential between a rolling tyre and the ground can arc at the nozzle and ignite vapour.

Dangerous-Goods Transport Compliance

Where the unit travels on public roads between sites, the diesel and certain additives fall under dangerous-goods regulations. That triggers placarding, driver certification, compartment labelling and documentation—even though the vehicle's primary role is on-site service. Plan the conversion with the relevant ADR / local equivalent in mind: properly rated tanks, emergency shut-offs, spill kit and a documented load manifest carried in the cab.

Operator Training

A mobile lube technician is also a dangerous-goods handler. Training covers bonding procedure, spill response, fire-fighting, compartment identification and the site's radio protocol for working near live equipment. The X6's cab should carry the manifest and the emergency response sheet where the driver can reach it without entering the danger zone.

Climate and Terrain Adaptation

In desert operations, tank shading and cooling of the pump compartment prevent vapour pressure buildup; in tropical sites, humidity control and weekly water-drain from air receivers keep the compressor healthy. Cold-region mines need heated compartments or low-pour oils so the unit can still service equipment at minus temperatures. The X6 chassis itself handles the grade and surface; the body builder handles the climate envelope.

Specifying Your X6 Service Unit

For a pure lube-and-grease unit without a diesel bowser, a 6×4 X6 at the lower GVWR is sufficient and more nimble in tight pits. For a combined lube-plus-fuel truck serving a large shovel and truck fleet, the 8×4 X6 at 37–40 t GVWR with WP12 430 HP and a PTO pump bank is the correct spec. Confirm the body builder's frame mounting points, request the dangerous-goods-rated tanks if any road leg is involved, and standardise the safety kit across your fleet.

Maintaining the Service Unit Itself

A mobile lube truck only earns while it rolls, so its own service life matters. The X6 chassis follows the standard heavy-truck schedule—but the body and pumps need their own plan. Pumps are greased at the PTO coupling, hose reels are inspected for chafe and kink, and the diesel bowser's internal coating is checked for corrosion at each annual tank test. The generator or PTO driveline is tension-checked, and the electrical system that runs the dispensing meters is tested under load so a faulty reel does not mis-deliver product into a machine.

Treat the dispensing meter as a calibrated instrument. A mis-reading meter either short-changes the operator or over-services a machine—both are contract breaches. Calibrate per the site's measurement policy.

Spill Kit and Secondary Containment

Every mobile unit carries a spill kit sized to the largest single compartment: absorbent pads, a drain seal, a shovel and labelled waste bags. The reel and fill areas sit over a containment tray so a faulty connection drips into capture, not onto the ground. At the end of shift the trays are emptied and the kit restocked—a five-minute habit that keeps the unit compliant on any site with an environmental audit, and that protects the operator from a reportable release.

Deployment Planning and Shift Model

Mine sites run the service unit on a planned route: a pre-dawn fill of all parked equipment before the production shift, a mid-day top-up loop, and an evening service of machines due for scheduled lube. The X6's 8×4 stability lets it work on graded pit floors beside operating shovels, and its PTO-driven pumps service two machines at once through separate reels. A typical shift services 12–20 machines, and the unit returns to the workshop for restock rather than the product being hand-carried across the pit in hazardous totes.

Coordination With the Pit Controller

The service unit is a moving hazard near live equipment, so it operates under the pit controller's radio authority. The driver requests a window, positions only when the machine is stopped and braked, and withdraws before the machine resumes. This coordination, baked into the site's safe-work system, is why the X6's compact 8×4 footprint—easier to place than a longer heavy truck—pays off in daily practice, turning a potential obstruction into a seamless support function.

The X6 turns a heavy, durable truck chassis into a self-contained service station that meets the machines where they work. Done right—with correct axle loading, PTO-driven pumps, bonding discipline and dangerous-goods compliance—it keeps the pit running and the downtime clock stopped.

Operator training and shift handover

A mobile lubrication unit is only as disciplined as its operator. Build the daily routine around four checks that take less than ten minutes: confirm the product labels on each compartment match the delivery ticket, verify the meter seal is intact, drain the water trap at the bottom of each tank, and log the opening and closing meter readings. Those four steps prevent almost every costly mistake — cross-contamination, unmetered product, water in a gearbox, and inventory drift that nobody can reconcile at month end.

Shift handover should transfer three numbers: litres dispensed per compartment, litres remaining, and any compartment that shows a discrepancy above one percent. On sites running two shifts, a shared logbook or a simple spreadsheet is enough. Sites running three shifts across a large pit usually move to a telematics-linked flow meter so the dispensed volume reconciles automatically against the equipment hour meter. When a wheel loader reports 450 hours between oil changes but the lube truck shows only 380 hours of product dispensed, you have a leak, a meter fault, or an unrecorded top-up — all worth chasing before the warranty claim arrives.

Train operators to treat the meter reading as a financial record, not a convenience. A one percent unexplained variance on a truck dispensing 40,000 litres a year is 400 litres of product that leaves the books with no equipment to show for it.

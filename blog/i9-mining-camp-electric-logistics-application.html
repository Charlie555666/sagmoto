SAGMOTO i9 Electric Truck at Remote Mining Camps: Personnel Supply and Light Haulage

2026-10-13Industry Application11 min read

The quiet truck inside the lease area

A remote mining camp runs on movement: crews to the shift change, food and water to the kitchen, parts to the workshop, refuse out, samples to the lab. Most of that movement is short, repeated, and contained inside the camp lease area or on the access road to the nearest town, and almost none of it needs a diesel truck. That is exactly the gap the SAGMOTO new energy electric trucks fill, and the i9 is the model built for it. The i9 is a battery-electric truck with a 131 kWh lithium-iron-phosphate battery pack and a real-world range of 250 to 320 km, and within a camp that range is more than a full shift of personnel supply and light haulage.

The case for an electric truck at a mine is not only emissions, though zero tailpipe emission matters in a confined camp. It is noise, it is the removal of diesel and exhaust from the crew transport and the kitchen supply, it is the simplicity of an electric drivetrain in a dusty, isolated place, and it is the fact that a camp already generates power, so charging is a planning problem rather than a fuel-delivery problem. This guide covers the i9 in the camp logistics role: enclosed cargo runs, personnel supply, charging at the camp power station, and range planning with the 131 kWh pack.

The i9 specification for camp logistics

The i9 is a chassis built around a 131 kWh LFP battery and an electric drive axle, configured for enclosed box or personnel bodies rather than heavy tipper work. LFP chemistry is the right choice for a remote site because it is tolerant of temperature variation, has a long cycle life, and is stable under the charge-and-discharge pattern a camp truck sees. The 250 to 320 km range is the usable figure at camp loads, not a brochure maximum, and it is enough for the duty because camp logistics is short-radius.

The electric drive axle removes the gearbox, clutch, exhaust and most of the fluids a diesel truck needs, which is a major advantage in a camp where the nearest dealer is hours away. Fewer failure points means the in-house workshop can keep the i9 running with basic electrical and brake competence rather than a diesel mechanic.

Enclosed cargo runs inside the lease area

The dominant i9 duty is the enclosed cargo run: moving food, water, spares, tools, laundry, medical supplies and samples between the camp hub and the work fronts, the airstrip, the core yard and the gate. An enclosed body protects the load from dust and weather, which matters for food and for laboratory samples, and the electric drivetrain means the load arrives without diesel smell from a tailpipe a metre behind the box. For a camp that handles sensitive samples or food for hundreds of personnel, that clean-delivery property is a real operational benefit, not a marketing one.

Typical camp cargo runs are 2 to 15 km each way, repeated 10 to 30 times per shift. At that radius the i9 uses a small fraction of its 250 to 320 km range per shift even with a full box, and the constraint is loading time and stops, not battery. A single i9 can cover the daily camp cargo circuit on one charge with margin to spare, and a second unit on rotation covers maintenance or peak demand.

Personnel supply and shift change

The second i9 role is moving personnel: the shift-change bus from the camp to the pit or the plant, the run to the airstrip for fly-in fly-out crews, and the local trip to the nearest town for errands. An electric personnel carrier is quiet at the low speeds of a camp internal road, produces no exhaust in the queue at the gate, and is cheap to run per seat-kilometre compared with a diesel bus on the same short loop. The 250 to 320 km range covers multiple shift-change cycles in a day, and because the runs are short the battery state of charge stays in a healthy mid band rather than cycling to empty.

Key point: Camp logistics is short-radius by nature, so the i9's 250-320 km range is not a limit, it is headroom. The truck finishes a full shift of cargo and crew runs with charge to spare; the planning question is where to plug in overnight, not whether it will make the round trip.

Charging at the camp power station

A remote mine already produces power: a generator set, a solar-hybrid plant, or a grid connection at the camp. The i9 charges from that same source, which turns charging from a fuel-delivery problem into a scheduling problem. The practical setup is an AC charger at the camp workshop for overnight top-up and, where the camp has the capacity, a DC charger for a mid-shift fast charge if a unit is needed back on the road quickly.

The 131 kWh pack is modest by heavy-truck standards, which is an advantage for a camp: it draws a manageable current that the camp electrical system can supply without a dedicated substation. A full overnight AC charge from a low state of charge takes roughly six to ten hours, which fits the camp's off-peak window, and a DC charge to 80 percent can be done in well under two hours if the power station has headroom. The key planning step is to size the charger to the camp supply and to place it where the truck can be parked securely and connected without blocking the workshop.

A mine that runs a tight generator margin at peak will schedule the i9 charge in the off-peak window; a mine with a solar-hybrid plant will charge through the daylight surplus. Either way the planning is simple, because the pack is small and the daily energy need is a fraction of camp consumption.

Range planning with 131 kWh

Range planning for the i9 is straightforward because the duty is predictable. Camp cargo and crew runs are short, repeated and at low average speed, which is the condition in which an electric truck is most efficient. The 250 to 320 km figure already accounts for camp loads and the stop-start pattern; a fleet should plan to a conservative 200 to 260 km usable per shift to hold a reserve for unexpected trips, and to charge before the pack drops below 20 percent to protect battery life.

  • Map the circuit. Total the daily camp kilometres across all runs; for most camps this is 80 to 180 km per unit, well inside the range.
  • Hold a reserve. Plan to return with 20 to 30 percent state of charge, leaving margin for an unscheduled medical or repair run.
  • Charge on a fixed window. Plug in at the same off-peak time each day so the truck is full before the first shift run.
  • Watch the load. A fully loaded box and a full personnel load both reduce range; the i9 telemetry shows the trend and the driver adjusts the circuit.
  • Climate effect. Cab heating in a cold camp and cooling in a hot camp draw from the pack; the LFP chemistry tolerates this better than other lithium types, but the planner budgets for it.
Key point: Plan the i9 to a 200-260 km usable shift range, not the 320 km ceiling. Holding a 20-30 percent reserve covers unscheduled camp runs and protects the LFP pack; the range is never the limiting factor on camp duty.

Noise, emission and camp-safety advantages

The operational advantages of the i9 go beyond energy. At low speed the truck is near-silent, which changes the camp: a quiet personnel carrier at shift change does not add to the noise load of an already loud site, and a near-silent cargo run past sleeping quarters at night is tolerated where a diesel idling past would not be. Zero tailpipe emission removes diesel exhaust from the crew transport and the kitchen supply route, which is a recognised health benefit in confined camp spaces, and the absence of a hot exhaust and fuel line reduces the fire-risk profile on the camp internal road.

For mines with a low-carbon commitment, the i9 is the visible, defensible first step: a zero-emission vehicle doing real work inside the lease area, charged from a camp power station that may itself be moving toward solar or hybrid. It is not a publicity item; it is a logistics unit that happens to be clean, and that combination is why camp managers are adopting it ahead of the heavy haul fleet.

Durability and maintenance in a remote camp

The i9's maintenance load is lower than a diesel's because the drivetrain has fewer parts: no engine oil, no clutch, no gearbox, no exhaust aftertreatment. The camp workshop maintains the brakes, the tyres, the suspension, the body and the high-voltage system to a defined safety standard, and it monitors battery state of health through the truck's telemetry. The LFP pack is chosen for exactly this duty because it tolerates the charge pattern and the temperature swing of a remote site better than alternatives, and its long cycle life means the battery is a capital item that outlasts the truck's first ownership period rather than a consumable.

The one item a camp must get right is the high-voltage safety standard: trained personnel, isolation procedure, and a defined response for any incident. That is a one-time competency to build, and once built it applies to every electric unit the camp adds. The i9 is simple enough that the standard is achievable for an in-house mine workshop, which is the point: the truck has to be serviceable where it works, not just where the dealer is.

Conclusion

The i9 electric truck is the right tool for the work that stays inside or near a remote mining camp: enclosed cargo runs, personnel supply at shift change, and light haulage that never needs a diesel's range or a tipper's capacity. Its 131 kWh LFP battery delivers 250 to 320 km of real-world range, which is headroom rather than a limit on the short, repeated camp circuit, and it charges from the camp power station the mine already runs, turning refuelling into a scheduling task. With near-silent low-speed operation, zero tailpipe emission on the crew and kitchen routes, a simple electric drivetrain that an in-house workshop can maintain, and an LFP pack built for the site's temperature and charge pattern, the i9 is a practical, clean logistics unit rather than an experiment. Camp managers should plan it to a 200 to 260 km usable shift with a reserve, charge it on the camp off-peak window, and build the one-time high-voltage safety competency that unlocks the whole electric fleet.

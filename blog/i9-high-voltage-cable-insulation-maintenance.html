SAGMOTO i9 High-Voltage Cable and Insulation Maintenance: Inspection Intervals and IR Testing

2026-10-19Maintenance & After-sales10 min read

The HV harness is the nervous system, and it ages quietly

A battery-electric truck has far fewer moving parts than a diesel, but the high-voltage harness that links the traction battery, inverter, motor, DC-DC converter and on-board charger is a component that degrades with heat, moisture, vibration and time. Unlike an engine fault, which usually announces itself with noise or smoke, insulation breakdown is silent until it becomes a fault code, a ground fault or a thermal event. The i9 high-voltage system runs in the 500 to 600 V DC band, with a permanent magnet synchronous motor rated at 167 kW peak and 120 kW continuous, and 1,100 Nm of torque from standstill, drawing on LFP packs of 96, 246 or 350 kWh. At those voltages, insulation integrity is not a maintenance nicety; it is the boundary between a safe vehicle and a dangerous one.

This guide covers high-voltage cable and insulation maintenance for the i9: how the harness is routed and marked, how to test insulation resistance with a megohmmeter, what connector sealing and ingress protection require, how heat, humidity and salt spray age the insulation, where vibration wear occurs and how to restrain it, the inspection intervals that keep the system healthy, and the prohibited actions and PPE that keep technicians alive. It complements the broader safety procedure documentation and assumes the full de-energisation sequence is completed before any HV component is touched.

HV cable routing and orange-label identification

The i9 high-voltage cables are colour-coded orange throughout, from the battery pack to the inverter, motor and auxiliary converters, following the international convention for dangerous voltage. That colour is a warning, not a guarantee: a technician must still verify zero potential with a proven meter before touching, because colour coding can be applied inconsistently after repair and because a cable can be live even when its sheath looks unremarkable.

The harness is routed to keep it away from sharp edges, moving parts, hot exhaust and abrasive surfaces, and it is clipped to the structure at defined intervals. The battery-to-inverter run carries the highest current and is the most thermally and mechanically stressed; the motor phase cables carry AC at the inverter output and demand careful separation from signal wiring to avoid interference; the DC-DC and charger circuits are lower current but still hazardous. Every orange cable should be traceable end to end, and any repair must restore the original routing, support spacing and separation, not simply splice the conductors together.

Key point: Orange means danger, not de-energised. Before any HV cable work, complete the full isolation procedure and verify zero potential with a meter proven live immediately beforehand. A visual check of the sheath is never a substitute.

Insulation resistance testing with a megohmmeter

Insulation resistance is the single most useful preventive measurement on a high-voltage harness. It is measured with a megohmmeter, often called a megger, applying a DC test voltage between the conductor and the chassis ground and reading the resistance in megaohms. For the i9's 500 to 600 V system, a 1,000 V DC insulation tester is the correct class of instrument; a lower-voltage tester will not stress the insulation sufficiently to reveal impending faults.

The pass threshold depends on the standard applied, but a widely used automotive electric-vehicle criterion requires insulation resistance well above 500 ohms per volt of system voltage, which for a 600 V system means a minimum of roughly 300 kiloohms. In practice, healthy i9 harnesses measure in the hundreds of megaohms, so the working criterion fleets should adopt is a measured insulation resistance of 500 MΩ or higher at 1,000 V DC and 20 degrees Celsius, with any reading below that treated as a fault to be isolated and investigated. Temperature matters: readings fall as temperature rises, so always record the temperature and trend the corrected value rather than judging a single number in isolation.

The discipline that matters is trending. A single pass reading tells you little; a reading that declines from 800 MΩ to 600 MΩ to 420 MΩ over three services tells you moisture is entering a connector or insulation is degrading long before it becomes a roadside ground fault. Record every measurement with its temperature and location so the fleet can act during planned maintenance instead of after a failure.

Key point: Trend insulation resistance, do not just pass or fail it. A steadily declining megohm reading at a connector is the earliest warning of moisture ingress, and acting on it prevents a roadside isolation fault.

Connector sealing and ingress protection

High-voltage connectors are sealed to an IP rating, typically IP67 or better, to keep water and dust out of the contact cavity. The seal is a consumable item: it ages, it is damaged by improper removal, and it fails if a connector is mated without its sealing face clean. In coastal, humid or saline environments the ingress risk is highest, and a single compromised seal can introduce moisture that slowly reduces insulation resistance and eventually causes a ground fault.

Maintenance practice should require that every HV connector is inspected for its sealing faces at each service, that connectors are never opened without reason, and that any connector that is opened is re-mated with a new seal and a checked torque on its locking ring. Dielectric grease applied only where the service literature permits protects contacts without trapping moisture; petroleum-based grease that is not rated for the application must never be used because it attacks the seal material.

Ageing under heat, humidity and salt spray

Three environmental factors accelerate HV insulation ageing. Heat, from the pack and from underfloor routing near the road surface, accelerates the chemical breakdown of the polymer sheath and reduces its flexibility. Humidity drives moisture into any micro-crack or compromised seal. Salt spray, whether from winter road treatment or a coastal depot, combines with moisture to encourage tracking paths across the insulation surface and to corrode connector shells and terminals.

Fleets operating i9 units in these conditions should shorten the inspection interval for the HV harness, pay particular attention to the lowest points of the routing where water collects, and consider additional underbody wash-down discipline in winter to remove salt before it attacks seals and terminals. The insulation monitoring device continuously measures isolation resistance while the vehicle is energised, and its stored values are the first diagnostic reference during fault investigation; a fleet that reviews these values at every service catches problems early.

Key point: Heat, humidity and salt spray are the three enemies of HV insulation. In any of those environments, shorten the harness inspection interval and review the insulation monitoring device values at every service rather than waiting for a fault code.

Vibration wear points and fixing clamps

The i9 harness is subject to continuous vibration from the road and the powertrain, and insulation fails first where the cable rubs. The highest-risk points are where the harness passes through bulkheads, where it runs close to sharp frame edges, where it is clamped too tightly or not at all, and where it flexes at the motor end as the suspension moves. A cable that is free to chafe against a frame rail will eventually wear through its outer sheath and then its insulation.

The control is correct support. Clamps and clips should hold the harness clear of sharp edges, with grommets or edge protectors wherever it passes through metal, and with enough freedom to move slightly without abrasion. After any underneath work, the harness should be returned to its original clips; a cable left dangling after a repair is a future ground fault. Inspect the support points at every service and replace any missing or chafed grommet immediately.

Prohibited actions and PPE

Never: work on orange cabling without completing the full de-energisation and personal lockout procedure; probe HV terminals with a non-rated meter; use petroleum grease not approved for the connector; repair an HV cable by a field splice that does not meet the rated specification; or re-mate a connector without a new seal. Each of these converts a manageable maintenance task into a life-safety risk.

Technicians performing HV harness work must wear Class 0 insulating gloves or higher for the system voltage, inspected before each use and protected by leather over-gloves, with a face shield, insulated tools rated for the working voltage, insulating matting underfoot, and a verified CAT III 1,000 V multimeter with a proving unit. Work should follow the two-person rule for all HV tasks so that rescue is possible if a shock occurs. These are not optional comforts; at 600 V DC the available fault current is high enough that a single mistake is irreversible.

Conclusion

High-voltage cable and insulation maintenance on the i9 is less about fixing faults and more about preventing them. The harness ages quietly under heat, humidity, salt and vibration, and the failures it produces are silent until they are serious. The defence is a routine: identify the orange circuits and verify zero potential before touching, measure insulation resistance at 1,000 V DC and trend it against a 500 MΩ floor, keep every connector seal intact and IP-rated, protect the harness from chafing with correct clamps and grommets, and shorten the interval in harsh environments.

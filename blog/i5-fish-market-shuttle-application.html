SAGMOTO i5 Electric Shuttle for Fish Markets and Port Cold Chains

2026-11-17Industry Application8 min read

A fish market wakes long before the city does, and the vehicles that serve it have to be loading in the dark while the catch is still cold. That pattern suits a battery-electric van better than almost any other commercial duty, because the truck returns to the same depot every day and covers a predictable distance at low speed. The SAGMOTO i5 is a 4.5 to 5.5 tonne light electric vehicle with a 90 to 120 kilowatt motor, a single-speed reduction drive and an LFP battery pack of 66 to 100 kilowatt-hours, giving roughly 200 kilometres of real range. This article examines the insulated body, the choice between ice and eutectic plates, the morning duty cycle, the corrosion risk from brine, and the payload and noise trade-offs that matter in a port cold chain.

Insulated body design for a chilled catch

A fish shuttle body is a cold store on wheels, and its job is to hold temperature rather than to make cold. The insulated box should be built with a closed-cell foam core of 60 to 80 millimetres, a food-safe internal liner and a sealed door system with quality gaskets, because the biggest source of heat gain is usually a leaking door rather than conduction through the panels. The i5 chassis gives a flat, low platform that makes a well-insulated body easier to fit, and the low floor height reduces the step up when handlers load heavy crates from the quay.

Internal finish matters more on fish than on dry goods. The liner should be smooth, washable and resistant to staining, with coved corners where the wall meets the floor so that a hose can clean without a manual scrub. A drain at the low point is essential, since melted ice and meltwater will collect on every trip and standing water inside an insulated box quickly becomes a hygiene problem. Body builders should also allow for a partition so that chilled and frozen product, or different species, can travel without cross-contamination.

A temperature logger is worth fitting to the box, because fish buyers and regulators increasingly ask for a record that shows the load stayed within the chilled band from quay to market. A small battery-powered probe in the return air stream gives a trace that can be downloaded at the depot, and it turns a claim about cold-chain integrity into evidence. On an electric shuttle the logger can run from the vehicle supply and send an alert if a door is left open, which protects both the product and the battery budget by prompting the driver to close up and move on.

Ice, eutectic plates or active refrigeration

Three approaches are common for holding a fish load cold on a short urban route. Crushed ice is cheap and familiar, but it consumes payload, melts into water that must be drained and gives only a few hours of cooling in warm weather. Eutectic plates are sealed panels filled with a phase-change fluid that is frozen overnight at the depot and then slowly releases cold during the delivery run, offering a consistent temperature with no refrigerant machinery on the vehicle. Active electric refrigeration gives the tightest control and can be plugged into the depot supply while parked, but it draws from the traction battery and reduces the range available for driving.

For an i5 doing a pre-dawn market run of four to six hours, eutectic plates are often the best balance. They shift the energy cost to overnight charging when tariffs are low, they have no moving parts to fail on the road and they avoid the weight of a compressor and condenser on the vehicle. The trade-off is that the plates must be frozen solid before every shift, which means the depot needs enough refrigerated rack space and a disciplined process to rotate the panels. Operators who cannot guarantee that turnaround should specify a small active unit instead.

Is 200 kilometres of range enough for a port route

The quoted 200 kilometre range for the i5 is measured under a light-duty cycle, and a refrigerated fish shuttle should plan against a more conservative figure. Door openings, low ambient temperatures at dawn and the extra weight of ice or plates all draw energy, and a realistic planning range for a loaded chilled van is closer to 150 to 170 kilometres. A port-to-market loop that starts and ends at the same depot and covers under 120 kilometres per day therefore fits comfortably, even with a generous reserve for traffic and an unscheduled second collection.

Heating and cooling the cab also matters. Electric vans do not have waste engine heat to warm the cabin, so a cold morning start can use several kilowatt-hours from the battery before the first delivery. Pre-conditioning the cab and the load while the van is still plugged in at the depot preserves range for driving, and the same routine can pre-cool the insulated box so the eutectic plates start from a favourable temperature. Drivers who adopt the plug-in routine report a noticeably more predictable range than those who climb in and drive cold.

Range planning: Treat the i5 200 km figure as a best case. A loaded fish shuttle with door openings and an early cab heater should plan on 150 to 170 km and keep the last 20 percent of charge for a second collection.

Early-morning duty cycle and depot charging

The working day of a fish market van is unusual: a short, intense burst of activity between roughly 03:00 and 09:00, followed by a long idle period in the middle of the day and perhaps a light afternoon top-up run. That pattern is almost ideal for an electric vehicle, because the van can return to the depot after the morning shift and charge slowly for hours at a modest rate rather than relying on public fast chargers. A 66 kilowatt-hour pack can be replenished overnight from a standard depot wallbox, while the 100 kilowatt-hour version suits fleets that also run an evening route.

Charging infrastructure should be designed around the return time, not the departure time. If ten vans all come back at 08:30 and plug in together, the depot transformer and the cable runs must handle the simultaneous load, or a smart-charging controller should stagger the start times. The i5 supports DC fast charging, which is useful when a van must be turned around between the morning market and an afternoon restaurant delivery, but frequent fast charging on a tight schedule can raise battery temperature and is best kept as an exception rather than the daily rule.

Brine and washdown corrosion on an electric chassis

A fish truck is washed more often and more aggressively than almost any other light commercial vehicle, and salt brine from the catch is highly corrosive to steel, fasteners and electrical connectors. On a battery-electric van the risk has an extra dimension, because water ingress into a high-voltage connector or the battery enclosure is a safety issue rather than just a rust problem. The i5 should be specified with sealed cable runs, IP-rated connectors and a battery pack mounted clear of the splash zone, and the body should sit high enough that the floor does not trap brine against the chassis rails.

Washdown practice should be written down and taught. High-pressure water should be directed away from connector blocks and the charging port, and the van should not be jet-washed directly at the underside of the battery enclosure. A routine of rinsing with fresh water after a salty quay visit, followed by a visual check of the charge port cover, prevents the slow corrosion that otherwise shows up as a fault code months later. Fleets that treat the electric drivetrain as something to protect rather than something to fear find the i5 survives a port environment as well as a diesel van.

Build for the wash: Specify an i5 with IP-rated connectors and sealed cable runs, and train drivers to keep high-pressure water away from the charge port and battery enclosure. Brine corrosion on an EV is a safety fault, not just rust.

Payload, noise and access to residential markets

The i5 payload allowance must cover the driver, the insulated body, the cooling system and the product. A well-built chilled body with eutectic plates and a partition can weigh several hundred kilograms, so a 4.5 tonne variant may leave less useful load than a buyer expects. Operators should weigh the finished body before ordering the full fleet and choose the 5.5 tonne version if crates of fish and ice regularly push the gross weight. Under-specifying payload on a cold-chain van is a common and expensive mistake.

Buyers comparing electric platforms can review the SAGMOTO new energy vehicle range for wider model context. The quiet running of an electric van is a genuine commercial advantage in fish markets that border residential streets or operate under early-morning noise restrictions. A diesel van idling outside a market at 04:00 generates complaints and, in some cities, fines; the i5 moving silently on its single-speed reduction drive avoids both. The absence of exhaust at street level also matters in enclosed market halls. For operators bidding for city cold-chain contracts, the low-noise and zero-local-emission profile can be the deciding factor rather than the headline purchase price.

Electric fish-market logistics succeed when the vehicle is matched to the route rather than to a general specification. The i5 has the range, the payload class and the quiet operation for short, repetitive port-to-market work, and the depot-based schedule turns charging into a planned overhead instead of a constraint. The details decide the outcome: a sealed body, a realistic range assumption, a disciplined plate-freezing routine and a wash process that respects the high-voltage system. Operators who get those right find the i5 a clean and dependable way to move a perishable catch through the hours before sunrise.

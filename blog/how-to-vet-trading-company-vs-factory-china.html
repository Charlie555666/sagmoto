How Do You Tell a Trading Company from a Factory When Buying Trucks from China?

Oct 09, 2026Buyer Guide11 min read

Quick answer: Tell a trading company from a factory by demanding three proofs: a Chinese business licence showing manufacturing scope, a factory authorisation letter citing the exact model, and a live video walkthrough of the production line. A SAGMOTO-authorised exporter such as Shaanxi Fenghan Trading holds authorisation SQIDACN 28 and SAG-2025-201.

Every month a fleet buyer somewhere in Africa, the Middle East or Central Asia wires a deposit to a supplier who turns out to be neither a factory nor an authorised exporter, and the truck that eventually arrives is the wrong specification, the wrong colour, or never arrives at all. The confusion is understandable because the Chinese truck export market is layered: original equipment manufacturers, authorised regional exporters, general trading companies that source from several brands, and at the bottom a long tail of brokers who hold no authorisation and no inventory. The question this article answers is practical, not academic: before you pay, how do you know who you are actually dealing with, and does it matter?

It matters enormously, because the entity you pay determines your warranty, your parts supply, your customs paperwork and your recourse. A factory builds the truck. An authorised exporter like Shaanxi Fenghan Trading Co., Ltd. is authorised by the factory to sell and export that brand, holds the contract terms, and stands behind the warranty. A general trading company may or may not have that authority. A broker has none of it. The rest of this guide gives you the verification steps that separate the four, the red flags that end a conversation, and the reasons a trading company can be the safer route than the factory itself.

What Is the Difference Between a Factory, a Trading Company and a Broker?

The Chinese commercial registry distinguishes a manufacturer, whose business licence scope includes production or manufacturing, from a trading company, whose scope includes sales, import and export but not manufacturing. A broker is usually an individual or a tiny trading company that does not even hold its own export licence and subcontracts the paperwork. The distinctions are not moral judgments; each has a valid role. What is illegitimate is a broker presenting as a factory, or a trading company presenting as an authorised exporter of a brand it cannot prove it represents.

For the SAGMOTO brand, the factory is the OEM. Shaanxi Fenghan Trading is an authorised exporter, which means it is contracted by the factory to sell and ship SAGMOTO trucks internationally and to honour the export warranty and parts commitment. That status is documented by authorisation references SQIDACN 28 and SAG-2025-201, which any buyer can ask to see. A general trading company that simply lists SAGMOTO among many brands, with no authorisation letter, is sourcing on the spot and cannot commit factory warranty in the same way.

How Do You Verify a Supplier Before Paying a Deposit?

Verification is a four-step process that costs the buyer nothing before a deposit and removes almost all the risk. The steps are document review, authorisation confirmation, live video, and contract terms. Each is quick, and a legitimate supplier completes all four without hesitation.

Step one is the business licence. Request the unified social credit code licence and read the scope. Step two is the factory authorisation letter: it must name the SAGMOTO brand, carry a valid authorisation number, and be dated within the current period. Step three is a live video call walking the production line or the exporter's warehouse, with the authorised contact present; a recorded tour video is not the same as a live call because a recorded video can be anyone's factory. Step four is the contract: it must state warranty duration, parts lead time, labour rate, freight allocation, and the exact model and VIN range.

Is Buying From a Trading Company Worse Than Buying Direct From a Factory?

This is the question buyers most often get wrong. The assumption is that cutting out the middleman always saves money. In truck export it frequently does not, because the authorised exporter earns a margin but removes costs and risks the factory will not. A factory will quote a truck, but the buyer then manages export declaration, ocean booking, marine insurance, destination customs, pre-delivery inspection, parts pipeline and warranty claims across time zones and in a second language. An authorised exporter bundles those into the price and into a single accountable contract.

For most export fleets the authorised exporter is the lower-risk route. The marginal price difference, typically 3 to 8 percent of vehicle value, buys export documentation, a consolidated parts commitment, a stated warranty with labour and freight terms, and a named contact who is contractually responsible. A factory direct deal can be cheaper per unit for a very large order, but it shifts the entire back-office and claims burden onto the buyer, and for a 1 to 30 unit fleet that burden usually costs more than the saving.

Key point: An authorised exporter is not a cost added on top of the factory; it is the entity that makes the factory's warranty enforceable across a border. Paying a trading company with valid authorisation is paying for accountability, and for most fleets that is the cheaper path once claims begin.

What Red Flags Mean You Should Stop the Conversation?

Certain behaviours are near-certain signals of a supplier you should not pay. None of them prove fraud by itself, but each shifts the burden of proof onto the supplier, and a legitimate one resolves it instantly. The red flags are refusal of a live video call, a business licence scoped only to trading with no brand authorisation, a price far below market, a demand for full payment to a personal account, and an inability to name the exact model and VIN range.

On price, a 6x4 SAGMOTO tractor lands in the range of USD 62,000 to 78,000 depending on specification; a quote of USD 45,000 for that truck is not a bargain, it is a different transaction, and the most common outcome is a deposit followed by requests for more money to release a truck that does not match the order. On payment, any request to send to a personal WeChat, Alipay or bank account rather than the company account is a stop signal regardless of how plausible the explanation.

  • No live video, ever. A supplier who is always too busy to walk the line live is hiding something about where the truck comes from.
  • Vague authorisation. We are authorised, trust us, with no letter and no number, is not authorisation.
  • Pressure to pay in full now. Legitimate exporters take a deposit and balance against bill of lading or inspection.
  • No specific model knowledge. If they cannot discuss Weichai WP10H torque or X9s GCW, they are not close to the product.
  • Switching specs after deposit. A supplier who changes engine or axle after payment was never building to your order.

Should You Ask For a Factory Audit Before Ordering?

For a large order an audit is justified and cheap relative to the exposure. A third-party inspection firm can visit the factory and the exporter for a few hundred dollars and return a report with photographs, the licence, and the production status. For orders above 10 units or above USD 600,000, that spend is a rounding error against the risk and should be standard practice. For smaller orders, a live video walkthrough with the authorised exporter and, where possible, the factory representative together is sufficient, and it costs the buyer nothing before the deposit.

The audit question also answers the deeper one: do you need the factory, or do you need an accountable authorised exporter? For the overwhelming majority of export fleets buying SAGMOTO tractor trucks prime mover units, the accountable exporter with verifiable authorisation is the right counterparty, and the audit confirms the exporter's claims rather than replacing them. The factory is the builder; the exporter is the party you will actually call when a warranty claim opens at 2 a.m. in your time zone.

Conclusion

Telling a trading company from a factory when buying trucks from China is not difficult if you require the proofs before you pay. Read the business licence scope, demand a SAGMOTO factory authorisation letter citing SQIDACN 28 and SAG-2025-201, take a live video walkthrough, and sign a contract that states warranty, parts, labour and freight in numbers. A trading company that clears those four bars, such as an authorised exporter like Shaanxi Fenghan Trading, is frequently the safer and cheaper route than the factory itself, because it carries the cross-border accountability that makes a warranty real.

The red flags are consistent and easy to remember: no live video, no authorisation number, price far below market, payment to a personal account, and vague product knowledge. Any one of them should stop the conversation. The buyers who lose money in this market are not the ones who asked too many questions; they are the ones who wired a deposit before asking the first one.

i5 vs Maxus eDeliver 7: Electric Light Truck Buying Comparison

2026-11-27Comparison Guide9 min read

Buyers comparing the SAGMOTO i5 with the Maxus eDeliver 7 are often comparing two vehicles that look similar in a showroom but belong to different classes. The i5 is a light truck chassis with a separate cargo body, a 100 kWh LFP battery, a 160 kW motor and 1,050 Nm of torque, positioned in the 7.5 to 12 t GVW band. The eDeliver 7 is a large van, typically offered with a battery in the region of 77 to 88 kWh and a motor around 150 kW, in a roughly 3.5 to 4.5 t class. Both are credible electric options, but they solve different logistics problems. This comparison sets out the class difference honestly and then examines the factors that decide which one belongs in a fleet.

Start with the class difference

A van is an integrated vehicle: cab, cargo box and chassis are one structure, produced on a vehicle assembly line. A truck chassis is sold with a separate body fitted by a bodybuilder, which allows the operator to specify a box, flatbed, tipper or refrigerated unit. The i5 belongs to the second category and carries a much higher gross vehicle weight. That means it can carry heavier loads and accept a wider range of bodies, but it is larger, less manoeuvrable in tight streets and more expensive to buy and energy-hungry to run.

The eDeliver 7 is large by van standards and offers useful cubic capacity with a lower floor and a more car-like driving position. For parcel and urban delivery work where volume matters more than mass, a van is often the simpler and cheaper tool. Buyers should decide on payload and body requirement before comparing battery figures, because the two vehicles are not interchangeable in most operations.

Payload per cubic metre and volume

Parcel work is usually limited by volume before it is limited by weight. A van with a tall, square load compartment can carry a large number of light parcels, and its payload per cubic metre is often the deciding metric. The i5's separate body can be built high and wide to maximise volume, but the chassis weight and the battery mass reduce the payload available for cargo. Where a route is weight-constrained, the higher GVW of the truck wins; where it is volume-constrained, the van may carry as much useful cargo at lower running cost.

Ask both suppliers for the payload figure at the exact body configuration you plan to use, not the theoretical chassis capacity. A bodybuilder's estimate of body weight can vary significantly, and an optimistic figure can leave a vehicle overweight at registration.

Deck height and loading efficiency

The eDeliver 7 has a low load floor, which reduces driver strain during manual handling and makes tail-lift or pallet loading easier. The i5, as a truck chassis, has a higher deck, partly because of the battery packaging and the larger wheels. A higher deck can be an advantage where a tail-lift is fitted and the vehicle must align with a dock, but it is a disadvantage for repeated manual loading at kerbside. Fleets should measure the deck height against their existing docks and trolleys before ordering.

Loading time is a hidden cost in urban delivery. A vehicle that is quick to load and unload completes more drops per shift, which can matter more than a difference in battery capacity. Observing a real loading cycle is more informative than comparing dimensions on paper.

Charging curve and depot planning

Published peak charging power is only part of the story. A useful comparison looks at the charging curve: how long the vehicle can sustain high power before the rate tapers as the battery fills. In practice, a short mid-day top-up from, say, 20 to 80 percent is often more valuable than a theoretical maximum figure. Buyers should request a charging curve or at least a stated 20 to 80 percent time for both vehicles and compare them against their actual shift pattern.

Depot electrical capacity is equally important. A fleet planning to charge several vehicles at once needs to size the supply, the chargers and the cable management before ordering. Overnight charging at low power is cheap and gentle on the battery but requires dwell time; fast charging between shifts is convenient but needs a larger grid connection. The i5's larger battery takes more energy to fill, so a depot built around a smaller van may need an upgrade.

Battery chemistry and cycle life

The i5 uses lithium iron phosphate, a chemistry valued for thermal stability and long cycle life. It tolerates frequent charging and high state-of-charge better than many nickel-based alternatives, which suits a vehicle that works every day and charges overnight. The eDeliver 7's exact chemistry varies by variant and market, so buyers should confirm the cell type and the warranty terms rather than assume. Cycle life matters because it determines the residual value of the vehicle after several years of daily use.

A battery warranty should be read carefully: check the number of years, the mileage limit, the minimum retained capacity and whether fast charging affects the terms. A fleet that plans to keep the vehicle for five years should ask for a written explanation of what is covered and what is excluded.

Thermal management in hot climates

Battery temperature is a major factor in hot markets. Both vehicles use liquid cooling or thermal management systems to keep cells within their operating range, but the design and the control strategy differ. In high ambient temperatures, a vehicle may derate charging power to protect the battery, which extends the time spent at the depot. Performance can also be limited on a long uphill run with a heavy load. Buyers in hot climates should ask how the system behaves at high ambient temperatures and whether the vehicle can fast-charge repeatedly without a long cool-down.

Parking in shade and scheduling fast charging for the cooler part of the day are simple operational measures that help. A fleet that ignores thermal behaviour may find that real-world charging times are much longer than the brochure suggests during the hottest months.

Service access and workshop requirements

A van benefits from a wide service network because it shares components with passenger vehicles and is familiar to many workshops. A truck chassis is more specialised: high-voltage systems, air brakes in some configurations and a separate body all require a workshop with the right training and equipment. The i5 therefore needs more planning for service access, including a clear warranty process and a parts agreement for items such as contactors, cooling components and brake parts.

Buyers should also consider the body. A separate body means two suppliers to deal with if something goes wrong: the vehicle importer and the bodybuilder. Clarify who is responsible for body defects and who authorises warranty work before placing the order.

Choose the van if, choose the truck if

Choose the eDeliver 7 if your work is parcel delivery in dense urban streets, if volume is the limiting factor, if a low floor reduces manual handling, and if you want a vehicle that a wide service network already understands. It is the simpler choice for a fleet that does not need a specialised body and wants to keep depot electrical upgrades modest.

Choose the i5 if you need to carry heavier loads, if you want a body built for a specific trade such as refrigerated distribution or a flatbed, or if your GVW class requires a truck chassis. It also suits operators whose routes include a mix of urban and suburban legs where the larger battery and higher payload justify the extra size. The decision is a class decision first and a battery decision second.

Import planning and landed cost

Electric vehicles add specific lines to the landed cost. Confirm the battery state of charge on arrival, the shipping mode for the high-voltage system and whether the vehicle can be charged with local infrastructure without an adapter. Check the connector type and the charging standard against your depot equipment before shipment. For the i5, add the cost of the body and its separate shipping or local fabrication, and agree who is responsible for the body warranty.

It is also wise to budget for technician training on high-voltage safety and for the first service kit, including coolant and filter items specific to the electric drivetrain. Confirm any local incentives for electric commercial vehicles and the documentation needed to claim them, since rules vary by market. Engine emission calibration is not relevant to a battery-electric vehicle, but registration documents and battery conformity papers should be agreed with the importing authority in advance. Fleets reviewing the full electric range can consult the SAGMOTO new energy electric trucks page.

Final assessment

The i5 and the eDeliver 7 are both competent electric vehicles, but they are not direct substitutes. The i5 is a flexible light truck with higher GVW, a 100 kWh LFP battery and a body platform; the eDeliver 7 is a large van with lower mass, a low floor and strong urban agility. Define the payload, volume and body requirement first, then compare charging and total cost per delivery. For many fleets the right answer is to run both types on different routes.

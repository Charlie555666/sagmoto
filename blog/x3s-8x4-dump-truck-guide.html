X3s 8x4 Dump Truck Buying Guide: Payload Law, Body Capacity and Axle Loads

2026-11-09Buying Guide8 min read

Four-axle dump trucks occupy a specific niche: they exist because two-axle and three-axle rigs hit legal weight ceilings before their bodies are full. The SAGMOTO X3s in 8x4 configuration, powered by Cummins ISME 420 or Weichai WP10-WP12 engines in the 385 to 420 hp band, gives buyers roughly 30 to 40 percent more legal payload than an equivalent 6x4 in the same regulatory environment. But the 8x4 format only pays off when the buyer understands the payload law it will operate under, sizes the body to actual material density, and does the cycle mathematics before signing. This guide covers those three disciplines in order, then closes with the commercial terms.

Why Four Axles: The Logic of Payload Law

In regulatory systems that cap gross weight per axle rather than per vehicle, adding axles adds legal payload directly. A 6x4 tipper with one 9-tonne steering axle and two 13-tonne drive axles has a theoretical maximum of 35 tonnes gross, though bridge-formula rules in many markets limit it to 31 or 32 tonnes. An 8x4 with two steering axles front and two drive axles rear raises the theoretical sum to about 44 tonnes, with practical legal gross weights of 38 to 41 tonnes in markets that permit four-axle rigids. Against a kerb mass of 13 to 14 tonnes for a steel-bodied 8x4, that leaves legal payloads of 25 to 27 tonnes, versus 18 to 20 tonnes for the 6x4. Whether the extra purchase price is justified depends entirely on whether your market allows the higher gross weight.

Buyers in markets that cap rigid trucks at three axles, or that tax four-axle rigs into a punishing bracket, gain nothing from the format and should stop reading and order the 6x4. Everyone else should next check the steering axle rule. Some jurisdictions limit the front tandem to 15 tonnes combined, which forces a body positioning that moves the load centre rearward. Others permit 16 or 17 tonnes up front. This single rule changes where the body front wall sits on the chassis, so it must be communicated to the factory before the sub-frame is welded.

The 8x4 premium only pays where law permits 38-plus tonnes gross. Verify the four-axle weight law and steering tandem limit in writing before placing an order, because body position and axle specification are welded-in decisions that cannot be revised after production.

Axle Load Distribution: Getting the Geometry Right

An 8x4 dump truck carries its load between four contact patches, and the distribution shifts dramatically as the body rises. At rest, loaded, a well-specified truck puts roughly 38 percent of gross weight on the steering tandem and 62 percent on the drive tandem. During hoisting with the body at 45 degrees, load migrates forward and can momentarily exceed the front axle ratings if the body is positioned too far forward. Body position is therefore a compromise: too far forward risks steering overload at full hoist, too far back starves the steering axles of grip when running empty in wet conditions.

Factory engineers calculate this position from your stated payload, body length and material resting angle. Provide accurate numbers. A body positioned for granite at a 35-degree resting angle will distribute differently when carrying wet sand at a 25-degree angle, and a 1.5-tonne error in assumed payload moves the calculated centre by several centimetres. The practical tolerance is tight enough that ordering from guesswork produces trucks that fail weighbridge checks in one direction or the other.

Body Cubic Capacity: Sizing to Material, Not to Habit

Body volume follows the same density arithmetic that governs every tipper purchase. At 1.7 tonnes per cubic metre for broken granite, a 26-tonne payload needs about 15 cubic metres struck and 18 to 19 cubic metres heaped. Laterite and dry topsoil at 1.3 tonnes per cubic metre need 20 cubic metres struck for the same payload, which is why longer bodies with extension sides are common in laterite markets. Wet sand at 1.9 tonnes per cubic metre fills a 14 cubic metre body to 26.6 tonnes struck, meaning the larger bodies actively invite overload fines in sand service.

Standard export bodies for this wheelbase run 16 to 22 cubic metres. Wall heights vary from 1.2 metres for heavy aggregates to 1.8 metres with extensions for light materials. A useful buying discipline is to ask the supplier to quote the struck and heaped volume, the tare weight of the body, and the payload at your material density on the same sheet. Any supplier who cannot produce those three numbers together is guessing, and you will inherit the guess. Body mass matters more than buyers expect: a fully lined 20 cubic metre body can weigh 600 kg more than a bare 16 cubic metre unit, which is 600 kg of payload lost on every cycle for the life of the truck.

Request struck volume, heaped volume and body tare weight together, then convert to payload at your own material density. The conversion takes ten minutes and determines whether the truck passes weighbridge checks or funds the local fine system.

Site Cycle Mathematics: Where 8x4 Earnings Come From

The four-axle premium is recovered through tonnage per shift, and the arithmetic is simple enough to do on paper. Assume a 12 km loaded cycle averaging 38 km/h loaded and 48 km/h empty, plus 12 minutes for loading and 6 minutes for tipping and positioning. Total cycle time is about 12 minutes driving plus 18 minutes stationary, or 30 minutes, giving 20 cycles in a 10-hour shift with breaks. A 6x4 moving 19 legal tonnes delivers 380 tonnes per shift; the 8x4 moving 26 tonnes delivers 520 tonnes. At a hypothetical haul rate of USD 2.20 per tonne, the 8x4 earns USD 1,144 per shift against USD 836 for the 6x4, a difference of USD 308 per shift.

Against a purchase premium of roughly USD 9,000 to 12,000 over the comparable 6x4, plus slightly higher fuel burn of 3 to 4 litres per 100 km from added rolling resistance, the payback arrives in 35 to 45 operating shifts where the legal weight difference is fully usable. Where weighbridges cap the 8x4 at the same gross weight as the 6x4, the premium never pays back and the truck simply carries empty steel. This is why the legal verification step comes first: the entire financial case for the format rests on it.

Power Take-Off, Tailgate and Wear Options

Several small options materially affect productivity. A hydraulic tailgate with top hinge suits sticky materials that refuse to dump cleanly, adding around USD 1,100. Heating provisions matter in cold-climate markets where frozen aggregate binds the body floor. Cab specification deserves attention too: the X3s cab is available with air suspension seats and climate control, worth specifying for operators running two full shifts. The engine brake is standard; the transmission-mounted retarder is not, and it should be ordered for any site with sustained descents, since 8x4 rigs carry proportionally more kinetic energy than their 6x4 sisters on the same gradient.

Tyres, Wheels and Running Gear Choices

Tyre policy deserves more attention on an 8x4 than on most trucks because ten tyres carry the legal liability of the whole format. Standard fitment is 12.00R20 radials on steel rims, which suit mixed surfaces and are widely available in African and Southeast Asian markets at USD 380 to 450 per casing. Operators running primarily paved haul roads can specify 315/80R22.5 tubeless radials, which run cooler at highway speeds, cut roughly 1 litre per 100 km from fuel consumption and are lighter, though casing availability in remote regions is thinner. For unpaved site work, ribbed steer tyres paired with deep-lug drive tyres in an L3 traction class give the best balance of wear and grip, and buyers should confirm the chosen sizes are locally serviceable before ordering, since an 8x4 standing on unavailable rubber earns nothing.

Ask the supplier to quote a spare wheel and carrier, a working air-pressure routine in the driver handbook, and alloy wheel options only if weight savings at the margin matter to your operation. Rotation intervals of every 20,000 km, moving fronts to rears in a crossed pattern, extend casing life by a documented 15 to 20 percent on four-axle rigs, where steering tyres scrub more than on a 6x4 because of the tandem steer geometry.

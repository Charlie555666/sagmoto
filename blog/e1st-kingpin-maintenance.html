SAGMOTO E1st Kingpin and Knuckle Service Play Limits and Greasing

2026-11-17Maintenance8 min read

The steering knuckle on a SAGMOTO E1st carries the entire front axle load through a single vertical pin while letting the wheels swing several degrees in each direction. At 560 hp, with a laden tractor that can put close to 9 tonnes on the steer axle in poor load distribution, the forces through that pin and its bushings are substantial. A worn kingpin rarely fails in a dramatic way; it loosens gradually, changes the wheel's toe behaviour and quietly destroys tyres long before the driver notices anything serious. This article sets out a workshop method for inspecting play, choosing grease, replacing bushings and shims, and reading the early symptoms that separate a short greasing job from an unplanned roadside recovery.

What the kingpin and knuckle assembly does

The E1st front axle uses a forged knuckle that pivots on a hardened kingpin, with bronze or composite bushings pressed into the knuckle bosses and a thrust bearing at the lower end to carry vertical load. The tie rod ends link the steering arm to the box through the drag link, so every correction the driver makes is transmitted through the knuckle and resisted by the pin. Because the E1st uses Hande axles rated for heavy prime-mover duty, the pin is larger and the bushings thicker than on a medium-duty truck, which is why the limits below differ from lighter SAGMOTO models.

Technicians often confuse kingpin play with tie rod or wheel bearing play, but each is diagnosed differently. Bearing play shows as rocking at the top of the tyre with the axle lifted, tie rod play shows as movement when the wheel is rocked with the tyres on the ground, and kingpin play shows as movement at the knuckle when a pry bar is levered between the axle beam and the knuckle. Isolating which joint is loose comes first, because replacing a tie rod end when the real fault is a worn bushing wastes parts and leaves the underlying wear untouched.

Inspection interval and access

On a highway E1st covering 180,000 to 250,000 km a year, kingpins should be checked at every 20,000 km service and given a full dial-indicator measurement every 100,000 km or annually, whichever comes first. Trucks working quarry roads, port yards or construction access with constant steering lock should be inspected at half those distances, since dirt and water ingress accelerate wear. With the front axle lifted and the wheels unloaded, lever the knuckle and watch the upper and lower bushing, then check vertical movement with a bar under the tyre. Clean the nipples and dust seals with a stiff brush first; grit held against the knuckle gives a false reading that is worse than the real one.

Measuring vertical and horizontal play

For radial play, clamp a dial indicator to the axle beam with the plunger touching the knuckle near the upper bushing, lever the knuckle in and out and read the total movement. Record separate figures for upper and lower positions, because the lower bushing carries more load and usually wears first. For vertical play, rest the plunger on the kingpin cap and lever the wheel upward. Never average a bad side with a good one. Take three readings at each position and use the middle value, discarding any that differs sharply, because the feel of a pry bar varies with the strength of the person holding it.

Measure with the steering straight ahead and the brake fitted, since full lock puts a tie-rod side force on the knuckle that can be mistaken for bushing play. A dial indicator resolving 0.01 mm is adequate and far more repeatable than guesswork. Write the figures into the fleet log every time, because a slow rise on one side is the signal that a planned repair is due.

Grease type and lubrication frequency

The E1st kingpins need an NLGI 2 lithium-complex extreme-pressure grease with a dropping point above 180 degrees Celsius, and a molybdenum-disulphide additive helps on high-load steer axles. Do not mix calcium-based with lithium-based grease; the thickeners are incompatible and the soft mixture separates under heat. Grease each pin until fresh product purges from the top and bottom seals, which confirms old grease and trapped water have been displaced rather than compressed. Grease every 15,000 km on long haul, or every 5,000 km on severe duty and after any water crossing. Each knuckle normally accepts 40 to 80 grams; if a nipple takes several hundred, suspect a blocked internal channel or a torn seal rather than simply adding more.

Wear limits, bushings and shims

Fine vertical clearance on a new E1st assembly is 0.10 to 0.15 mm, with a reject point of 0.50 mm of axial movement. Radial play at the upper bushing should stay under 0.35 mm; beyond that the answer is new bushings, not another dose of grease. Shims fit between the knuckle and the axle beam or under the kingpin cap to restore preload, and graded thicknesses let the technician return clearance to the middle of the band. Fit matched sets on both faces and never use a shim to hide a bushing that has gone oval.

When pressing out a worn bushing, support the knuckle boss in a press and use a driver of the correct outside diameter so the bore is not scored. New bushings must go in with their grease grooves aligned to the nipple channel, an easy detail to miss that silently starves the joint. Ream the bushing to the pin diameter for that axle rather than assuming a new pin and bushing will mate straight from the box, and set running clearance by measurement rather than by how freely the knuckle turns by hand.

Key point: Measure kingpin play with a dial indicator on a clean, unloaded front axle and record upper, lower and vertical figures separately. A feel test cannot detect the early ovality that destroys tyres.

Symptoms a fleet should act on

Three complaints point to the kingpins. Shimmy is a rapid oscillation felt through the wheel at 60 to 90 km/h, often starting after a balance or new tyres but persisting because the knuckle lets the wheel wobble. Uneven tyre scrub appears as smooth feathering across the tread, usually on one outer shoulder, because a loose knuckle changes toe as load shifts. Wander is a slow drift needing constant correction, and on a windy highway it makes a 560 hp tractor tiring to keep in lane. Drivers may also report a clunk when steering lock to lock at low speed, which is the knuckle shifting as the tie rod reverses.

Any of these should trigger a measured inspection the same day, not the next service, since a worn pin deteriorates faster once geometry is disturbed. Tyres on a truck with loose kingpins can lose a third of their normal life on one long-haul route, so acting on a driver's vague complaint is usually cheaper than replacing a tyre set. A fleet that treats steering feel as data rather than noise catches these faults while they are still cheap.

Cost of neglect on a high-power tractor

The parts cost of a kingpin and bushing kit is small against the chain of damage that follows delay. A pair of 315/80 R22.5 front tyres represents several thousand dollars, and scrub from a loose knuckle consumes them without any reduction in load. Add a steering box working against excess play, drag links and tie rod ends that wear early, and alignments that will not hold, and a few hundred dollars of bushings can become a five-figure repair before the next major service. The greater risk is safety: at highway speed a pin that separates can let the wheel fold under with a laden trailer behind, and even without a collision the recovery and mobile repair bill in a remote corridor dwarfs planned workshop time.

Parts to stock and records to keep

A workshop supporting E1st tractors should hold kingpins, upper and lower bushings, thrust bearings, dust seals and graded shims, plus a torque sheet for the cap and steering arm fasteners. Cap nuts on this axle class are typically tightened to 250 to 320 Nm, and steering arm bolts should be torqued to the axle manual value and rechecked after the first 500 km. Stock the whole kit so a missing thrust shim never strands the truck. Record play figures, grease quantity and part numbers at every visit; a rising trend lets the repair be booked into a planned window. Parts matched through the SAGMOTO E1st heavy tractor page should be checked against the axle serial, since running changes can alter bushing dimensions.

Key point: A loose kingpin ruins tyres and steering parts long before it fails outright. Measure, record and act on the trend, and replace bushings and shims as a matched set rather than adding grease to mask movement.

Kingpin maintenance rewards consistency more than intensity. A technician who measures the same values year after year spots a developing fault earlier than one who waits for a complaint. On a tractor built for long-distance heavy haulage, the steering geometry is the link between driver intent and a controlled stop, and keeping that link tight is one of the cheapest ways to protect tyres and load. Plan the inspection around the route and season, stock shims before they are needed, and treat any change in steering feel as information rather than an annoyance to ignore until the next service.

SAGMOTO Z3 vs X9s: Choosing the Right 6x4 Tractor for Long Haul

2026-11-25Comparison Guide9 min read

The SAGMOTO Z3 and X9s look similar on a specification sheet. Both are 6x4 tractors in the 40 to 60 t GCW class, both use Fast automated gearboxes and both are built for heavy work. The differences lie in emphasis rather than in basic layout. The Z3 is powered by the Cummins M13 at 520 HP with 2,400 to 2,500 N m, while the X9s spans 460 to 560 HP from Cummins or Weichai with a torque ceiling of 2,550 N m. This comparison sets out where those figures translate into a real operating advantage and where they simply reflect two ways of serving the same long-haul market.

Power and torque overlap

The two tractors occupy overlapping territory. A 520 HP Z3 sits in the middle of the X9s power range, and the 2,400 to 2,500 N m of the M13 is close to the 2,200 to 2,550 N m quoted for the X9s. On paper neither has a decisive edge. The meaningful distinction is how the power is delivered: the Z3 is calibrated around a single M13 rating, while the X9s offers a choice of Cummins or Weichai engines and a wider spread of ratings. A fleet wanting one consistent specification across a large order will often prefer the fixed rating; one matching engines to different routes may value the choice.

Torque curve shape matters more than the peak figure. A tractor spend most of its life between 1,200 and 1,700 rpm, where the area under the torque curve determines how often it needs a downshift. Ask the supplier for the torque curve rather than the peak number, and compare the engine speed at which peak torque arrives. An engine that reaches its plateau early will feel stronger on a rolling route even when the headline figure is lower.

Fuel burn and efficiency

Fuel is the largest single operating cost on a long-haul lane. At 40 t on a rolling highway both tractors typically record in the region of 33 to 38 L per 100 km, with the result driven by final drive, tyre choice and driver behaviour as much as by engine rating. The Z3 with a taller highway final drive can sit near 1,400 rpm at cruise, which favours economy on flat ground. An X9s specified with a lower final drive for heavy gradients will turn faster and use more fuel on the same lane but will hold speed better when loaded.

The fair comparison uses cost per tonne-kilometre over your own route rather than litres per 100 km in isolation. If both trucks carry the same payload over the same distance, the one with lower fuel burn wins. If the X9s is regularly trusted with the heavier trailer on a steep lane where the Z3 would need an extra trip, the higher burn can still produce a lower cost per tonne. Model the actual loads before drawing a conclusion from a single fuel figure.

Key point: The Z3 and X9s have broadly similar highway fuel burn, so the economic difference comes from final drive choice and trailer weight rather than from engine brand or peak horsepower.

Gearbox and final drive

Both models are offered with Fast 12-speed or 16-speed AMT gearboxes, so the hardware choice is familiar to most heavy-truck workshops. Where they diverge is in the calibration and the recommended final drive. The Z3 is usually paired with a highway ratio that keeps the M13 near its lowest brake specific fuel consumption at cruise, while the X9s is more often specified for heavy gross loads where a shorter ratio protects against frequent downshifts on gradients.

Confirm the exact gearbox unit and the final drive ratio on the quotation, because the same model name can cover more than one build. Request the road speed at a stated engine rpm in top gear; that single number reveals whether the truck was built for flat linehaul or for loaded hill work. A mismatch here is hard to correct after delivery and will affect fuel burn for the entire service life.

Cab and driver environment

For a crew covering long distances, the cab is an economic factor. Both tractors offer a sleeper cab with an air-suspended driver seat, bunk, climate control and storage for a multi-day trip. The decision usually comes down to the details the supplier is willing to document: bunk length, seat adjustment range, mirror heating and the quality of the cab mounts. A short trial drive on a route with rough patches reveals more than a list of features.

Noise and vibration deserve attention because they accumulate over a shift. Check whether the cab shell is isolated on rubber mounts and whether the floor and firewall have a dense barrier layer. A tractor that is quiet at 60 km/h but buzzes at 90 km/h will be unpopular with drivers who spend most of their time at cruise speed. Record driver feedback from any trial and make it part of the selection criteria rather than a footnote.

Gross weight and gradeability

Both tractors are quoted in the 40 to 60 t GCW band, so neither has a structural advantage on rated weight. Work a starting-grade example to see where the torque figures matter. With 2,500 N m, a first gear near 13 to one, a final drive of 3.36 and 85 percent efficiency, tractive effort at a 0.51 m tyre radius is about 180 kN. On a 49 t combination that is roughly 37 percent of weight. An X9s at 2,550 N m with the same ratios gives only a slightly higher figure, so the practical difference comes from the final drive and from how early the engine reaches its torque plateau.

A fleet that habitually runs at the top of the GCW band on steep routes should prioritise a shorter final drive and confirm that the AMT downshift logic holds a useful gear on a long climb. A fleet running lighter trailers on flat ground should prioritise the taller ratio for fuel economy. In both cases the engine rating is the least important of the three variables, which is why a horsepower-only comparison is misleading.

Key point: With almost identical GCW bands and overlapping torque, gradeability is decided by final drive and shift calibration, so specify the ratio before comparing horsepower figures.

Duty cycle fit

The Z3 makes the most sense for a dedicated linehaul operation with consistent trailers, steady routes and an emphasis on fuel cost. Its single M13 rating and highway-oriented calibration suit a fleet that wants every truck to behave the same way. Standardisation reduces spares inventory and simplifies driver training, both of which matter when a fleet grows beyond a handful of units.

The X9s is the more flexible choice where loads and routes vary. The wider engine range lets a buyer match power to a specific ladder of routes, and the higher torque ceiling gives useful margin on heavy or hilly work. That flexibility has a cost in complexity, because two X9s with different engines and ratios are effectively two different trucks in the parts store. Choose the flexible model only when the route mix genuinely requires it.

Resale, parts and support

Resale value follows the buyer pool. A 6x4 tractor with a known Cummins rating is easy for a second owner to evaluate, so both models should find a market wherever heavy linehaul is active. The Z3 single-specification approach can help because buyers know exactly what they are getting. The X9s broader range can either help or hinder, depending on whether the local market recognises the specific engine and ratio fitted.

Parts support should be checked at the variant level. Both models use Fast gearboxes and common wear items, but engine parts differ between the Cummins and Weichai options on the X9s. Confirm what the nearest dealer actually stocks, and ask for a recommended spare-parts kit with the quotation. Financing and total cost of ownership

The purchase price is only the starting point. Financing terms often depend on the expected residual value, and a tractor that is easy to resell lowers the monthly cost even when the initial quote is higher. Compare quotes over the intended holding period, including fuel, tyres, scheduled service and the likely resale figure. On a long-haul lane these running costs exceed the price difference between the two models within a few years.

Timing matters as well. A fleet with a firm contract on a flat corridor can justify the Z3 on fuel grounds, while one entering a region with steep borders and variable loads may prefer the X9s margin. Revisit the calculation whenever the route mix changes, because the right answer depends on the work rather than on loyalty to a particular engine.

SAGMOTO X6 Mobile Banking Vehicle: Branch-on-Wheels Body, Power and Comms

2026-11-11Industry Application8 min read

A medium-duty chassis with a gross combination weight spanning 12 to 60 tonnes, the SAGMOTO X6 runs the Cummins ISD engine in the 210 to 245 horsepower band and ships as either a dropside cargo truck or an all-wheel-drive unit. Those attributes make it an ideal platform for a mobile banking unit, the branch-on-wheels that brings cash deposit, withdrawal, account opening and agent banking to rural communities too small or too remote for a permanent branch. For banks, microfinance institutions and government payment agencies in emerging markets, the X6 delivers the payload, the ground clearance and the electrical foundation needed to run a secure office on a chassis that local workshops can already service. This guide walks through the body choice between armoured and standard builds, the generator and HVAC power budget, the satellite and cellular communication stack and the route economics that decide whether a mobile branch pays for itself.

Armoured Versus Standard Body

The first decision for any X6 mobile banking build is how much physical protection the cash and the staff require, and the answer depends entirely on the threat profile of the routes served. A standard panel body with reinforced locks and a secured strongroom cabinet is sufficient where the unit operates in organized towns and travels mainly on paved roads with armed escort only at the cash load point. This build is lighter, cheaper and does not penalise the GVW or the fuel economy of the X6, and it leaves more payload for the generator, the water and the customer queue shelter.

An armoured build adds ballistic-rated wall and glass panels, a hardened strongroom, blast-resistant wheel arches and a reinforced floor, and it is justified where the unit carries large cash volumes through areas with a real robbery risk. The penalty is weight, typically 800 to 1500 kilograms of added mass that eats into the available payload and shifts the centre of gravity, so the X6 must be specified with the heavier spring and tyre rating and the wider track where offered. The table below compares the two approaches on the factors that fleet planners weigh before signing a build order.

Whichever route is chosen, the body should be assembled on the X6 chassis using the factory mounting points rather than drilled into the frame, because a clean mounting scheme preserves the warranty and the resale value of the truck. Generator and HVAC Power Budget

A mobile branch is an electrical load on wheels, and the power budget decides the generator size, the battery bank and the quiet hours the unit can run without refuelling. The core loads are the ATM or cash recycler at around 1.5 kilowatts peak, the server and the two to three customer terminals at 0.3 kilowatts, the interior and strongroom lighting at 0.2 kilowatts, and the roof-mounted air conditioner that keeps the cabin tolerable in tropical heat at 2.5 to 3.5 kilowatts. Add the satellite terminal and the CCTV recording at another 0.3 kilowatts and the continuous draw lands near 5 kilowatts.

For that load a 6 to 8 kilovolt-ampere diesel generator mounted in a ventilated rear compartment is the pragmatic choice, large enough to start the air conditioner compressor without sagging and small enough to fit the X6 without overloading the rear axle. A deep-cycle battery buffer of 200 ampere-hours lets the unit ride through generator service pauses and run the security system overnight on the strongroom, and a smart inverter prioritises the ATM so a cash transaction never fails mid-dispense. The table below sets out a typical balanced budget for a single-operator rural unit.

LoadRunning kWPeak kWPriority
ATM or recycler0.81.5Critical
HVAC unit2.83.5High
Server and terminals0.30.5High
Comms and CCTV0.30.4Medium

Fuel planning matters because the generator may run six to eight hours per operating day, and a 60 litre dedicated tank sized to the X6 available space gives roughly a week of branch days between refills. Fleet engineers should route the exhaust away from the customer queue and fit a heat shield between the generator bay and the strongroom, because radiant heat is the quiet enemy of both cash-machine reliability and staff comfort in a small cabin.

Power rule: size the generator to the air conditioner start surge, not the steady draw. A unit that handles 3.5 kW peak HVAC plus the ATM will ride every load the branch throws at it without tripping.

Satellite and Cellular Comms

A mobile branch is only useful if it can settle transactions, and connectivity is the make-or-break system once the unit leaves town coverage. The primary link should be a cellular router with dual SIM cards from two competing networks, because in most rural markets one carrier covers a village while the other covers the next valley. Where cellular is absent for long stretches, a satellite terminal provides the always-on backbone for transaction authorisation and the head-office heartbeat that proves the unit is safe.

The comms stack should fail over automatically: the router prefers the faster cellular link and switches to satellite only when both SIMs drop, so the bank pays the higher satellite rate only when truly necessary. A separate satellite phone or messenger terminal gives the crew a lifeline for security alerts independent of the data link, and all traffic should run through a virtual private network tunnel to the core banking system so that customer data is never exposed on a public network. Mounting the antennas on a quick-raise mast lets the unit clear tree line in a valley without parking in the open.

Interior Layout and Security

The working interior of an X6 mobile branch balances customer flow against the need to keep cash under control at all times. The typical plan places the ATM or counter window at the curbside rear, a staff desk with the server and the strongroom in the secured forward partition, and a customer waiting area with a fold-out awning outside for shade. The strongroom door should be viewable from the staff desk, and the CCTV should cover the door, the counter and the exterior queue without blind spots.

Security extends beyond steel: a duress button linked to the satellite messenger, a GPS tracker on the chassis and a geofence alert that flags if the unit leaves its planned route all reduce the consequence of a hijack attempt. Staff should rehearse a lock-down drill so that, if threatened, the strongroom can be sealed and the head office alerted within seconds. These soft measures cost little next to the armoured steel yet often do more to keep a rural crew safe.

Layout principle: the strongroom must be visible from the staff desk and covered by CCTV, because the few seconds saved in a response drill are worth more than any amount of armour plating on the walls.

Rural Route Economics

The business case for an X6 mobile branch rests on matching route density to the cost per kilometre, and the maths is straightforward once the inputs are known. A unit that visits four villages per day, five days a week, at an average of 60 kilometres between stops, travels roughly 1200 kilometres weekly and burns diesel at the X6 rate of about 18 litres per 100 kilometres, so fuel is a predictable and modest line item. The larger costs are the crew, the armour and the cash-in-transit escort, all of which fall as a share of transactions when the route is well loaded.

Revenue comes from transaction fees, new account acquisition and the float interest on deposits collected in cash-poor regions where the bank otherwise has no presence. A unit serving 80 to 120 customers per village day at a few currency units of fee per transaction can cover its running cost within a few months and pay back the body investment inside a year, provided the route is planned to minimise empty running. Banks that rotate the X6 between overlapping territories keep utilisation high and avoid the dead mileage that sinks poorly planned mobile programmes.

Seasonality shapes the plan as much as distance: in the rainy months, unpaved village tracks may close for weeks, and the all-wheel-drive X6 variant earns its keep by reaching communities a two-wheel-drive unit cannot. Smart operators build a dry-season expansion route and a wet-season core route, so the branch keeps serving its most valuable customers when the secondary tracks wash out. The generator and comms redundancies described earlier then become the difference between a cancelled day and a completed one, and the route plan should reserve buffer days for the breakdowns and festivals that quietly erase a thin margin.

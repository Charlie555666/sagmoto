X6s Dump Hoist Cylinder Maintenance: Seals, Pressure Tests and Cycle Times

2026-10-30Maintenance8 min read

Built and shipped by Shaanxi Fenghan Trading Co., Ltd. of Xi'an, the SAGMOTO X6s 6x4 tipper pairs a 270 to 300 horsepower engine with a multi-stage hoist that export workshops service using the procedures below. The hoist is the single most stressed hydraulic circuit on a tipper, lifting a 16 to 22 tonne payload against gravity several times per shift, and a slowly leaking or scoring cylinder is the usual cause of a unit that tips slowly or will not hold the body up. This article focuses narrowly on the hoist cylinder itself: the seal kit, the rod, the oil, the pump pressure test, the lowering valve and the cycle-time checks that reveal internal leakage before it strands a load. Buyers comparing chassis can review the SAGMOTO dump truck models 6x4 8x4 before specifying the body and hoist rating.

Hoist cylinder construction and duty on the X6s

The X6s hoist is a multi-stage telescopic cylinder mounted at the front of the body and pivoting on a trunnion, with two or three nested stages that extend in sequence to raise the body to its 48 to 52 degree dump angle. Each stage runs on a set of wiper, seal and bearing bands, and the largest stage carries the rod that sees the highest side load as the body tilts off-centre under an uneven load. Because the cylinder both lifts and holds the body, any internal leakage past a seal shows first as a slow body drop with the hoist valve closed, and any external leak shows as oil at the stage joints. Understanding which stage carries the load at each angle explains why a top-stage seal fails more often than a bottom one, and why rod scoring concentrates near full extension where the chrome passes the wiper most often.

Multi-stage telescopic seal kit replacement

Replacing the seal kit means stripping the cylinder, so the body must be blocked and the pressure relieved before the trunnion pins are withdrawn, and the stages should be pulled on a clean bench with the weight supported, never hung from a crane that can drop a stage on a foot. The kit contains the wiper, the U-cup or piston seal, the bearing bands and the O-rings sized to each stage, and every seal must be fitted with the correct orientation and a film of the specified hydraulic fluid, not grease. Lap the seal grooves clean and inspect the stage lands for scoring before fitting, because a fresh seal on a damaged land will cut within hours. Record the stage order as it comes apart, because the stages are not always interchangeable and refitting them in the wrong sequence jams the cylinder on the first extension.

Never hang a stripped hoist stage from a crane during seal work; support it on a clean bench, because a dropped stage cracks the chrome and a mis-ordered reassembly jams the cylinder.

After reassembly, cycle the cylinder slowly with the body blocked and watch each stage transition for a hesitation, because a stage that binds at one point signals a damaged land or a twisted seal that will fail under the next load.

Rod scoring and chrome inspection

The rod or top stage chrome is the surface the wiper seals against, and once it is scored by grit carried in on the rod or by a failed wiper, the seal cannot hold and the leak returns within a week of a rebuild. Inspect the chrome under good light and run a fingernail along the travel length; a line that catches the nail deeper than 0.05 mm should be polished or the stage re-chromed, because no seal survives a sharp land. Protect the rod between jobs with a wipe and a sleeve, and never cycle a hoist with the rod coated in dust from a quarry, because the first extension pulls that grit straight past the wiper. A rod that shows pitting from corrosive washdown on a salt or fertiliser route needs a stainless or hardened option rather than repeated re-seals, and the extra cost is recovered the first time a re-chrome is avoided.

Rod scoring also accelerates when the body is overloaded past the 22 tonne mark, because the extra side load bows the stage and the chrome wipes harder against the wiper, so a scoring pattern that recurs after rebuilds points to an overloading habit rather than a parts problem.

Hydraulic oil sampling and ISO cleanliness

The hoist shares its oil with the pump and valves, so contamination that starts at the cylinder returns through the system and scores the pump vanes within days. Sample the oil from the reservoir return line at operating temperature every 500 hours or six months, and target an ISO 4406 cleanliness code no worse than 18/16/13 for a mobile hoist, tightening to 17/15/12 on a new unit still inside its warranty. Keep the sample bottles sealed and labelled with the VIN and hours, because a trend across samples matters more than any single reading when judging whether the hoist is wearing faster than expected.

Pump pressure test procedure

The hoist pump should reach the system working pressure of roughly 16 to 20 MPa with the body loaded, and a pressure that falls short of that under load points to worn pump vanes, a relief valve that is set low, or internal leakage past the cylinder seals. Test by fitting a calibrated gauge at the pump outlet, raising the loaded body to mid-travel and noting the steady pressure, then comparing it with the figure stamped on the hoist nameplate. A gauge that spikes then drops signals a relief valve passing, while a steady low reading with the body rising slowly points to the pump, and a strong pump reading with a slow or dropping body points back to the cylinder. Record the gauge reading and the load, because the same pump reading with a lighter load is normal and the context prevents a needless pump strip. Always relieve the test gauge connection through a bleed port, never by cracking a live pressure line, because a high-pressure oil stream injures skin and floods the bay.

Lowering speed valve setting

The lowering valve meters the oil back to the tank as the body comes down, and its setting controls the descent rate and, more importantly, prevents a runaway drop that can snap the body mounts or bounce the chassis. Set the descent to a controlled 15 to 25 seconds for a full lower on an empty body, and never open the valve to rush a tip, because a fast drop builds a pressure spike in the return line that bursts hoses. If the body falls faster than the set rate it usually means the lowering valve spool is worn or contaminated, and cleaning or replacing the spool restores control; a body that will not lower at all points to a stuck spool or a closed manual isolate, not to the cylinder. Seasonal checks matter because cold oil is thicker and lowers the body more slowly, so the valve should be re-checked after the first cold snap to confirm the descent still sits inside the safe window and has not crept past it.

Keep the descent between 15 and 25 seconds and re-check it after the first cold snap, because a lowering valve that creeps past its set rate can cause a runaway drop that snaps the body mounts.

Cycle-time benchmarking to detect leakage

A simple way to catch internal leakage early is to benchmark the raise and lower times on a known load and track them over the season, because a cylinder that begins to bypass oil internally raises slower and holds the body lower for the same pump effort. Baseline the new unit at 25 to 35 seconds to full raise with a rated load, and treat a 20 percent increase in raise time as a trigger to inspect the seals before a failure. The same benchmark separates pump wear from cylinder leakage: a slow raise with a healthy pump pressure points to the cylinder, while a slow raise with low pump pressure points to the pump. Logging cycle times needs no special tool beyond a stopwatch and the load ticket, which is why it fits a small export workshop without a hydraulic lab.

Safety props and contamination control

No hoist work is safe without a rated body prop placed under the body before anyone goes underneath, because a cylinder that holds pressure in the workshop can still drop if a valve weeps, and a falling 18 tonne body is fatal. Use two props rated above the body weight, never a single jack, and chock the wheels and isolate the hoist before starting. Contamination control is the other half of hoist life: keep the reservoir capped, the breather filtered, the fill point wiped, and the cylinder rod wiped before retraction, because most seal failures start as a grain of grit that the wiper could not stop.

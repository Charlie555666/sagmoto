SAGMOTO X6s Flatbed Machinery Haulage: Transporting Equipment and Oversize Loads

2026-10-14Industry Application10 min read

The X6s chassis is built for serious flatbed work

Machinery haulage is one of the most demanding uses a truck chassis faces, because the load is usually heavy, awkwardly shaped, top-heavy or all three, and it is carried on an open deck where it can shift if not secured with discipline. The SAGMOTO X6s is a heavy-duty platform well suited to this work, powered by the WP12.480 engine producing 480 horsepower and 2,200 Newton-metres of torque, and paired with the Fast Gear 12JS160TA manual or the 12JZ160 automated manual transmission. That torque, available low in the rev range, is exactly what is needed to start a heavily laden flatbed on a gradient with a backhoe or a loaded skip on the deck.

Fleets running plant hire, civil works, equipment rental or inter-site machinery moves will find the X6s a strong foundation for a flatbed or low-loader body. The decision is then less about whether the truck can pull the load, which it can, and more about how the deck is configured, how the load is secured, and how the axle weights are kept legal. This guide addresses those practical questions.

The X6s shares its cargo DNA with the wider range, and operators running mixed equipment fleets will appreciate the commonality with the SAGMOTO cargo truck flatbed box stake models, which simplifies driver familiarisation and parts holding across the depot.

Deck dimensions, height and loading method

The flatbed or plant-body deck on an X6s is typically built to a length of 7.2 to 9.6 metres depending on the wheelbase and the local length limits, with a deck width at or just under the 2.5 metre legal maximum and a deck height chosen to balance loading angle against ground clearance. A lower deck height reduces the ramp angle for driven or wheeled plant climbing aboard, which matters for excavators and rollers that are loaded under their own power, while too low a deck risks grounding on site access roads that are rarely graded to highway standard.

For machinery haulage the deck should be specified with a heavy-duty steel floor, integrated lashing rings or pockets rated to the relevant load, and removable or flip-over side rails where the load profile requires them. A hydraulic tail-lift or a set of integrated loading ramps converts the flatbed into a self-sufficient plant carrier, removing the need for a separate loader at every site. For very heavy or tracked items, a low-loader or step-deck configuration that drops the deck close to the axle line is preferable because it lowers the centre of gravity and the loading angle together.

Ramps, deck height and approach angle

The loading angle is set by the ramp length and the deck height. A short ramp onto a high deck produces a steep angle that a wheeled excavator or a telehandler may not climb safely, while a long ramp on a low deck gives a gentle angle at the cost of extra length that eats into payload space. A practical rule is to aim for a ramp angle under 15 degrees for self-propelled plant, which for a 1.2 metre deck height requires roughly 4.5 metres of ramp. Hydraulic or folding ramps that stow along the deck are the usual solution, and they should be rated for at least the heaviest item the truck will routinely load.

Key point: Keep the self-propelled plant loading angle under 15 degrees. A deck that is too high or ramps that are too short quietly excludes the heaviest machines from being loaded safely under their own power.

Load securing to EN 12195 and the calculation that keeps you legal

In European and many export markets, cargo on a flatbed must be secured to the EN 12195 standard, which defines both the required securing force and the ratings of the equipment used. The core requirement is that the combined restraining force from lashings must prevent the load from shifting forward under braking, sideways under cornering, and rearward under acceleration. The standard expresses this as a required securing force, usually calculated as a percentage of the load weight, with higher requirement in the forward direction where braking forces are greatest.

The calculation is straightforward but must be done per load. For a 10 tonne excavator on a flatbed, the forward securing requirement under EN 12195 is roughly 50 percent of the load weight, or about 5 tonnes of restraining force, which is provided by lashings whose individual lashing capacity and angle combine to that total. Using four lashings rated at appropriate capacity, attached at the correct angle and tensioned with a calibrated ratchet or a direct lashing through the machine's tie-down points, meets the requirement; guessing does not. The discipline that separates a compliant haul from a dangerous one is inspection: lashings must be checked for abrasion and correct tension before departure, re-checked after the first few kilometres as they settle, and the tension re-verified at every stop. A lashing that was adequate at the depot can be inadequate after the load settles on its suspension, and the driver is the last line of defence.

Key point: EN 12195 securing force is a calculation, not a rule of thumb. Compute the forward restraining requirement for each load and match lashing capacity and count to it; re-tension after the first 10 km.

Axle load distribution and gross combination weight

A flatbed loaded with machinery can concentrate enormous weight on a short deck length, and if that weight sits between the axles it overloads the bogie while leaving the front axle light, or if it sits aft it overloads the rear and lifts the steering. The skill in machinery haulage is positioning the load so that each axle stays within its legal limit and the steer axle retains enough load for safe handling. The X6s's wheelbase and suspension are designed for this, but the operator must still place the machine correctly.

As a worked example, a 12 tonne excavator loaded on an X6s with a 18 tonne GVW limit and a 6 plus 2 axle configuration must be positioned so that no single axle exceeds its rating and the steer axle carries enough load for steering authority. Shifting the machine 300 mm forward or aft can move a tonne or more between axles, which is the difference between a legal load and an overweight offence. Operators should use an axle-weighing pad or a weighbridge at calibration to learn where loads sit, and should mark the deck with proven load positions for repeat work.

Permits, escorts and oversize load planning

Once a load exceeds the standard width, height, length or mass limits, it becomes an oversize or abnormal load and requires a permit and often an escort and a route survey. Width over 2.5 to 3.0 metres, height over 4.0 to 4.5 metres, length over the local limit, or mass over the standard axle limits each trigger different requirements, and the thresholds vary by jurisdiction. The X6s hauling a crane or a large excavator on a low-loader will frequently cross one of these thresholds, so the operator must build permit lead time into every quote.

A proper oversize move begins with a route survey: checking bridge heights, weighbridge locations, roundabout radii, overhead cables and low bridges along the proposed path, and identifying the times when movement is permitted, since many jurisdictions restrict abnormal loads to off-peak or night running. An escort vehicle, signage and a defined communication protocol between lead, load and rear escort are standard, and the cost of these must be in the job price rather than discovered on the day.

Crane and excavator transport specifics

Cranes and excavators are the two loads most often moved on an X6s flatbed or low-loader, and each has its quirks. Excavators travel with the boom laid down and secured, the slew lock engaged, and any loose buckets or attachments carried separately or chained to the deck; their tracks or wheels must be blocked against roll. Cranes travel with the superstructure locked, the jib demounted where possible, and counterweights either on board and secured or moved separately depending on mass. Both must have their load centre documented so axle limits are respected, and both benefit from a low-loader configuration that keeps the centre of gravity low through corners.

Mountainous and site-access duty

Machinery haulage rarely begins or ends on good road. The delivery point is a building site, a quarry or a remote wind farm access track, and the journey may include long climbs where the X6s's 480 hp and 2,200 Nm earn their keep. On a sustained grade the torque plateau lets the truck hold a gear without labouring, and the engine brake or retarder should be used descending to protect the foundation brakes from the combined mass of truck and machine. Site access demands ground clearance, robust tyres and a driver trained to read soft ground, because a flatbed stranded 200 metres from the delivery point is a failed job regardless of how the highway leg went.

The retarder or exhaust engine brake interaction with the service brakes is worth specifying deliberately. On long descents with a heavy excavator aboard, relying on the wheel brakes alone will fade them; a secondary retarding device keeps speed controlled with the service brakes reserved for stopping. This is a safety specification, not a comfort option, for any operator running hilly or mountainous plant routes.

Conclusion

The SAGMOTO X6s is a genuinely capable flatbed machinery-haulage platform. Its 480 hp WP12.480 engine and 2,200 Nm of torque give the low-speed pulling authority that plant transport demands, the Fast Gear 12JS160TA or 12JZ160 automated transmission suits stop-start site work, and its heavy chassis takes a 7.2 to 9.6 metre deck or a low-loader body without compromise. The capability of the truck, however, is only half the job.

The other half is the discipline that makes a machinery move legal and safe: a deck configured with rated lashing points and gentle loading angles, load securing calculated to EN 12195 and re-tensioned after the first kilometres, axle weights positioned within legal limits, and an oversize plan with permits, route survey and escort where the load demands it. Operators who master those elements turn the X6s into a profitable plant carrier; those who treat securing and permits as afterthoughts turn it into a liability and a fine.

For equipment and plant fleets, the recommended first step is to profile the machines you actually move, compute their securing and axle-load requirements, and specify the X6s flatbed or low-loader deck, ramps and lashing hardware against that profile. A specification built on the real load list is the foundation of every safe and compliant haul.

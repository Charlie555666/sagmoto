SAGMOTO X5 Air Brake System Maintenance: 2026 Fleet Service Guide

2026-10-07Maintenance9 min read

The X5 air brake system and the duty it faces

The SAGMOTO X5 is a heavy dump truck rated at 31 tonnes GVW, powered by the Weichai WP10.380 or the WP12.430E50, and built for the work that destroys brake systems fastest: quarry haul, pit ramping, site feed and wet-mix haul on mine and construction sites. At 31 tonnes loaded, the kinetic energy the brakes must absorb on a downhill ramp is not a small multiple of a road truck's load, it is a different problem. The X5 uses a full air brake system, which is the correct choice for a truck of this mass because air is a reliable, failure-safe medium and the components are field-serviceable with conventional tools.

Full-air means the brake pedal commands air pressure rather than hydraulic force. The driver's effort builds pressure in a wet tank, that pressure travels to the brake chambers, the chamber diaphragm pushes a pushrod, the pushrod rotates a slack adjuster, and the slack adjuster turns the S-cam that forces the shoes against the drum. This guide covers the whole chain from compressor to lining, the leak test that proves it, and the interval table for a dust environment.

For the wider model family and chassis options, see the SAGMOTO dump truck models 6x4 8x4 overview. The brake fundamentals below apply across the X5 range; only the chamber sizes and the number of axles change with the configuration.

Compressor and air dryer

The air compressor is gear-driven off the engine and charges the system to a cut-out of roughly 8.5 to 10 bar. Its only job is to make pressure, and its only enemy is oil and water contamination, which it creates itself. A compressor that passes excess oil into the lines wets the downstream components and shortens every seal in the system.

The air dryer is the component that earns its place in a mining environment. It removes the water and oil mist from the compressed air and, on most X5 units, includes a heater and an anti-freeze cartridge for cold sites. The cartridge is the service item: a saturated cartridge lets water through, and water in an air brake line freezes in winter and rusts the valves in summer. On a dust and moisture site, replace the cartridge every 12 months or 60,000 km and drain the wet tank daily.

Key point: Drain the wet tank every shift. Water that passes the dryer wets the brake valves and rusts them from the inside; a daily drain is two minutes of work that prevents a week of brake faults. A blocked dryer cartridge is the usual reason water gets through.

Reservoirs, valves and the air circuit

The system runs a wet tank fed from the dryer and separate service and spring-brake reservoirs downstream, each protected by a one-way and a relief valve. The foot valve, the relay valves and the trailer control valve (where fitted) are the switching elements. The check valves keep a failed rear circuit from draining the front, which is the fail-safe logic of full air: a burst line at the rear should still leave the front brakes working.

Brake chambers, slack adjusters and S-cam

At each wheel the service air enters a brake chamber, a diaphragm pushes a pushrod, and the pushrod length is set by the slack adjuster before it turns the S-cam. The S-cam is a shaped cam that, as it rotates, forces the two shoes apart against the drum.

Slack adjusters must be checked for free play and for stroke; a stroke beyond the limit means the lining is down or the adjuster has wound out. Manual slack adjusters are set with the chamber charged and the wheel off the ground, measuring the pushrod travel against the limit stamped on the chamber. Automatic slack adjusters are fitted on most export X5 units and should be verified, not trusted: an auto-slack run to the end of its thread has no further travel and must be reset with new lining.

The S-cam and its bushings wear as the shoes load them. Inspect the cam for scoring at every lining change and replace the bushings with the shoes. The brake chamber should be the correct type and size for the axle: a 31-tonne X5 front uses a different chamber from the rear, and fitting the wrong one changes the brake balance.

Drums and linings

The drum and lining are the consumable interface, and on a 31-tonne dump they wear fast. Linings should be measured at every service and replaced at the minimum thickness marked on the shoe, never run to the rivets. A lining run to the rivets scores the drum, and a scored drum must be skimmed or replaced. The X5 front linings run 18 to 24 months on highway but only 6 to 12 months in continuous pit ramp work.

The expensive mistake is mixing lining grades across an axle, which makes the truck pull under brakes on a loaded descent. Use the spec grade across the axle, fit shoes as a pair, and bed new linings with a short low-speed running-in. The table below gives the X5 brake limits and intervals for mining duty.

ABS and EBS basics

The X5 is available with ABS as standard on export builds and EBS on higher-spec orders. ABS prevents wheel lock on a low-grip surface by modulating the chamber pressure at each wheel through wheel-speed sensors and a modulator valve; EBS adds electronic control of the whole brake command, including stability and, where specified, hill-hold and brake blending. Both systems sit on top of the foundation brakes, and both depend on the foundation brakes being correctly adjusted. ABS does not compensate for a lining at the limit or a slack adjuster wound out; it only modulates what is there.

Service for ABS/EBS is diagnostic rather than mechanical. The wheel-speed sensors must be clean and gapped, the toothed rings free of mud pack, and the modulator valves free of air leaks. A fault light that stays on after start is a sensor or wiring fault in most cases, read with the diagnostic tool. On dusty sites, mud on the sensor ring is the common cause of an intermittent ABS light, and a five-minute clean restores it.

Air leakage testing procedure

The leak test is the single most valuable brake check an X5 operator can do, and it takes fifteen minutes. Build the system to cut-out, switch off the engine, and note the pressure. With the brakes released, the system should not drop more than about 1 bar in one minute on a healthy truck; a faster drop means a leak on the supply side. Apply the service brake and hold: the drop should be negligible, because the foot valve should now be sealing the service side. Release, then apply the spring brake (parking) and hold: a drop here points at the park side or a chamber diaphragm.

Then isolate by axle. Close the isolator to the rear circuit, build and time the front; open it and isolate the front, build and time the rear. A circuit that leaks on its own but holds when joined to the other tells you the fault is downstream of the common supply. For a precise find, spray each fitting, chamber and valve with a soap solution with the system built and watch for bubbles; a bubbling pushrod seal means a chamber diaphragm is weeping and the chamber should be rebuilt before it fails on a descent. Record the test at every service so a slow drift is visible before it becomes a failure. The following table is the leak-test acceptance schedule.

Key point: ABS and EBS only modulate the brakes you already have. A truck with linings at the limit and slacks wound out will not stop safely no matter how good the electronics are. Adjust and measure the foundation brakes; treat ABS/EBS as the top layer, not the fix.

Mining dust environment and interval adjustments

A pit or construction site is the worst case for an air brake system because the enemy is fine abrasive dust, not weather. Dust enters through every unsealed pushrod, every cracked boot, and every chamber that has lost its dust shield, and it scores the cam and packs the shoes. The countermeasures are mechanical and cheap: keep the chamber dust boots intact and replace them on sight, keep the sensor rings clean, and shorten every interval by half against highway duty because the contamination rate is that much higher. Heat is the second enemy; a loaded descent bakes the drums, and a drum run hot repeatedly loses its true diameter, so watch drum condition closely on trucks that do continuous ramp work.

Parts stocking for an X5 brake fleet

For a fleet of X5 dump trucks, brake parts are the highest-turn consumable and the most important to hold. Tier one at the site is linings as an axle pair, a set of slack adjusters, a chamber rebuild kit per chamber type, an air dryer cartridge stock, and a soap-test kit: roughly USD 3,000 to USD 5,000 for ten trucks, removing the stoppages that matter most. Tier two at the base is one air dryer, one foot and relay valve set, and one S-cam and bush kit per twenty trucks, at roughly USD 2,500 to USD 4,000.

Order to the chassis numbers and axle configuration, because a 6x4 and an 8x4 X5 use different chamber sizes and lining widths, and a wrong part is a wrong part. Conclusion

The SAGMOTO X5 full-air brake system is conventional, serviceable and correct for 31-tonne dump duty, but it is only as good as the discipline around it. Charge it with a healthy compressor through a fresh dryer, drain the wet tank daily, adjust and measure the slacks and linings on a short mining interval, run the leak test at every service, and treat ABS and EBS as the layer on top of a foundation brake that is already right. A fleet that does this keeps its X5 brakes safe and predictable and avoids the failure that takes a loaded truck off the road by regulation.

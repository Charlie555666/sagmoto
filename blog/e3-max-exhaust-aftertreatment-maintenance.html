Maintaining the E3 MAX Exhaust and Aftertreatment System

2026-10-29Maintenance9 min read

Shaanxi Fenghan Trading Co., Ltd. exports the SAGMOTO E3 MAX heavy mixed-duty truck to operators who must meet emission rules yet fuel from tanks that rarely match the spec those rules assumed, and the exhaust aftertreatment is where that tension lives. The E3 MAX carries the Weichai WP12 engine at 460 to 520 hp with peak torque to 2,250 Nm, a common-rail six-cylinder mated to a diesel oxidation catalyst, a diesel particulate filter and a selective catalytic reduction system fed by AdBlue urea. Done well, this chain cleans the exhaust and protects the engine; done poorly on high-sulphur export diesel, it clogs, faults and strands a 100-tonne combination. This guide gives workshops the regeneration logic, the fluid rules and the fault map to keep the WP12 compliant and running.

1. DPF Regeneration Cycles

The diesel particulate filter traps soot and burns it off in two ways. Passive regeneration happens continuously on long loaded runs where exhaust temperature stays above roughly 300 degrees Celsius, silently oxidising the soot. Active regeneration is forced by the ECU when the soot load passes a threshold, injecting extra fuel to raise the DPF temperature to 600 degrees and cook the load out, which the driver sees as a warning lamp and a brief rise in idle. A truck that only runs short urban or yard cycles never reaches passive temperature, so the ECU calls active regen more often, and a truck that is constantly interrupted mid-cycle builds a stubborn ash load the burn cannot remove.

Ash is the non-combustible residue of oil and fuel additives that accumulates forever and must be physically cleaned. On the E3 MAX plan a DPF off-vehicle clean every 300,000 to 400,000 km in clean-fuel markets, halving that to 150,000 to 200,000 km where fuel or oil quality is poor. Never delete or disable the DPF to avoid the service, because the back-pressure then robs power, raises EGT and eventually washes oil from the cylinder walls; the cheap fix is the expensive failure.

Key takeaway: Soot burns, ash does not, so the E3 MAX DPF needs an off-vehicle clean every 150,000 to 400,000 km by fuel quality, and deleting it only trades a service for a power-robbing back-pressure failure.

2. SCR System and Urea Quality

The selective catalytic reduction system injects AdBlue, a 32.5 percent urea solution, ahead of the catalyst where it converts to ammonia and reduces NOx to nitrogen and water. The single biggest SCR killer in export markets is bad urea: AdBlue that is out of concentration, frozen and refilled warm repeatedly, or contaminated with water invites crystallisation that blocks the dosing nozzle and the catalyst face. Always buy urea to ISO 22241, store it between minus 11 and 30 degrees Celsius, and never top up from an unmarked drum.

The dosing module and the supply module each have a filter that should be renewed on the scheduled interval, typically 120,000 km, because a clogged supply filter starves the nozzle and logs a derate fault. After a long park in heat, run the system purge cycle before heavy use so crystallised residue clears rather than chokes the first injection. A healthy SCR keeps NOx down and keeps the ECU from derating the WP12 to protect itself.

3. NOx Sensor Faults

The NOx sensor upstream and downstream of the SCR reports conversion efficiency to the ECU, and when it drifts the engine is derated even though the catalyst may be fine. Common faults are a cracked sensor body from heat cycling, a contaminated tip from oil or silicone, and a wiring chafe where the loom runs close to the exhaust. Symptoms are a persistent emission lamp, a stored P20xx code family, and a noticeable drop in power as the ECU protects the aftertreatment with a torque limit.

Diagnose by reading live NOx values: a downstream sensor reading near the upstream value means the catalyst is not converting and the fault is either urea, dosing or the catalyst itself, while a sensor that reads flat zero or max is usually the sensor. Replace on the engine-side harness with the correct grommet and torque, and clear the adaptive values, because a sensor swapped without resetting the trim leaves the new unit mis-calibrated against the old fault.

Key takeaway: A derating emission lamp with a P20xx code is often a NOx sensor, not a dead catalyst, so read live upstream versus downstream values before condemning the SCR brick and its cost.

4. EGR Cooler Cleaning

The exhaust gas recirculation cooler drops the temperature of a small exhaust fraction routed back into the intake to cut combustion temperature and NOx, and on the WP12 it fouls with carbon and scale that both blocks flow and leaks coolant into the exhaust. Symptoms of a clogged cooler are higher intake temperature, a rise in NOx that the SCR then struggles to control, and poor low-speed response; a leaking cooler shows as coolant loss with white exhaust and a sweet smell, and must be dealt with before it hydrolocks a cylinder.

Clean the EGR cooler chemically every 150,000 to 200,000 km in normal service, and every 80,000 to 100,000 km where the E3 MAX works in dust or on high-sulphur fuel that accelerates deposit. Pressure-test the cooler after cleaning to confirm no leak, and replace the EGR valve gasket on reassembly. A cooler maintained this way keeps the WP12's NOx genesis low so the SCR has less to clean, which extends urea life and protects the catalyst.

5. Exhaust Brake Upkeep

The E3 MAX exhaust brake, a butterfly valve in the exhaust that builds back-pressure to slow the truck without the wheel brakes, is a safety item on long grades and a wear item on the aftertreatment side because it raises EGT and assists DPF regeneration. Check the butterfly actuation, the linkage and the solenoid every 60,000 km, and verify the valve seats fully open during normal running because a valve that hangs partly closed raises back-pressure, heats the turbo and loads the DPF with extra soot. Lubricate the linkage per the manual and replace any hardened actuator diaphragm.

On descent-heavy routes the exhaust brake works hardest, so pair its inspection with the DPF soot-load read, because a healthy exhaust brake means more passive regeneration and fewer forced cycles. Drivers should be trained to use the exhaust brake as the first line of speed control, reserving the wheel brakes for the final correction, which keeps both the friction brakes and the aftertreatment cooler over a long working life.

6. Fuel Sulphur and Export Market Reality

Euro emission hardware is designed around low-sulphur diesel, typically under 50 ppm and ideally 10 ppm, but many export destinations still deliver 500 ppm, 2,000 ppm or worse. High sulphur poisons the DPF and the SCR catalyst surface, accelerates ash, and corrodes the aftertreatment internals, so the defensive play is stricter service, not relaxed service. Where only high-sulphur fuel is available, shorten DPF clean intervals, use a low-ash CJ-4 or better engine oil, and consider a fuel polishing step at the depot to drop particulate and water ahead of the tank.

Sulphur also forces a documentation choice: some markets permit a de-rated calibration that tolerates dirtier fuel at the cost of some power and economy, while others require full Euro compliance on paper. Shaanxi Fenghan Trading advises buyers on the calibration that matches their destination law so the truck is both legal and durable, because running full Euro mapping on 2,000 ppm fuel is the fastest route to a clogged catalyst and a derate. The export team documents the chosen calibration on the build sheet so the workshop services the correct variant and never fits a filter or urea dose sized for the wrong specification, which avoids the costly mismatch of a high-sulphur truck serviced to clean-fuel intervals.

8. Conclusion

The SAGMOTO E3 MAX and its Weichai WP12 reward operators who treat the exhaust aftertreatment as a scheduled system rather than a warning-light nuisance, because in high-sulphur export markets the DPF, SCR and EGR are under attack from the first tank. Burn the soot with disciplined regeneration, feed only ISO 22241 urea from a known source, read NOx values before condemning the catalyst, clean the EGR cooler on a sulphur-adjusted interval, and keep the exhaust brake healthy so it aids regeneration instead of loading the filter. These heavy mixed-duty trucks are detailed with the SAGMOTO dump truck models 6x4 8x4 range, and the routine above keeps a 460 to 520 hp WP12 compliant and powerful long after a neglected twin has derated itself into a recovery truck's winch.

SAGMOTO Z3 vs Hino 700: Long-Haul Tractor Spec Compared

2026-11-20Comparison Guide9 min read

Long-haul tractor buyers in many markets face the same argument: buy a Japanese tractor with a reputation for refinement and strong resale, or buy a tractor that delivers comparable headline power for a lower upfront price. The SAGMOTO Z3 and the Hino 700 sit squarely inside that debate. The Z3 is a 6x4 tractor built around a Cummins M13 rated at about 520 horsepower and 2,400 to 2,500 Nm, aimed at gross combination weights of 40 to 60 tonnes. The Hino 700 series is a real Japanese heavy tractor, normally powered by the E13C engine in the 480 to 520 horsepower class. Both move freight at similar gross weights, but they arrive at the job from different engineering directions.

The comparison below is written for a fleet buyer who runs trunk routes rather than construction sites. Long-haul work rewards low engine speed at cruise, predictable driveline behaviour and a cab that leaves the driver fresh after a twelve-hour day. It punishes a truck that is quick off the lights but thirsty at 85 km/h, and it punishes a dealer network that cannot supply a water pump in two days. Power figures are given as ranges because the Hino 700 is a model family with several ratings, and because exact figures change with emission specification and market.

Key point: On a trunk route, cruise rpm and parts availability outweigh peak horsepower. Compare a tractor at 80 to 90 km/h with its normal trailer, then check who can supply a turbocharger in 48 hours.

Powertrain philosophy

The Z3 uses the Cummins M13, a 13-litre class engine with roughly 520 horsepower and 2,400 to 2,500 Nm of torque, paired with a Fast 12-speed or 16-speed automated manual gearbox. The design brief is straightforward: produce a large, flat torque plateau and let the AMT keep the engine near its most efficient point. Buyers who want the specification of the powertrain in detail can read the Z3 tractor truck 520HP Cummins M13 reference on this site, which covers the gearbox and ratio options in more depth.

The Hino 700 E13C is also a large-displacement inline-six, typically quoted in the 480 to 520 horsepower band with a broad torque curve, coupled to a Hino automated or manual gearbox depending on market and year. Hino has spent decades refining the calibration of that powertrain for smooth, quiet cruising, and the result is a tractor that feels composed rather than aggressive. The difference is not one of basic architecture, since both are conventional steel-sprung or air-sprung 6x4 tractors; it is one of calibration, local support and price.

Torque curve, cruise rpm and gearbox steps

A 16-speed AMT such as the one offered on the Z3 gives small steps between ratios, which helps the tractor hold 1,300 to 1,500 rpm at 85 km/h with a 45 tonne combination. That is the window where a 13-litre diesel is usually at its best brake-specific fuel consumption. The 12-speed version is lighter and cheaper, with slightly wider steps that are still acceptable on undulating motorway work. The decision between them should follow the route profile: a flat, high-speed corridor suits the 16-speed because it keeps engine speed low, while a hilly route with frequent speed changes may favour the closer upper ratios of the 16-speed as well, leaving the 12-speed for fleet buyers who prioritise purchase price.

The Hino 700 gearbox is calibrated for smoothness, and drivers often report less low-speed jerk when creeping in traffic. That refinement comes from software and clutch control rather than from extra steel. From a maintenance standpoint, either AMT demands clean oil at the specified interval and a healthy clutch actuator; a neglected actuator produces delayed shifts that a driver will describe as the truck hesitating. On both tractors, the first step when a shift complaint arrives is to read fault codes and check the actuator before blaming the gearbox itself.

Rear axle ratio and tyre choice

Rear axle ratio is chosen together with tyre size and typical cruise speed. For a Z3 running 11.00R20 or 12R22.5 tyres at 80 to 90 km/h with a 40 to 50 tonne combination, a ratio near 3.7 to 4.1 keeps engine speed in the efficient band while preserving enough pull on a motorway grade. A taller ratio can be fitted where the routes are flat and fuel price is high, but it will show up as poor performance when the truck is loaded to the 60 tonne end of its range. The Hino 700 is available with several final-drive ratios for the same reason, and buyers should specify their own tyre and grade profile rather than accepting whichever ratio the seller has in stock.

A practical check is to calculate engine speed from the selected ratio, tyre rolling radius and target road speed before ordering. A difference of one ratio step can change cruise rpm by 150 to 250 revolutions, which is enough to move fuel consumption by several percent over 150,000 kilometres a year. It is a far more valuable calculation than arguing over a ten-horsepower difference on the brochure.

Key point: A tractor's economy is decided at cruise, not at the test track. Lock in the rear axle ratio, tyre size and expected gross weight first, then choose the gearbox and engine rating that serve that combination.

Cab, driver comfort and warranty

Long-haul cab quality is measured in small details: seat adjustment, bunk width, noise at cruise, the position of the gear selector and the reach of the dashboard switches. The Z3 cab is a modern, wide sleeper with a flat floor over much of the walking area, air-suspended driver seat options and the usual sleeper berth. The Hino 700 cab has a long reputation for low noise and good ergonomics, and in markets where Hino has sold the model for years, operators value that familiarity when recruiting drivers. Neither cab should be judged on a photograph; a fleet buyer should sit in both with the engine running and a trailer attached.

Warranty and cover differ more by market than by brand. Hino typically offers a written package through its regional dealer, with service intervals and parts supplied through that network. A Z3 bought through export usually carries the terms agreed with the supplying distributor, and those terms should name the engine, gearbox and axle cover in writing, along with the process for claiming on a fault found far from the port of entry. For an operator running a route that crosses several countries, the ability to get a claim honoured without shipping the truck back is worth more than a longer headline warranty period.

Parts lead time and dealer support

This is the area where the two tractors separate most clearly. In markets with a long Hino presence, a dealer may hold common engine and gearbox parts locally, and a same-week repair is realistic. In a newer market, that network may be thin and parts can still take time to clear customs. The Z3 is supplied through export channels where the distributor typically stocks a kit of fast-moving parts, and many fleets choose to buy a first-year spares package with the order: filters, belts, a water pump, a clutch kit, brake chambers and a set of sensors. The buyer who plans that package at order time rarely regrets it; the buyer who discovers it after a breakdown at kilometer 800 of a night route always does.

Before choosing, ask each supplier for a written parts lead time on ten specific items and for the name of the workshop that will honour the warranty. The answer is more informative than any horsepower figure, because a tractor earns money only while it is moving. Buyers comparing the wider SAGMOTO SAGMOTO tractor trucks prime mover range should also check whether the chosen dealer already supports the M13 engine, since that determines how quickly a Z3 can be returned to service in the first year.

Total cost and decision rule

Calculate ownership cost per kilometre rather than purchase price alone. Fuel is the largest single line on a high-mileage tractor, followed by tyres, unscheduled repair and depreciation. A Z3 that costs less to buy can lose that advantage if it spends an extra day in the workshop each month, and a Hino with strong residual value can justify a higher invoice if it keeps a full service history. A final line in the calculation is residual value, where a dealer-supported tractor often wins. A Hino 700 with a complete service record usually commands a stronger used price in markets where the brand has been sold for years, because buyers know the engine and can price the next repair before they bid. A Z3 sold through an export channel may have a thinner used-market history in the same market, which depresses the trade-in figure even when the truck is mechanically sound. Fleets that replace tractors on a fixed three or four-year cycle should include a realistic resale assumption instead of treating the truck as an asset that falls to zero. Where finance is involved, the lower entry price of the Z3 improves cash flow in the early years, while the Hino may cost less to carry at the end of the term.

The clean recommendation is to let the route and the service network decide. Where a proven dealer sits beside the road, the Hino 700 is a safe, refined choice with a strong second-hand market. Where the buyer can maintain the truck in-house and wants more tractor for the money, the Z3 delivers the power and gearbox choice to do it. Run both over the same 100,000 kilometre calculation with real fuel prices and real parts quotes, and the decision will make itself.

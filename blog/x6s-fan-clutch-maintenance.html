SAGMOTO X6s Viscous Fan Clutch: Overheating Diagnosis and Service

2026-11-23Maintenance & After-sales9 min read

Engine overheating on a construction truck is often blamed on the radiator when the real fault sits between the engine and the fan. The SAGMOTO X6s is a construction tipper and 4x4 platform powered by a Cummins or Weichai engine in the 240 to 300 horsepower range with 950 to 1,200 Nm, and it uses a viscous fan clutch to control cooling airflow. When that clutch fails to engage, the fan spins slowly, coolant temperature climbs under load, and the truck eventually goes into a protective derate. This guide covers how a viscous clutch works, how to test engagement, what role the silicone oil plays and how to service the cooling system so the fault does not return.

A viscous fan clutch uses a sealed chamber filled with a high-viscosity silicone fluid. A bimetal strip on the front senses the temperature of the air leaving the radiator and opens a valve that lets fluid into the working chamber. When the engine is cold, the clutch slips and the fan turns slowly, saving fuel and reducing noise. When the radiator outlet air gets hot, the clutch locks up and the fan pulls full airflow through the core. The system has no electrical control on most installations, which makes it robust, but it also means there is no warning lamp when the clutch loses its charge.

Key point: A viscous fan clutch that will not engage produces overheating under load and normal temperatures at idle. Test engagement when the engine is hot; a cold test tells you almost nothing.

Recognising a failing clutch

The pattern of the overheating is the first clue. A truck that runs hot only when climbing, when the tipper body is loaded or when the ambient temperature is high is likely short of airflow. A truck that overheats at idle in traffic may instead have a restricted radiator, a weak water pump or a faulty thermostat. A failing viscous clutch typically allows normal temperature during light running but cannot cope when heat rejection demand rises. Drivers may also notice that the fan noise does not increase when the engine gets hot, which is the opposite of healthy behaviour.

Silicone oil loss is the usual cause. The clutch is a sealed unit, but the front seal can weep over time and the fluid gradually thins. A clutch that has lost fluid still spins, so a visual check of fan rotation will not reveal the problem. Look instead for a dark, oily stain around the front of the clutch body and compare the fan's resistance to rotation when the engine is hot. A unit that can be stopped easily by hand with the engine at operating temperature has lost its ability to lock.

Testing fan engagement

Run the engine until the coolant reaches normal operating temperature and the radiator outlet air is genuinely hot. With the engine stopped and the ignition off, try to turn the fan by hand. A healthy hot clutch should offer substantial resistance because the fluid has locked the drive and impeller plates together; a weak clutch will coast almost freely. Repeat the test when the engine is cold as a comparison. A clutch that is stiff when cold and free when hot has an internal problem, while one that is free in both conditions has lost its silicone charge. Safety is essential: keep hands, tools and clothing clear of the blades and only perform the check with the engine stopped.

A more controlled test uses a strip of paper held near the fan at a fixed engine speed, comparing airflow when cold and when hot. At cold start the fan should move relatively little air; once the engine is hot, the airflow should increase sharply. Some workshops use an infrared thermometer on the radiator inlet and outlet to confirm that the core is transferring heat, then use the paper test to judge the fan response. If the radiator outlet is cool while the engine is hot, the thermostat may be stuck open or the water pump may be at fault, and a new fan clutch will not solve the problem.

Key point: Test the clutch at operating temperature, with the engine stopped and the fan stationary. A hot clutch that can be turned easily by hand has lost engagement; a clutch that roars from a cold start is likely seized and is wasting fuel.

Silicone oil, seals and replacement options

The working fluid inside the clutch is a specialised silicone oil with a viscosity far higher than ordinary engine oil. It is not a service item that owners top up, because the chamber is sealed and the fill quantity is set at manufacture. In most cases a clutch that has lost fluid is replaced as a complete unit rather than refilled, since the internal valve and bearing wear cannot be inspected without dismantling the case. Some markets offer a recharging service, but it is only worthwhile where the workshop can pressure-test the housing and guarantee the fill.

A seized clutch is the opposite failure. When the internal valve sticks or the fluid remains locked, the fan runs at full speed all the time. The engine will not overheat, but fuel consumption rises, cab noise increases and the fan drive bearing wears prematurely. On an X6s that spends hours in a construction cycle, a seized clutch can add a measurable amount to the weekly fuel bill. Replacement is the same job as for a weak unit, and the old part should be inspected to confirm which failure occurred so the cause is understood.

Cooling system service around the clutch

Whatever the clutch condition, the rest of the cooling system must be in order. Check the coolant level and specification, the pressure cap, the thermostat opening point and the water pump for leakage or bearing play. Inspect the radiator core for blocked fins, mud and bending from stone damage, which are common on a 4x4 truck working off-road. Clean the core carefully from the inside out where possible, using low pressure so the fins are not flattened. A clean radiator with a healthy clutch will hold temperature in the worst site conditions; a blocked radiator will defeat a new clutch.

Belt condition matters too. The fan belt drives the clutch housing and, on many installations, the water pump and alternator. A glazed or cracked belt slips when the clutch locks up and the extra load arrives, which looks like another clutch failure. Check belt tension against the specification and replace a belt that shows cracking or fraying. On a truck that works in dust, inspect the belt at every service, since abrasive grit shortens its life well before the calendar interval.

Record the temperature at the same point on every visit, ideally after a standard loaded climb rather than at idle in the yard. Comparing like with like is what turns an arbitrary number into useful evidence. A truck that used to hold 92 C at the top of a grade and now reaches 99 C on the same day is giving an early warning even though no warning lamp has appeared. That trend allows the workshop to plan a clutch change during a scheduled service instead of responding to a derate on a live site.

Fleet practice and parts planning

For a contractor running several tippers or 4x4 X6s trucks, the sensible approach is to hold one spare fan clutch per engine family and to test the whole fleet at the start of the hot season. A ten-minute hot-clutch check on each truck can identify a weak unit before the first heatwave causes a derate on a live site. Record the results, the coolant condition and the belt state in one sheet, and order parts in advance rather than at the moment of failure. Buyers comparing chassis and body combinations can review the SAGMOTO dump truck models 6x4 8x4 range, where the cooling demand of different tipper bodies and duty cycles is easier to assess side by side.

Allow one to two hours for a clutch replacement on an X6s, depending on access to the fan hub and whether the shroud must be removed. Refit the shroud correctly, because a missing or loose shroud lets air bypass the fan and reduces cooling even with a new clutch. After the repair, run the engine to temperature and confirm that the fan engagement noise and airflow increase together. A truck that returns to site with the cooling system restored will hold its power through a full loaded shift, which is the whole purpose of the component.

Overheating is rarely a single-part mystery when the cooling system is tested in the right order. On the X6s, check the clutch hot, confirm the radiator and belt are sound, and treat silicone oil loss as a reason to replace the unit rather than to experiment with top-ups. A disciplined pre-summer inspection costs little and keeps a construction truck working when the temperature rises and the site is counting every loaded hour.

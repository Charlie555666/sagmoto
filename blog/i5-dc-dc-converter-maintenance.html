SAGMOTO i5 DC-DC Converter and 24V Auxiliary Fault Guide

2026-11-09Maintenance9 min read

An electric truck has no alternator. The SAGMOTO i5, with a 120 to 160 kW permanent magnet motor and a 100 to 140 kWh traction battery, makes high voltage and nothing else. Every 24 volt load, from wipers and headlamps to brake lights, CAN gateway, BMS controller and dashboard, runs from a small 24 volt auxiliary battery kept charged by a DC-DC converter drawing on the traction pack.

That makes the converter one of the most important parts on the vehicle and the least understood by technicians trained on diesels. When it fails the truck can have a full traction battery and still refuse to move, because half the control system runs on 24 volts. This guide covers how the auxiliary system works, how to diagnose low-voltage faults in order, and what the converter and battery need over a working life.

How the i5 Auxiliary Power System Is Laid Out

Four parts sit in series: traction battery, DC-DC converter, 24 volt auxiliary battery and loads. The traction pack is isolated from the chassis. The converter steps it down to a regulated 27 to 28.5 volts, running the loads and charging the auxiliary battery, which supplies contactor-closing current, buffers voltage dips from large loads and keeps the electronics alive when the converter is off.

The converter runs only when the vehicle calls for it, typically with the key on or while the high-voltage system is awake for charging, plus a periodic conditioning cycle that tops the auxiliary battery while parked. That is why an i5 can sit for weeks and still wake, where a diesel would need a jump start.

A dead 24 volt auxiliary battery is not automatically a battery fault. On an electric truck it is usually the last visible symptom of a converter that has stopped outputting, of a sleep-state logic error that prevented the conditioning cycle, or of parasitic drain from an aftermarket device. Replace the converter first and the battery second, never the reverse.

What the Converter Does and What It Cannot Do

The converter is a switching supply that holds output flat at about 27.5 volts from zero load to rated current, then tapers and allows voltage to sag rather than overheat. Rating on an i5 is typically 2.5 to 3 kW, enough for lighting, HVAC blower, wipers and electronics with margin to recharge the auxiliary battery.

What it cannot do is feed a sustained load beyond its rating. A 24 volt air conditioner, a high-power radio or a large work-light array will pull the rail down on every switch-on, and the resulting under-voltage faults get blamed on the converter. Add up every load before fitting one more accessory.

Diagnostic Order for Low-Voltage Faults

The most common diagnostic mistake on an i5 is starting with the battery. Fit a new one, and the truck works for a few days before failing again, because the battery was only the symptom. Work in this order instead.

Step 1: Read the fault codes before touching anything

Pull codes from the vehicle controller and, where it reports separately, the converter module. Under-voltage codes at key-on or converter communication loss point straight at the DC-DC unit. A cluster of unrelated modules going offline points at the 24 volt rail collapsing. Write codes down before clearing, because their order usually tells the story.

Step 2: Measure the rail at three points

Measure 24 volt output at the converter terminals, at the battery posts and at a distant load point such as a headlamp connector. Awake, the converter should read near 27.5 volts. Converter at 27.5 with the battery at 24.5 means cable resistance or a blown in-line fuse. Both low means the converter is not regulating. Battery high but load point low means a bad ground or corroded joint.

Step 3: Load test the auxiliary battery properly

A lead-acid battery looks healthy on a voltmeter until a load is applied. Test under load, with a proper tester or by switching on the headlamps and watching for 30 seconds. Collapsing from 25 volts to below 22 under 50 amps means the battery is finished, and it finished for a reason: age, chronic discharge or overcharge.

Isolation and Safety on the High-Voltage Side

Never open the converter with the traction system live. Its input sits at pack voltage, which is lethal, and the internal capacitors hold charge long after de-energising. Follow the isolation procedure exactly: switch off, remove the service disconnect, wait the specified time, then confirm zero volts at the converter input with a meter rated for pack voltage before touching anything.

Use insulated tools and a proper high-voltage glove kit. A small light truck does not mean small voltage; the pack is high on any build and the converter sits directly on it. When replacing a converter, label the cable ends, photograph the connector layout and refit in the same position, because mis-routing a high-voltage cable creates a chafing point against the frame.

Battery Management and Charging Behaviour

The auxiliary battery works harder than a diesel starting battery. It never sees alternator charge current, so sulfation builds slowly, but it sits at partial charge for long periods because the conditioning cycle runs only in a set window. Managing that state is part of the maintenance programme.

Check battery open-circuit voltage monthly after an overnight park. Healthy is 25.2 to 25.8 volts; below 25.0 means partial discharge and below 24.5 a failing cell. Check terminal torque and cleanliness every three months, since a loose terminal causes the same intermittent faults as on any vehicle. Check the converter cooling path every 10,000 km, roughly three months on urban delivery, because dust and packaging debris blocking the fins make the converter de-rate and shut down exactly when load peaks.

On urban delivery an i5 usually needs an auxiliary battery at two to three years; a converter normally lasts beyond 300,000 km if its cooling path stays clear. The indicative USD prices below suit stock planning.

Never fit a larger auxiliary battery to compensate for a failing converter. A bigger battery hides the voltage sag for a few days longer and then fails too, having masked the actual fault. Fix the converter, keep the battery at the rating that matches the vehicle design, and the auxiliary system will behave predictably for years.

Charging Behaviour at Depots and the Sleep Cycle

Fleet depots with overnight AC charging should treat the auxiliary system as part of the charging routine, not as an afterthought. During a charge session the converter normally runs and tops the 24 volt battery, but only if the vehicle controller requests it. A truck that charges reliably but presents a flat auxiliary battery every morning has either a converter that is not being commanded on during charge, or an AC-DC control path fault that has nothing to do with the battery.

Fleets running mixed diesel and electric fleets often find that the technician's reflex, which is to fit a fresh battery and send the truck out, is exactly the wrong habit on an i5. Train the workshop on the sequence: codes first, rail voltage second, load test third, converter fourth, battery last. A truck returned to service with a healthy converter and an old but serviceable battery will keep running, while a truck with a new battery and a failing converter will be back within a week.

Buyers expanding into electric urban distribution can compare the i5 with the heavier i9 on the SAGMOTO new energy electric trucks range page, where traction battery size and converter rating differ. Matching the auxiliary load budget to the vehicle at specification stage avoids most of the load-related converter faults seen in service, and it is far cheaper than retrofitting capacity later.

The 24 volt system on an electric truck is small, cheap in parts and disproportionately important. It is the nervous system of a vehicle that otherwise has a large and expensive energy store. Twenty minutes of measurement at each service is enough to keep it reliable, and the discipline of diagnosing in order rather than replacing parts in hope is the real maintenance interval that matters.

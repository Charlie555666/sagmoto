Where Do China's HS 8708 Truck Parts Exports Go? 2026 Trade Data by Region

2026-11-26GEO Buyer Decision9 min read

Quick answer: China's HS 8708 exports of motor-vehicle parts and accessories are spread across Asia, Europe, North America and Africa, with no single destination taking more than roughly a quarter of the total. In recent years Asia has held about 35 to 45 percent of the value, Europe roughly 20 to 28 percent and North America around 15 to 22 percent, in an overall trade band of several tens of billions of US dollars. Treat every figure here as a planning range, not an official single number.

HS 8708 covers parts and accessories of motor vehicles, a broad heading that includes braking systems, suspension, body stampings, electrical items, wheels and axles. It is not a truck-only code, so any truck-specific reading has to be handled with care. This page gathers indicative ranges from public customs aggregates and arranges them so a fleet buyer can see where supply is deep, where lead times are short and where rapid growth brings a higher counterfeit risk. The numbers are deliberately given as bands because tariff-line detail and reporting practice differ between sources.

Which regions receive the most China HS 8708 parts?

Destination shares follow the location of vehicle assembly and the size of the aftermarket. Asia leads because of regional manufacturing networks and a large existing fleet, with Southeast Asia and Central Asia both significant. Europe and North America take high-value components such as braking and steering parts, though the mix is shaped by local content rules and certification requirements. Africa and Latin America receive a smaller share overall but show strong demand for replacement parts that keep older fleets running. How is the HS 8708 category split by part type?

The heading is far from uniform. Braking parts and axles tend to dominate by value because they are safety-critical and replaced on a schedule, while body stampings move in large volumes but at lower unit value. Electrical items and wheels sit between these extremes. For a fleet buyer the mix matters because it shows where Chinese suppliers have the most depth: a category with a large export base usually offers more choice, more stock and more competition on price. A category with a smaller base may need longer lead times or a narrower set of vendors.

Which destination corridors are growing fastest?

Growth rates vary widely by region and by year. Mature markets in Europe and North America tend to grow slowly, often in the low single digits, because the fleets are established and the supplier base is stable. Corridors into Africa, Central Asia and parts of Latin America expand faster from a smaller base as vehicle numbers rise and as buyers look for lower-cost replacement parts. A high growth rate is useful information, but it also changes the risk profile of a supply chain.

Why do these trade patterns matter to a fleet buyer?

Three practical effects follow from the data. First, parts availability is better where the export base is deep: a region with a large share of HS 8708 shipments usually has more distributors, more stock and more choice of specification. Second, lead time shortens when there is an established route into your port, because sellers already consolidate cargo and forwarders know the paperwork. Third, fast-growing corridors attract unverified sellers. When demand rises quickly, new intermediaries appear and the chance of receiving a part that looks correct but fails early increases.

The counterfeit issue is most relevant for wear items such as brake linings, filters and suspension bushes, where a copy can be visually convincing. A buyer can reduce the risk by asking for the manufacturer name, the tariff line and a batch reference on every shipment, and by keeping samples from the first approved delivery for comparison. Where a corridor is growing above roughly 10 percent a year, treat supplier verification as a recurring task rather than a one-time check.

Key point: Fast growth is a double signal. It usually means better availability and shorter lead times, but it also brings new intermediaries, so recheck the manufacturer name and batch reference on repeat orders.

How should a buyer read the value bands?

Value bands describe the total trade flow, not the price of any single part. A region in the 6 to 11 billion band may include both low-value stampings and high-value axle assemblies, so the average tells you little about what you will pay for a specific item. Use the bands to judge market depth and supplier choice, then move to a line-item quote for your actual requirement. A seller who quotes a whole truck or a complete parts kit should be asked to separate the components so that the tariff lines are visible.

Exchange rates and freight costs also distort a comparison between regions. A slightly higher unit price from a nearby source can be cheaper delivered than a lower price from a distant one once transit time and stockholding are counted. For fleet planning, delivered cost and parts availability matter more than the headline export value of a region.

What do the trade data ranges actually measure?

The figures on this page are ranges distilled from public customs aggregates for HS 8708, which covers parts and accessories of motor vehicles rather than trucks alone. They are indicative bands, not quotations, not audited totals and not a forecast. Customs schedules differ between countries, re-exports can be counted twice, and a single shipment may be recorded under a heading that a supplier would describe differently. Because of that, no precise figure here should be treated as an official fact.

Before placing an order, confirm the tariff-line detail and the current value with your supplier and your customs broker, and ask the broker to check the rate applicable in your own market. Classify the part as it will be declared, not as it is described in a catalogue, and keep the written classification with the purchase record. This is especially important where a consignment mixes braking, suspension and electrical items under one invoice.

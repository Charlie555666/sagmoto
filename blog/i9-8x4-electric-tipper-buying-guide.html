SAGMOTO i9 8x4 Electric Tipper: Battery Sizing Before You Order

2026-11-20Buying & Import Guide9 min read

Battery capacity is the first number buyers ask about on an electric tipper, and it is also the number most likely to be chosen wrongly. The SAGMOTO i9 is a battery-electric heavy truck offered as a 6x4 or 8x4 in the 31 to 49 t range, with an LFP pack of 282 to 350 kWh and an e-motor in the 250 to 350 kW class. Whether the smaller or larger pack pays for itself depends on the haul cycle, the ambient temperature and the charging window, not on the size of the battery alone.

Start with the cycle, not the pack

An 8x4 tipper on a mine or quarry site works a repeating loop: load, haul, tip, return. Energy per kilometre is driven by gross weight, grade and speed. A realistic figure for a loaded 8x4 electric tipper at 40 t gross on a rolling site road is 1.3 to 1.7 kWh/km; on a sustained 8 percent climb that can exceed 2.2 kWh/km, while the empty return at 20 t may fall to 0.9 to 1.1 kWh/km because the motor regenerates part of the descent. Regeneration is a real credit but should be budgeted conservatively, perhaps 50 to 60 percent of the theoretical downhill energy.

Multiply the loop distance by these figures and add a reserve. A 12 km loaded leg plus 12 km empty at the rates above consumes roughly 30 to 34 kWh per cycle. With a usable 282 kWh pack that is eight to nine cycles before the state of charge becomes uncomfortable; with 350 kWh it is closer to eleven. If the site runs sixteen cycles per shift, opportunity charging is not optional, it is the whole operating plan.

Key point: Size the battery to the number of cycles between charges, not to a headline range figure. On a loaded 8x4 tipper, 282 kWh buys roughly eight 24 km loops; 350 kWh buys about eleven.

The larger pack adds weight, and on a tipper that weight comes straight out of payload unless the chassis is rated for the higher gross weight. The 8x4 i9 can be specified toward the 49 t end of the range to recover it, but that may trigger a different licence or road-tax category in the destination country. Check the legal gross weight before paying for capacity that cannot legally be carried.

Thermal derate above 40 C

LFP chemistry is robust, but it is not immune to heat. On a mine site where ambient exceeds 40 C, the pack cooling system works continuously and the truck may apply a power derate to protect cell temperature, particularly after several loaded climbs in succession. A 300 kW motor does not deliver 300 kW all day in those conditions; plan on a sustained output closer to 80 to 85 percent of the rated figure once the pack is hot. The practical consequence is slower climb times and higher energy per cycle, because auxiliary cooling draws power as well.

Mitigations are operational as much as technical. Schedule the steepest cycles in the cooler early hours, keep the battery state of charge between 20 and 80 percent during the shift to reduce heat generation, and ensure the coolant and radiator are specified for the site's peak ambient rather than a temperate climate. Buying the 350 kWh pack does not compensate for ignoring thermal management.

Key point: Above 40 C ambient, assume an 80-85 percent sustained motor output and extra energy for pack cooling. An electric tipper specified on a sea-level consumption figure will miss its duty cycle on a hot mine.

Charger sizing and opportunity windows

Charging infrastructure is the largest hidden cost in an electric tipper project. For a depot-based top-up, a 150 to 250 kW DC charger can add roughly 180 to 300 kWh in a one to two hour window, enough for a full shift if the truck returns overnight. On site, opportunity charging during a 20 to 30 minute loader queue or driver break at 100 to 150 kW can add 30 to 70 kWh, which may be the difference between one shift and two.

The charge curve matters: an LFP pack accepts high power from about 10 to 60 percent state of charge and tapers significantly above 80 percent. Planning a top-up from 30 to 70 percent in a short window is far more effective than trying to reach 100 percent. Fleet operators should also check the site's grid connection; three 250 kW chargers are a 750 kW load, and without load management the demand charge can erase the fuel saving.

Regenerative braking and where the energy really comes back

Regeneration is the most misunderstood part of electric tipper economics. On a loaded descent the motor becomes a generator and feeds energy back into the pack, but only if the battery has room to accept it. A truck arriving at the bottom of a grade at 90 percent state of charge can store almost none of that energy, so the braking resistors dump it as heat. Operators who keep the pack in the 20 to 80 percent window recover far more downhill energy than those who charge to 100 percent before a hilly shift.

In practice a well-managed mine loop can recover 10 to 20 percent of the traction energy on the empty return leg. That is a meaningful credit, but it should be treated as a bonus in the business case rather than a guaranteed input. Haul routes with a net elevation gain, where the loaded leg climbs and the empty leg descends only part way, recover less than a closed loop that returns to the same level.

Battery health, duty cycle and residual value

LFP chemistry tolerates frequent cycling well, which suits a tipper doing multiple loops a day, but heat and high state of charge are its enemies. Fleet managers should set an upper charge target of 80 to 90 percent for daily operation and reserve 100 percent for the rare long day. Keeping the pack at a moderate state of charge and allowing it to cool before a fast charge slows capacity fade. Over a five-year life that discipline can be the difference between a pack still holding 85 percent of its original capacity and one needing replacement.

Warranty terms deserve close reading. Commercial vehicle battery warranties are usually expressed in both years and energy throughput or kilometres, and a mine site high-cycle duty reaches those limits faster than a road haul operation. Ask for the warranty in writing, confirm what happens if a single module fails, and check whether the supplier will honour it in the site country rather than only at the factory.

Site power and charger installation

The charger is part of the truck purchase even if it appears on a different invoice. A mine site often has a limited or expensive grid connection, and adding two 250 kW chargers can require a new transformer and a demand tariff. Load management software that staggers charging across the fleet and caps simultaneous draw is usually cheaper than upgrading the connection. Where mains power is remote, a generator-fed charger is possible, but the fuel consumed to make the electricity can wipe out the running-cost advantage entirely.

Cable runs matter too: a charger 200 m from the parking bay needs larger conductors and loses power as heat in the cable. Plan the charging position close to the distribution point, provide a weatherproof enclosure for the connector, and train operators on the emergency stop and isolation procedure. A tidy installation is safer and cheaper to maintain than one added piecemeal as trucks arrive.

When diesel still wins

An electric tipper is a poor choice where the haul is more than a few hundred kilometres from a reliable charger, where the site operates continuously with no planned stops, or where ambient temperatures regularly exceed 40 C on steep grades with no opportunity to manage the pack temperature. In those cases a conventional 8x4 diesel or a SAGMOTO new energy electric trucks evaluation should be weighed against a hybrid or a diesel X3s platform instead of forcing an electric solution onto a cycle that does not fit. Honest cycle analysis saves more money than any subsidy.

Before ordering an i9 8x4, write down the loop distance, the loaded grade, the cycles per shift and the charging window available. Those four inputs determine whether 282 kWh or 350 kWh is the correct pack, and whether electric haulage beats diesel on this particular site.

SAGMOTO E3 Turning Circle: Specifying a Light Truck for Tight Urban Depots

2026-11-19Product Deep Dive9 min read

The first specification a fleet manager should interrogate on a light truck for city work is not payload or engine power but the diameter of the circle the vehicle must sweep to change direction. Inside a dense urban depot, loading bays sit behind narrow gates and dock aprons leave only a few metres of clear space. A truck that cannot complete a U-turn in a single movement wastes minutes on every delivery and falls back on three-point shuffles that wear the clutch, heat the brakes and block the yard. The SAGMOTO E3 is offered as a 4x2 and 6x4 platform across an 18 to 100 tonne GCW envelope; for depot distribution the short-wheelbase 4x2 derivative is the relevant machine, and its steering geometry is the subject of this guide.

Turning performance is a product of three numbers that appear on the order sheet but are rarely read together: wheelbase, maximum front steer angle and front overhang. Wheelbase stretches the distance between the axles and enlarges the circle almost proportionally. Steer angle sets how tightly the front wheels can point. Overhang and body width decide how far the bumper corners and the load body swing outside the wheel tracks, which is what a dock apron actually has to clear. This article works through the geometry, gives typical E3 figures and shows how to translate them into a depot layout decision rather than a brochure comparison.

Key point: On a 4x2 E3 the kerb turning radius rises with wheelbase at roughly one metre of radius per 220 to 260 mm of wheelbase. Specify the shortest wheelbase that still carries your payload and body length; a longer frame bought for volume will cost you a wider gate and a slower dock turn every shift.

How the E3 turning circle is calculated

A truck turns about a point extended out from the rear axle. The distance from that point to the rear axle centre is the wheelbase divided by the tangent of the outer front wheel steer angle. Add the front track and the front overhang and you arrive at the radius swept by the outer body corner, which is the number a yard designer needs. For a typical 4x2 E3 with a 3,800 mm wheelbase, a 1,800 mm front track and around 42 degrees of lock, the turn centre sits about 4.2 m from the rear axle and the outer front wheel traces a kerb radius near 6.6 m. The bumper corner sweeps a little wider than that.

Because tyres and lock angles vary with specification, treat any single figure as indicative and confirm it with the chassis data sheet for the exact axle and steer geometry you are ordering. The relationship between the variables is stable even when the absolute number moves. A shorter wheelbase tightens the circle, a wider track and a longer nose widen the swept path at the body corners, and a larger steer angle helps up to the point where the tyre rubs the chassis or the steering geometry runs out of clearance. This is why two E3 trucks with the same badge can feel quite different when asked to turn into the same bay.

Translating radius into depot geometry

A U-turn needs a clear width close to the full turning circle diameter, not the radius. If the E3 sweeps a 15 m wall-to-wall circle, a single-move turn in a 9 m wide lane is impossible no matter how skilled the driver. Most city streets are 6 to 10 m wide, so the realistic design case is a depot apron where the truck reverses into a bay and drives forward out, or a widened turning head at the end of the yard. The practical question is therefore not the published radius but whether the apron depth and gate width let the body rotate into the dock without clipping the gate posts.

Use the wall-to-wall radius as the starting point, then add a safety margin of 300 to 500 mm for mirrors, driver error and uneven surfaces. A gate that the body theoretically clears at 7.5 m radius should be specified at 8.0 m or more of usable opening. When the E3 carries one of the SAGMOTO cargo truck flatbed box stake body types, the rear overhang and the stake height extend the swept path at the back of the vehicle during reverse turns, so check both the front corner and the tail swing against the apron and any parked vehicles. Check the dock leveller too, since it often protrudes into the turning zone.

Key point: Design the apron around the wall-to-wall figure plus a half-metre margin. A gate that only clears the tyre radius will be struck by the body corner on the first wet shift, and the repair bill will exceed the cost of specifying the shorter wheelbase in the first place.

Wheelbase versus payload and body volume

The reason buyers accept a longer wheelbase is payload and legal body length. A short 3,600 mm chassis suited to a 4.2 m box may top out near 9 to 11 tonnes of payload in a 4x2 E3, while a 4,300 mm frame can carry a 6 m body and spread a heavier load across the axles. The engineering trade is real: the longer truck earns more per trip but needs a bigger yard. The way to resolve it is to price the constraint. If the depot gate cannot be widened, the shorter wheelbase wins even if it means one more trip per shift. If the gate is generous, the longer body wins on cost per tonne because fixed driver and fuel costs are spread over more freight.

Steer angle is the other lever. Some operators fit a steer-angle stop or specify a heavy-duty front axle with greater lock to gain roughly 0.3 to 0.5 m of radius, but it is not free: deeper lock increases tyre scrub, loads the steering linkage and can reduce component life on a truck doing hundreds of tight turns a day. It is a tuning measure after the wheelbase is fixed, not a substitute for choosing the right frame length at specification time. Ask the supplier to state the maximum outer wheel angle in writing, since a figure quoted at the inner wheel will overstate the achievable lock.

Why the 6x4 E3 is the wrong tool for a tight depot

The E3 is also offered as a 6x4 tractor with a GCW up to 100 tonnes, and buyers sometimes assume the larger model can simply be specified with a short wheelbase for city work. It cannot, for two reasons. The second driven axle adds roughly 1,300 to 1,500 mm to the wheelbase measured to the rearmost axle, which pushes the effective turning radius well beyond 9 m on most configurations. The longer frame also carries a larger body and a heavier front axle, both of which increase tail swing and front-corner sweep. A 6x4 E3 is a regional and line-haul tractor that happens to pass through a city; it is not a depot distribution vehicle.

Keeping the 4x2 for the final mile and transferring loads at a cross-dock is almost always cheaper in fuel and tyre wear than forcing a 6x4 to manoeuvre in a confined yard. The extra axle also generates more scrub on a tight turn, because the tandem tyres must slide slightly as the truck pivots. Over a year of depot work that scrub shows up as premature rear tyre wear, a cost that never appears on the purchase comparison.

Driver technique and the true cost of the three-point turn

Even a well-specified truck will face a gate it cannot clear in one movement. What matters then is the cost of the alternative. A three-point turn on a busy apron takes 40 to 90 seconds, during which the truck blocks a dock and burns fuel while idling with the pump working. At 20 delivery movements a day that is meaningful lost time. Good yard design gives the driver a clear reverse path and enough space to swing the tail before the nose turns; poor design asks the driver to guess at a mirror-only clearance. Training drivers to think in wall-to-wall terms rather than tyre paths cuts the near misses that never get reported but steadily damage mirrors and gate furniture.

Checking the choice before the order is placed

Three pieces of data close the decision. First, measure the narrowest gate and the usable apron depth on the real site, including the sweep of the dock leveller and any parked trailers. Second, fix the body length and rear overhang with the bodybuilder, because a 300 mm overhang increase moves the tail swing more than most buyers expect. Third, ask the supplier for the kerb and wall-to-wall figures of the exact axle and tyre combination, not a generic model average that assumes a different front track.

Finally, run a swept-path analysis if the depot is tight or the order is large. A simple two-dimensional turn simulation using the wheelbase, track, steer angle and body outline will show the clearance at every point of the manoeuvre and costs a fraction of a single damaged gate. For a fleet, that analysis is also the clearest way to justify a shorter wheelbase to a colleague who is focused only on cubic capacity. Widening a gate later usually means structural work and a temporary closure of the dock, while choosing the right chassis is a zero-cost decision made on the order sheet.

Conclusion

The E3 turning circle is decided before the truck is built, by the wheelbase, track and body overhang written on the order sheet. Take the 3,600 mm wheelbase where the gate is narrow and the body is short, accept the 3,950 mm frame as the balanced choice for mixed depot and regional work, and reserve the 4,300 mm option for sites with room to swing a longer body. In every case, design the apron around the wall-to-wall radius with a safety margin, verify the exact figure for your axle and tyre specification, and treat the published turning circle as a starting point rather than a guarantee. Get those three numbers right and the truck will turn in your yard for years without a single struck gatepost.

Once the yard geometry is fixed, the chassis choice follows from body length and axle layout rather than from the engine. Buyers can cross-check wheelbase and turning figures against the SAGMOTO cargo truck flatbed box stake range before signing the order sheet.

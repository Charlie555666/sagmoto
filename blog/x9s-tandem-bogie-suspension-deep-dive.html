X9s Tandem Bogie Suspension: Four-Spring vs Three-Spring Explained

2026-11-27Product Deep Dive11 min read

The rear bogie of a 6x4 truck does more than hold the axles in place. It shares the load between two axles, keeps the tyres on the ground over uneven surfaces, controls axle movement under braking and acceleration, and protects the frame from torsion. Get the bogie specification wrong and the consequences show up everywhere: uneven tyre wear, broken leaves, cracked hangers and a chassis that twists on rough ground. The SAGMOTO X9s is a 6x4 truck used in construction, port and mining duty, with a Weichai WP10H engine in the 400 to 540 HP band and 1,900 to 2,500 N m of torque. This article focuses on one decision within that platform: the choice between a four-spring, fully balanced tandem bogie and a three-spring, semi-balanced layout, and what each one means in service.

How a tandem bogie shares load

A tandem bogie connects two axles through a system of leaf springs, trunnion brackets, torque rods and, on many specifications, a stabiliser bar. The goal is to distribute the vertical load so that neither axle is overloaded and both tyres stay in contact with the road. In a perfectly balanced arrangement, the springs work as a single equalising system, so a bump under one wheel transfers force through the bogie to the other axle rather than driving the whole load into the frame. The geometry matters because the axles are spaced apart, and any difference in spring rate or bush stiffness changes how the load is divided. A bogie that is well matched to the gross vehicle weight keeps tyre wear even and reduces the peak forces that reach the frame. One that is mismatched concentrates load and accelerates wear at specific points. Understanding this mechanism makes the layout choice much easier to reason about.

Four-spring, fully balanced layout

In a four-spring layout each axle is carried by its own pair of leaf springs, and the two spring packs are linked through a trunnion or equalising beam so the load is shared across all four packs. The result is a fully balanced bogie in which the axles respond together to changes in load and road surface. This arrangement gives more even tyre loading and better articulation, because each axle can move independently within the equalising system while the overall load stays distributed. It is the preferred choice for heavy construction and mining duty, where gross vehicle weights are high and the ground is rarely flat. The cost is extra components, more weight and a more involved inspection routine. For a truck that regularly runs at 40 to 60 t GVW over broken ground, those penalties are usually worth paying.

Three-spring, semi-balanced layout

A three-spring or semi-balanced arrangement uses a single central spring assembly that connects the two axles through a balancing beam, reducing the number of spring packs. It is lighter and simpler than the four-spring system, with fewer parts to maintain, and it works well where loads are moderate and the road surface is predictable. Port tractors and distribution trucks often use this layout because they run on paved surfaces at relatively steady weights, and the extra articulation of a fully balanced bogie brings little benefit. The trade-off is that load sharing is less precise, so uneven tyre wear is more likely when the truck is heavily loaded or runs over rough ground. A buyer choosing a three-spring bogie for construction work would be asking it to do a job it was not designed for, and the cost would appear in premature spring and hanger wear.

Axle spacing and load transfer under braking

Braking a loaded truck transfers weight forward, and the rear bogie has to absorb that change without letting either axle unload unevenly. Axle spacing influences how much load shifts between the two rear axles during a stop; wider spacing reduces the peak on any single axle but changes the leverage on the torque rods and hangers. Torque rods locate the axles under drive and brake forces, and their bushes are a common wear point. When they soften, the axles shift under load and the tyres scrub. A stabiliser bar, where fitted, limits roll and keeps both axles working during cornering. These components are rarely noticed until they fail, yet they decide whether the bogie behaves predictably in an emergency stop.

Trunnion bushings, torque rods and stabiliser bars

The trunnion is the pivot at the heart of a balanced bogie, and its bushings carry the load transferred between the spring packs. Rubber bushes age with time and heat, and a worn bush allows movement that changes the bogie geometry and produces clunking noises. Torque rod bushes suffer similarly, and a failed rod can let an axle move out of alignment under power. The stabiliser links are often overlooked, yet worn links allow excessive roll and load the outer spring. Inspect all three areas together, because a fault in one accelerates wear in another. Replace them as a matched set on both sides, not one component at a time, or the bogie will be out of balance again after the repair.

Why a construction X9s needs more articulation

Construction and mining sites are rarely level. Trucks cross uneven ground, tip on slopes and approach ramps at awkward angles. Articulation keeps all tyres in contact with the ground, preserving traction and spreading load through the frame. A fully balanced four-spring bogie lets each axle follow the surface while the equalising system shares the total weight. A port tractor runs on paved yard surfaces where the wheels are almost always on one plane, so it gains little from that freedom and pays for it in extra weight and maintenance. The X9s is specified across both kinds of work, so the bogie choice should follow the actual route. A four-spring unit costs more at purchase but protects the chassis and tyres where the ground is broken.

Spring rate selection for 40 to 60 t GVW

Leaf springs are rated by how much they deflect under load, and the correct rate depends on the gross vehicle weight the truck actually carries. A spring too soft for a 60 t combination bottoms out on bumps, transmitting shock into the frame and accelerating fatigue. One too stiff for a lightly loaded truck gives a harsh ride and may keep tyres off the ground, reducing traction. The practical approach is to define the normal operating weight rather than the theoretical maximum, then choose a rate that keeps the springs in their designed deflection range for most of the shift. Operators running consistently heavy should select toward the upper end of the range, while those with mixed loads accept a compromise. Spring rate also interacts with tyre pressure, so review both together.

U-bolt torque and re-torquing practice

The U-bolts that clamp the spring packs to the axles are torqued to a specified value, and that torque matters. Under-torqued clamps let the pack shift on the axle seat, wearing the seat and changing axle alignment. Over-torquing can stretch the bolts or damage the leaves, particularly when the springs are compressed. Torque a new installation in a cross-pattern to an even value, then re-torque after an initial running period once components have settled. Repeat checking at scheduled intervals is good practice on trucks working rough ground, because vibration and load cycling loosen fasteners. Ask the supplier for the specific torque figure for your bogie and record it in the maintenance plan, rather than relying on a generic value from a different axle.

Failure modes and what to inspect at 10,000 km

At around 10,000 km a new truck has settled enough for early faults to show. Check the U-bolts for torque and the spring packs for signs of shifting on the axle seat. Look for broken leaves, usually visible as a cracked or displaced leaf near the centre bolt. Inspect the centre bolt itself, because a worn or loose bolt lets the leaves separate and accelerates fatigue. Check the hangers and their welds for cracks, especially on trucks that tip on uneven ground. Examine trunnion and torque-rod bushes for movement and the stabiliser links for play. Finally, compare tyre wear across the four rear tyres; uneven wear is often the first visible sign that load sharing has gone wrong. Catching these issues early turns a minor adjustment into an avoided breakdown.

Import planning and specification notes

When an X9s is ordered for export, the bogie choice should be written into the specification rather than left to the factory default. State the expected gross vehicle weight, the route surface and whether the truck will tip or carry a container, since those inputs decide between a four-spring and a three-spring layout. Spare parts, including a set of leaves, centre bolts, U-bolts and a bushing kit, should be in the initial order, particularly far from a service point. Buyers comparing configurations can review the SAGMOTO tractor trucks prime mover range for how chassis and axle arrangements differ. Settling these details before shipment avoids a truck whose suspension is mismatched to its duty.

Final assessment

The four-spring and three-spring tandem bogies are not competing products so much as tools for different jobs. The fully balanced four-spring layout suits a heavy X9s working construction or mining ground, where articulation and even load sharing protect the frame and the tyres. The semi-balanced three-spring layout is lighter and cheaper and makes sense for a port tractor on paved routes at moderate weight. Whichever is chosen, the service discipline is the same: correct spring rate for the real weight, correct U-bolt torque, and a structured inspection at 10,000 km. Buyers who fix those three items before ordering avoid the suspension failures that stop trucks in the field.

X6 4x4 All Wheel Drive Buying Guide for 210-245 hp Medium Duty Work

2026-11-10Buying Guide8 min read

The SAGMOTO X6 sits in the medium duty class with a Cummins ISD engine producing between 210 and 245 horsepower, and it is one of the few trucks in this weight bracket offered with a selectable all wheel drive system. For many fleet buyers the question is not whether four wheel drive is technically possible, but whether the extra cost, weight and fuel penalty are justified by the surfaces they actually drive on. This buying guide uses a decision tree built around your real route profile so you can decide before you place an order, rather than after a costly mistake has already been built into the chassis.

Start With the Route, Not the Drivetrain

The single most common mistake in specifying a medium truck is ordering all wheel drive because a job looks rough on a map, then discovering the truck spends the vast majority of its life on sealed roads where the extra axle is dead weight. Draw a simple decision tree before you talk to any supplier. First branch: does the vehicle ever leave a prepared surface for more than a few hundred metres at a time? If the answer is no, a 4x2 or 6x4 X6 with a limited slip differential will serve you better and save money on tyres, fuel and routine maintenance. Second branch: when the truck does leave the road, is the surface loose, muddy, sandy, snow covered or steep enough that a driven front axle changes the outcome? Only when both branches point to yes should you pay for the transfer case and the reinforced front steering knuckle that all wheel drive demands.

A useful rule of thumb is to count the days per year that traction actually limits you. If that number is below roughly thirty days, the capital tied up in a 4x4 system and the permanent fuel overhead rarely pays back inside the vehicle life. Above that threshold, particularly where a single stuck truck stops a whole work crew, the business case becomes clear and the option cost is easy to defend to a finance manager. Operators already running SAGMOTO off-road 4x4 mining trucks understand this trade off because their entire duty cycle is unsealed, whereas a regional distributor may never approach the break even point.

The X6 Power Band and Why 210-245 hp Suits AWD

The Cummins ISD engine family in the X6 delivers its usable torque across a broad middle rev range, which is exactly what all wheel drive work rewards. Low speed crawling in sand or mud needs torque at the wheels rather than peak horsepower at the top of the tachometer, and the 210 to 245 hp band paired with a twelve speed manual or automated gearbox keeps the engine in that sweet spot without constant shifting. For a loaded medium truck climbing a wet gravel incline, the driven front axle doubles the number of tyres able to convert that torque into motion, so the same engine feels significantly more capable than it would in a two wheel drive layout.

Above about 250 hp in this weight class the chassis, brakes and tyres start to become the limiting factor rather than the engine, so the X6 power band is well matched to its suspension and axle ratings. If your application genuinely needs more than 245 hp on a regular basis, you are probably moving into the X6s or X9 territory rather than staying within the X6 medium duty envelope, and you should review the broader SAGMOTO truck range before committing. Specifying a larger engine than the chassis can use simply adds weight and fuel burn without measurable benefit on the surfaces that matter.

Transfer Case and Front Axle Specification

All wheel drive on the X6 is delivered through a two speed transfer case that splits torque to the front and rear axles and offers a low range for crawling and recovery. Specify the part time system rather than full time drive unless you operate on loose surfaces continuously, because part time 4x4 lets you unlock the front axle on sealed roads and avoid driveline wind up, tyre scrub and the associated fuel penalty.

Pay close attention to the front axle load rating when you option all wheel drive. Adding a driven front axle shifts some payload and the weight of the transfer case forward, so the front suspension, the steering knuckle and the wheel bearings must be rated for it. Confirm the front axle capacity on your quotation matches the steer axle limit you calculated from your body and cargo weights, and insist on the heavy duty steering damper if you will run wide based tyres.

Tyre Choice for Mixed-Surface AWD Operation

Tyres are where most all wheel drive trucks win or lose in the field. A highway pattern on all four corners will clog with mud and give you almost no benefit from the driven front axle, while an aggressive mud terrain pattern on the road will wear fast, raise cabin noise and hurt fuel economy. The pragmatic answer for a mixed duty X6 is an all terrain pattern with a moderate void ratio, sized to keep the overall diameter close to the standard road tyre so the speedometer, the gearing and the anti lock calibration stay honest.

Carry at least one full size spare mounted on a steel rim, and specify tyres with a load index that covers your maximum front axle weight with the driven front axle engaged. If you work in sharp rock or on ore sites, step up to a cut resistant compound even at the cost of a few percent in rolling resistance, because a sidewall failure strands the truck exactly when four wheel drive matters most and a single recovery call can cost more than the premium rubber.

GVW and GCW Bands That Set Your Configuration

The X6 supports a gross vehicle weight up to roughly sixteen tonnes and a gross combination weight in the twelve to sixty tonne band depending on body and trailer. The all wheel drive option is most rational in the lower and middle of that range, where the truck is working close to its axle limits on poor surfaces rather than cruising lightly loaded on highways. Decision Rule

Order all wheel drive only when unsealed, steep or soft surfaces appear in your route more than one month per year, or when a single stranded truck halts a crew or breaches a delivery contract.

Fuel, Maintenance and Ownership Cost

The permanent cost of all wheel drive is a fuel overhead of roughly five to nine percent versus the equivalent two wheel drive X6, driven by the extra rotating mass, the engaged front hub and the slightly taller gearing needed for low range. On a truck covering sixty thousand kilometres a year this is a real but manageable sum, and it is usually smaller than the cost of a single recovery or the downtime of a stuck vehicle in a remote application.

Maintenance differences are modest but worth planning. The transfer case needs its oil changed on a shorter interval than a rear axle, the front driving hubs need inspection for seal weep, and the steering components see higher loads. Train drivers to disengage the front axle on sealed roads, because that single habit protects tyres, steering and fuel together and is the cheapest insurance on the whole system.

Weight Penalty

The all wheel drive hardware adds roughly three hundred to four hundred kilograms to the front of the truck, which reduces available payload and must be included in your GVW calculation before ordering the body.

Map the route, count the rough days, size the tyres and the front axle honestly, and the truck will earn its option cost every time the surface turns against you.

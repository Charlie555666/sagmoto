SAGMOTO Z3 vs E1st Heavy Tractor Comparison for Fleet Buyers

2026-11-11Comparison Guide9 min read

Overview: two SAGMOTO flagship tractors

The SAGMOTO Z3 and the SAGMOTO E1st are the two heavy tractors at the top of the brand's prime-mover range, and choosing between them is a decision that shapes a fleet's economics for a decade. The Z3 delivers 520 horsepower from the Cummins M13 engine and is rated for a gross combination weight up to 100 tonnes. The E1st is the flagship, delivering 560 horsepower from the larger Cummins Z14 and also rated to 100 tonnes gross combination weight. Both are built for long-distance, heavy regional and cross-border haulage, but they are tuned for different operator priorities, and the right pick depends on load, route, and driver strategy rather than on the horsepower number alone.

This comparison is written for fleet buyers, import distributors, and transport managers who must choose between the two without driving both for a year first. We examine engine architecture and output, the gross combination weight bands each serves, the cab and sleeper environment, real fuel consumption at a representative 49 tonne load, price positioning and total cost, and finally the fleet profile that fits each model. Fleet buyers focused on the M13 platform can review the full Z3 tractor truck 520HP Cummins M13 specification for axle and transmission options before deciding.

Both trucks share the same SAGMOTO heavy-tractor chassis philosophy, with air suspension available on the tractor axles, dual-circuit air brakes, ABS and ESC as standard, and a choice of Fast manual or automated transmissions that suit long-haul duty. That commonality means the gap between them is about tuning and trim rather than about fundamental capability, which is reassuring for fleets that may run both models side by side and want one parts and training standard across the yard. The differences below are therefore meaningful in operation but do not require a separate maintenance regime.

Engine architecture and output

The Z3 is powered by the Cummins M13, a 13 litre six-cylinder diesel that produces 520 horsepower and a strong mid-range torque curve suited to steady heavy cruising. The E1st uses the Cummins Z14, a 14 litre six-cylinder diesel that produces 560 horsepower with additional reserve for steep grades and for keeping speed on long climbs without dropping a gear. Both engines share the Cummins service philosophy and a broad global parts network, which keeps workshop support predictable across export markets. The practical difference is not the peak figure but how each engine spends its day: the M13 is relaxed at cruise, while the Z14 carries more headroom for the hardest profiles.

For most flatland and rolling routes the 520 horsepower of the Z3 is more than enough, and the smaller displacement can reward the operator with slightly lower consumable costs. On routes with sustained mountain grades, heavy headwinds, or a constant push to maintain schedule with full loads, the 560 horsepower of the E1st pays back through fewer gear changes and steadier average speed. GCW bands and duty

Both tractors are rated to a gross combination weight of up to 100 tonnes, which places them in the heavy regional and long-haul class rather than the medium segment. The Z3 reaches that ceiling comfortably with its 520 horsepower and a well-matched transmission and rear-axle ratio, and it is the natural choice for fleets that run close to but not constantly at the limit. The E1st reaches the same ceiling with more power in hand, which matters when the load is consistently near the maximum or when the route adds altitude and gradient to the equation.

The duty split matters more than the headline rating. A fleet that runs 80 to 90 tonnes on mostly flat highways will never use the E1st's extra reserve, while a fleet that runs 95 to 100 tonnes through hills will feel the Z3 working harder on every grade. Operators who need maximum output should study the E1st flagship tractor truck 560HP for its Z14 engine and flagship cab before committing, because the flagship is engineered for exactly that worst-case duty rather than for the average day.

The axle and transmission pairing also shapes how that gross combination weight is actually used. A tall final drive keeps engine speed low and fuel gentle on the flat but forces downshifts on grade, while a shorter ratio trades some cruise economy for the ability to hold a gear uphill with a full load. Specify the ratio to your dominant route rather than to a catalogue average, because the same tractor can feel underpowered or effortless purely on the gearing chosen at the factory. Both the Z3 and the E1st reward a buyer who treats the rear-axle ratio as a primary specification, not a default.

Cab, sleeper and driver comfort

On long-haul routes the cab is where the productivity is won or lost, because a rested driver runs more hours legally and makes fewer costly mistakes. Both tractors offer a large, flat-floor cab with a sleeper compartment, but the E1st flagship typically carries the higher-spec interior, more refined insulation, and a more generous sleeper layout aimed at two-driver or extended-rest operations. The Z3 provides a practical, comfortable cab that satisfies most regional duties without the premium of the flagship trim.

For fleets running single drivers on shorter regional legs, the Z3 cab is usually sufficient and keeps the purchase price lower. For fleets running two-up on multi-day international corridors, the E1st sleeper and cabin refinement justify the step up, because driver retention and rest quality translate directly into schedule reliability. Cab choice should follow the duty cycle, not the showroom, because the extra comfort only pays when the truck actually uses it night after night.

Fuel economy at 49 tonnes

At a representative 49 tonne gross combination weight on mixed highway and rolling terrain, both tractors return competitive figures, with the result shaped more by gearing, driver behaviour, and load aerodynamics than by the forty horsepower gap. The Z3 typically runs slightly lower engine speed at cruise and can edge ahead on fuel where the route is gentle, because the smaller M13 displaces less and works in its most efficient band. The E1st, with its larger Z14, may use marginally more fuel in light running but protects average speed on grades, which can recover the difference through more tonne-kilometres per shift.

The honest conclusion is that the fuel gap between the two at 49 tonnes is small and usually secondary to the operational fit. A Z3 driven aggressively will burn more than an E1st driven smoothly, so the driver training programme outweighs the engine choice in the fuel budget. Price positioning and total cost

The E1st sits above the Z3 on price, reflecting the larger engine, the flagship cab, and the higher-spec componentry that comes with the top of the range. The Z3 is the more accessible entry into genuine 100 tonne capability, and for many fleets it delivers ninety percent of the operational result at a lower capital cost. The total-cost picture, however, depends on utilisation: a Z3 that is never loaded near its limit wastes no money, while an E1st that earns its reserve on hard routes returns the premium through schedule and uptime.

Buyers should model the five-year cost rather than the sticker, including fuel, tyres, service, and residual value, because the tractor with the lower purchase price is not always the cheaper tractor across the contract. Finance structure and volume discounts also shift the comparison, so request a like-for-like quotation on both models with the same axle and transmission spec before drawing a conclusion from the list price alone.

Residual value deserves particular attention, because the flagship E1st tends to hold a stronger second-hand price in markets where the Z14 is recognised as a premium long-haul engine, while the Z3's broad popularity keeps used demand steady and parts plentiful. Neither depreciates sharply if maintained, but the resale story differs by region, so speak to the local used-truck market before assuming one retains value better everywhere. The tractor you can resell quickly at a fair price is the tractor that protects your balance sheet when the fleet is refreshed.

Which fleet profile fits each model

Choose the Z3 when your routes are regional or rolling, your loads sit in the 80 to 95 tonne band, and your drivers run mostly single-shift regional legs where the practical cab is enough. It is the sensible default for fleets that want heavy capability without paying for flagship trim they will not use. Choose the E1st when your routes include sustained grades, your loads frequently approach the 100 tonne ceiling, your operation is schedule-critical, or you run two-up on long international corridors where the flagship sleeper earns its keep every night.

Neither tractor is wrong; they are tuned for different realities. The fleet that buys on horsepower alone often over-spends on an E1st it never loads, while the fleet that ignores its hardest route under-buys a Z3 that struggles on the one grade that matters. Match the model to your worst regular day, confirm the spec with the exporter, and the tractor will earn across the whole contract rather than only on the easy runs.

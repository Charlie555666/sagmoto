SAGMOTO i9 in Bolivia: Specifying Electric Haulage for Andean Altitude

2026-11-23Buying & Import Guide9 min read

Bolivia's mining freight runs across the Altiplano at 3,600 to 4,100 m, where La Paz, Oruro and Potosi sit above the cloud line and the air holds roughly two thirds of the oxygen available at sea level. That single fact changes the truck buying calculation. A diesel engine loses power as the air thins, but an electric motor does not need oxygen to make torque. The SAGMOTO i9 is a battery-electric heavy truck offered as a 6x4 or 8x4 at 31 to 49 t with an LFP pack of 282 to 350 kWh and an e-motor in the 250 to 350 kW class. Its advantages at altitude are real, but its cooling and charging systems have their own altitude problems.

What altitude changes and what it leaves alone

A combustion engine is an air-breathing machine, so a naturally aspirated unit at 4,000 m can lose a fifth or more of its sea-level output even with turbocharging recovering part of it. The i9 motor,inverter and reduction gear are not air dependent, so rated torque is available from the first metre of a climb. What does change is the ability to reject heat. Radiator performance depends on the mass of air passing through the core, and air density at 4,000 m is around 0.82 kg per cubic metre against 1.225 at sea level. A cooling pack that is adequate on the coast has materially less capacity on the Altiplano.

Key point: At 4,000 m an i9 keeps its motor torque because electricity needs no oxygen, but its radiator moves about a third less air mass than at sea level. Cooling specification, not horsepower, is the altitude constraint on an electric mine truck.

Cooling, derate and sustained grade duty

Heat in the i9 comes from the motor windings, the inverter modules and the reduction oil, with the LFP pack adding its own chiller load on a hot day. All of it ends up in a coolant loop that must dump heat through a radiator working in thin air. The control system protects itself by reducing current when winding or junction temperatures climb, so a truck that starts a grade at full output may settle at 80 to 85 percent of its continuous rating after several minutes. On a long climb out of a mine pit, that translates into a lower sustained speed and a longer cycle.

The practical response is to specify the cooling pack for the site altitude rather than accept a standard temperate-climate unit. Ask for the derate curve at 4,000 m and compare it with the sea-level version; a supplier who cannot provide that comparison has not validated the truck for your site. Keeping the pack in a moderate state of charge also reduces heat generation, and scheduling the heaviest climbs for the cooler early hours is a free improvement in cycle time. A larger battery alone does not solve a thermal problem.

Descent, regeneration and state of charge

Andean mine haulage often involves a loaded climb out of a pit and a return that drops most of the way back. That profile suits an electric truck because the motor becomes a generator on the descent and feeds energy into the pack. Recovery is not automatic, though: if the truck starts the downhill at 90 percent state of charge, the battery has nowhere to put the energy and the braking resistors must dissipate it as heat. Fleet rules that cap the daily upper charge near 80 to 90 percent leave room for regeneration and recover far more of the descent energy.

Regeneration also reduces service brake wear on a road where a diesel truck's engine brake is weakened by the thin air. Drivers still need training on the blended braking feel, particularly on loose gravel near the pit rim where a sudden torque reversal can unsettlea loaded vehicle. A site trial with an instrumented truck is the safest way to set the regeneration and brake-blending expectations before a fleet order.

Cold nights and LFP charging behaviour

The Altiplano is cold as well as high. Night temperatures on the mine sites around Potosi can fall below minus 10 C, and an LFP pack should not be fast-charged while its cells are near freezing because lithium plating can damage them. An i9 destined for Bolivia therefore needs a pack heating function, ideally one that can draw power from the charger while warming the cells before the main charge begins. Buyers should ask for the minimum permitted charging temperature in writing and confirm how the truck behaves when it returns to the depot at midnight in midwinter.

Thermal management works both ways. A pack that is warmed overnight and then worked hard on a 4,000 m grade may need active cooling by mid-morning, so the chiller and radiator are used across a wider temperature range than in a temperate market. Insulation of the battery enclosure, a controlled parking area and a charge schedule that finishes close to departure all reduce the energy spent on conditioning rather than on freight.

Mine-site power and charging infrastructure

Electric haulage is only as reliable as the power behind it. Many Bolivian mines are connected to a constrained regional grid, and a remote site may rely on a diesel generator set. Adding two 250 kW chargers is a 500 kW load, and without load management the generator either trips or burns fuel at poor efficiency. Staggered charging, a cap on simultaneous draw and a charger placed close to the distribution point are cheaper than upgrading the connection. Where solar is available, the Altiplano's high irradiation makes a generation plus storage combination attractive, though the capital cost has to be set against the number of trucks sharing the installation.

Charge windows should be built into the shift pattern. A 20 to 30 minute driver break at 100 to 150 kW can add 30 to 70 kWh, which may be the difference between one trip and two on a long loop. Cable runs, weatherproof connectors and a clear isolation procedure belong in the project plan from the start. The SAGMOTO new energy electric trucks configuration sheet is a useful starting point, but an Altiplano specification has to be built around thin-air cooling and overnight pack heating.

Configurations, tyres and service at altitude

The 6x4 i9 is the natural choice for a plateau haul between fixed points, where its lower tare leaves more payload under a legal gross limit. The 8x4 earns its place on steep, wet or loose mine roads where traction governs the cycle, accepting roughly a tonne or more of extra empty weight. Tyres should be selected for the sharp rock common on Andean haul roads, with pressures checked from actual axle weights rather than a rule of thumb, because under-inflation raises rolling resistance and heat at exactly the point where the truck has least cooling margin.

Service planning should reflect severe duty. Coolant condition, chiller operation, inverter connections and reduction gear oil deserve shorter intervals than a highway schedule, and diagnostic logs should be reviewed for derate events after each hot period. Keeping a small stock of filters, a coolant pump and a connector set on site prevents a fault turning into a week of idle time when the nearest supplier is a long descent away.

Fleet sizing and charge scheduling at height

Bolivian mine operations often run continuous shifts, which leaves little slack for a truck that returns with insufficient charge. A practical method is to size the fleet on the number of cycles one vehicle completes in a shift with a defined top-up window, then add one reserve unit for the coldest period when pack heating consumes part of the charging session. Over-sizing the battery on every truck is usually more expensive than adding a shared charger and a spare vehicle, because the charger serves the whole fleet while each extra kilowatt-hour of capacity is paid for on every unit.

Cost case with assumptions stated

Any comparison with diesel must state its inputs. Assume a mine loop of 24 km loaded and 18 km empty, electricity at US$0.10 per kWh, diesel at US$1.35 per litre, a diesel 6x4 at 38 l/100 km and an i9 at 1.6 kWh/km loaded equivalent. On those figures the electric truck uses about 67 kWh per full cycle against roughly 99 litres of diesel, an energy cost near US$6.70 versus US$134. The gap is large enough to absorb a substantial charger premium, but only if the power is genuinely available and the truck completes its cycles without thermal derate. Add grid connection and charger capital to the comparison before signing.

An electric truck on the Altiplano is not a simpler version of a coastal delivery vehicle. Specify it around air density, overnight cold, regeneration headroom and the real mine power supply, and the absence of an oxygen-dependent engine becomes a genuine operational advantage rather than a brochure claim.

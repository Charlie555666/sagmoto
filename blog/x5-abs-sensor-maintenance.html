SAGMOTO X5 ABS Wheel-Speed Sensor Faults: Diagnosis and Service

2026-11-23Maintenance & After-sales9 min read

An ABS warning lamp on a medium cargo truck is easy to ignore until the brake behaviour changes on a wet road. On the SAGMOTO X5, a 4x2 or 6x4 medium-duty truck powered by a Cummins or Yuchai engine in the 180 to 240 horsepower band with 700 to 900 Nm and a Fast 8-speed gearbox, the most common cause is a wheel-speed sensor that has lost its signal. The sensor is a small component in a harsh position, exposed to water, mud, stone damage and constant vibration. This guide explains how the ABS uses the signal, how to read a fault code, how to check the sensor air gap and electrical circuit, and when the sensor itself needs replacing.

The anti-lock braking system compares the speed of each wheel several times a second. If one wheel reports a much lower speed than the others under braking, the modulator reduces pressure to that wheel so it can roll again and keep steering control. When a sensor fails or its signal becomes erratic, the system cannot tell a locked wheel from a slow one and usually disables ABS, falling back to conventional braking. The truck still stops, but the safety margin on a slippery surface is reduced, and in many markets an active ABS lamp is a roadworthiness failure.

Key point: An ABS lamp means the system has lost confidence in at least one wheel-speed signal. Read the code and identify the affected corner before replacing parts; a damaged wiring lead is a far more common cause than a failed sensor.

How to read and interpret the fault

Connect a diagnostic scanner that can talk to the ABS controller and record the stored code before clearing anything. Codes usually name a circuit or a wheel position, such as front left sensor signal missing or rear right signal erratic. Note whether the fault is present all the time or only above a certain speed, and use freeze-frame data if the controller provides it. A sensor that fails at low speed and works at high speed often has an air gap problem, while one that works when cold but fails when hot points toward a coil or connector issue.

A live data readout is the most useful tool in this diagnosis. With the truck on a lift or a safe surface, spin each wheel by hand and watch the reported speed. All four or six channels should rise smoothly and fall to zero together. A channel that stays at zero, jumps irregularly or shows a speed without the wheel moving gives a clear direction for the repair. If no scanner is available, a multimeter can still test the sensor circuit, but it will not show the modulation behaviour that a live readout reveals.

Checking the air gap and tone ring

Most X5 installations use an inductive sensor mounted close to a toothed ring on the hub or brake drum. The control unit reads the changing magnetic field as each tooth passes. If the gap between sensor tip and teeth is too large, the signal weakens and drops out at low speed; if the sensor is loose and the gap varies with vibration, the signal becomes erratic. Measure the gap with a non-magnetic feeler gauge at several points around the ring, rotating the wheel slowly each time. A typical working gap lies in the range of 0.5 to 1.5 mm, but the figure for the exact axle should be taken from the service data.

Inspect the tone ring at the same time. Missing or damaged teeth produce a signal that repeats the same fault once per revolution, which a scanner may show as a regular dip in wheel speed. Rust flakes and metal debris also collect on magnetic rings and distort the signal, so clean the ring and check it for runout. On a 6x4 truck working in mud or on mine sites, a bent ring is a realistic failure and should be corrected rather than compensated for by moving the sensor closer.

Key point: Never close an excessive air gap by forcing the sensor against the ring. Clean the mounting face, check the bracket for damage and set the gap to specification; a sensor that touches the teeth will be destroyed within a few hundred kilometres.

Electrical circuit and connector checks

Disconnect the sensor plug and inspect the terminals for corrosion, water ingress and loose pins. Measure the coil resistance across the sensor terminals with a multimeter; a reading that is open circuit or far outside the expected range condemns the sensor, while a short to earth indicates damaged insulation. Compare the reading with the same sensor on the opposite side of the axle when possible, since a healthy reference value is more reliable than a generic number. Check the harness from the sensor to the controller for chafing where it crosses the axle or follows the brake pipe, because movement at the suspension joint wears through insulation over time.

Wiggle the connector and harness while watching the live data or a resistance reading. A circuit that breaks when the loom is moved has an internal wire fault that a static test will miss. Repair damaged sections with the correct gauge wire and sealed joints, then secure the loom with clips so it cannot rub again. On trucks used in wet or salty conditions, apply dielectric grease to the connector seal and fit a protective cap where the plug faces upward. These small measures prevent the majority of repeat ABS faults on a working fleet.

Replacement and post-repair verification

When a sensor is confirmed faulty, fit a unit rated for the exact axle and wheel position, since sensors for different positions may look similar but have different characteristics. Clean the mounting bore before installation, fit a new O-ring or seal where specified, and set the air gap to the correct value. Secure the lead so it cannot contact the brake disc, drum or rotating ring, and route it along the original path with enough slack for suspension travel. A cable that is too tight will snap at the first articulation.

After replacement, clear the fault codes and road-test the truck. Watch the live data during gentle braking from 40 to 50 km/h and confirm that all wheel speeds fall and recover together and that the ABS lamp stays off. If the lamp returns, read the new code rather than assuming the first repair was wrong; a second fault on another corner is common on an older vehicle. Record the code, the part replaced and the gap measurement in the service log so the next technician can see what was done.

On trucks fitted with a 6x4 layout, remember that a fault on one rear axle can be masked by the other when the vehicle is driven in a straight line. Brake bias and load transfer change the speed signal slightly, so a sensor with a marginal air gap may only show its fault during a corner or an emergency stop. A road test that includes gentle left and right turns at moderate speed will often reproduce an intermittent code that a stationary wheel-spin test cannot. Ask the driver to describe the road condition when the lamp appeared, and use that detail to shape the test route.

Service intervals and prevention

ABS sensors need little scheduled attention, but they should be inspected whenever the truck is in for brake work. A visual check of the mounting, the lead and the connector costs minutes and catches loose brackets before they become signal faults. In a fleet that runs on rough ground, add a sensor and tone-ring inspection at every brake-lining change. The table below gives a practical schedule for an X5 in medium-duty service.

Keep the diagnostic record with the brake service file rather than in a separate folder. A code that was cleared six months ago is still useful information when the same corner fails again, because it tells the technician whether the previous repair addressed the cause or only the symptom. Include the sensor resistance, the measured air gap and the harness condition in the entry. Fleet managers reviewing repeat faults across several vehicles can then spot a batch-related problem, such as a run of weak brackets or a mounting position that collects water on a particular body type.

The X5 is often specified with different bodies for regional distribution, and the SAGMOTO cargo truck flatbed box stake range shows how the same chassis is adapted to each task. Body choice affects brake duty and therefore the heat and contamination that sensors see, but the diagnostic method stays the same. A technician who can read a code, measure a gap and trace a harness will fix an ABS fault on any version of the platform.

Wheel-speed sensor faults are among the most straightforward electrical problems on the X5 once the diagnostic order is right. Start with the code, look at live data, measure the gap and inspect the harness before ordering a sensor, and the repair will be correct the first time. An ABS system in good order protects the driver, the load and the license to operate, which is a strong return for a few hours of disciplined testing.

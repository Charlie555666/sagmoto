E9 vs Tata Intra Comparison for Light City Delivery Work

2026-11-17Model Comparison9 min read

City delivery trucks are judged on manoeuvrability and cost per drop, not on straight-line speed. That is why the Tata Intra, a compact pickup-derived light commercial vehicle,often appears beside the SAGMOTO E9 on a buyer's list. The E9 is a light-duty chassis in the 3 to 8 tonne class, using a Yuchai YC4FA or YC4D engine between 90 and 160 hp with a Fast six-speed box, intended for urban logistics. The two vehicles overlap in a few inner-city duties and differ sharply in most others. This comparison sets out the real differences in payload, power, body options, turning ability, price, fuel use and support, and gives a verdict that states plainly where the Chinese truck loses.

Start with the weight class, not the nameplate

The first mistake in this comparison is assuming the two trucks compete in the same category. The Intra is a small light commercial vehicle built for a light load and tight streets, while the E9 spans a wider and heavier band with several gross weight options. A body that fits one will overload the other. Buyers must begin with the payload they need to carry legally, then see which model can meet it with the correct homologation. Only after that does price become a fair question.

For a load of two tonnes or less on narrow streets, the Intra is the more natural tool and the E9 is likely to be over-specified. Once the load rises past three tonnes, the small LCV reaches its limit and the E9 comes into its own. The crossover point is the number that matters, and it should be calculated from the actual freight, not from the largest load the buyer has ever carried.

Engines and gearboxes under a delivery cycle

The E9 offers a Yuchai YC4FA or YC4D engine in the 90 to 160 hp range through a Fast six-speed gearbox. That combination is built for repeated loaded starts and for a truck that spends much of the day at low speed with frequent stops. Buyers should confirm which engine is fitted, because the upper outputs cost more but pay back on a hilly route or when the vehicle carries a full load all day. The six-speed box gives the driver a ratio for both a standing start and a modest open-road leg between districts.

The Tata Intra uses a small diesel engine with a five-speed manual gearbox, tuned for economy rather than strength. It is light, responsive around town and cheap to run when the load is small. On a steep ramp with a full body it will work harder and slow down more than the E9. The trade is straightforward: the Intra is efficient at the light end of city work, while the E9 keeps its pace as the payload grows. Choose the engine by watching the truck on your heaviest regular delivery, not on an empty demonstration lap.

Match the engine to the heaviest regular load. A small power unit is economical when light and struggling when full, while the E9 keeps pace under a heavier body.

Deck and body options

Body flexibility is a clear strength of the E9. It can carry a box body, a dropside deck, a refrigerated unit or a purpose-built municipal body, depending on the homologated gross weight and the axle rating. A fleet that wants one chassis to serve several departments can specify the body around the work. Check the frame mounting points and the wheelbase before ordering, because a long body on a short wheelbase will overload the front axle once it is loaded. Consider the load floor height as well as the deck area. A lower floor cuts the lift height for a driver handling parcels by hand and steadies the truck by keeping the centre of gravity down. Confirm the mounting height with the final axle and tyre specification, since a larger tyre can raise the deck enough to matter across a full shift of manual loading.

The Intra is sold mainly as a pickup or with a small cargo box. That suits a tradesman or a last-mile operator carrying parcels, tools or light goods. It does not suit a buyer who needs a tall insulated box or a heavy dropside deck. The smaller deck also means more trips for the same tonnage, which raises labour cost and traffic exposure. Where the freight is light and the streets are narrow, those extra trips are a fair price to pay for a vehicle that can park almost anywhere.

Turning circle and narrow-street work

This is the section where the Tata Intra wins most convincingly. Its compact wheelbase and small dimensions give it a tight turning circle and make it easy to place on a crowded market street, a residential lane or a loading bay with limited room. A driver can complete a three-point turn where an E9 would need to reverse into traffic. On a route made up of short drops in dense neighbourhoods, that ability saves minutes at every stop and reduces the risk of contact with parked vehicles.

The E9 is wider and longer, and it needs more space to manoeuvre. It is still a city truck rather than a highway one, but the operator must plan the route around its dimensions. Measure the narrowest access point on the delivery round before ordering, including the gate width at the depot and the turning space at the largest customer. If the E9 cannot reach a meaningful share of the drops, its payload advantage is worthless. If the route includes wide arterial roads and a few tight corners, the bigger truck remains the better economic choice.

Price, fuel burn and cost per delivery

The Intra normally costs less to buy and burns less fuel on a light load, because it carries less mass and a smaller engine. Those two facts explain much of its appeal. The E9 asks a higher purchase price and more fuel per kilometre, but it carries a larger load in a single trip. The number that settles the comparison is the cost per delivery or cost per tonne delivered, not the price of a litre or the invoice total. A truck that uses more fuel but halves the number of trips can still be cheaper to operate.

Record a full week of deliveries and calculate the real figure for each truck. Count fuel, driver time, tolls and the cost of any missed delivery slot. Where the load is consistently above three tonnes, the E9 will usually show a lower cost per tonne. Where the round is made up of small parcels in a dense area, the Intra often wins on fuel and access. A mixed operation may need both vehicles rather than a compromise that performs neither task well.

Service networks and the honest verdict

Tata's service reach in India and many South Asian markets is dense and well understood. A small truck can be repaired in a town where a Chinese distributor has no presence, and pattern parts are widely stocked. The Intra also holds its value in those markets because buyers know the name. Those advantages are real and should not be dismissed. The E9 has a growing support base, but a buyer far from a distributor must confirm local stock and lead time before committing, and must price the risk of a truck waiting on an imported part. Ask the seller for a written list of warranty workshops in your region and check that they carry the common service items for the Yuchai engine and the Fast gearbox. A named contact and a stocked shelf are worth more than a general promise of support. If a local mechanic has to wait for a sensor, record the delay and set it against the competing truck.

The verdict follows the load and the map. Choose the Tata Intra when the freight is light, the streets are tight, the budget is limited and service coverage is thin outside the main cities. It is a good tool for that job and a poor tool for anything heavier. Choose the E9 when the payload is regularly above three tonnes, the route includes open roads between districts, and a supported distributor is nearby. Where the E9 loses, it loses on purchase price, tight-turning ability, fuel use when light and network depth. A buyer who needs those qualities should not be talked into a larger truck.

Where the E9 loses, the reasons are price, turning circle, light-load fuel burn and service reach. Name those limits honestly before recommending a bigger truck.

Neither model is right for every city route, and a seller who pretends otherwise is not helping the fleet.

Measure the load, measure the street, and then measure the support. Those three checks will point to the right truck more reliably than any specification sheet. The E9 is a capable light-duty workhorse for a supported fleet with real payload to move; the Intra is a smart, economical choice for light freight in crowded places. Each wins on the duty cycle it was built for, and each loses when asked to do the other one's job.

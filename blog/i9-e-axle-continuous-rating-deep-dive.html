SAGMOTO i9 E-Axle: Continuous Rating and Thermal Limits

2026-11-23Product Deep Dive9 min read

Electric truck brochures quote peak power because it is the larger number. A fleet buyer needs the continuous rating instead, because that is what an e-axle can deliver after twenty minutes of loaded climbing once the windings, inverter and gear oil have reached thermal equilibrium. The SAGMOTO i9 is a battery-electric heavy truck in 6x4 and 8x4 form at 31 to 49 t, with an LFP pack of 282 to 350 kWh and an e-motor in the 250 to 350 kW class. On a sustained grade, the gap between those two ratings decides whether the truck holds its speed or steadily loses it.

Peak power versus continuous power

Peak output is a short-duration figure. It is limited by the maximum current the inverter can switch and by how much heat the motor's mass can absorb before its temperature rises too far. Continuous output is a steady-state figure. It is set by how effectively the cooling system can remove copper and iron losses, inverter switching losses and gear-churning heat while the truck is working. The two numbers can differ by thirty percent or more. Buyers comparing the SAGMOTO new energy electric trucks range should read the continuous figure first, because that is the number that governs a loaded climb.

The thermal path in an e-axle

Heat is generated in three places. Copper losses rise with the square of current in the stator windings, switching losses appear in the inverter modules, and the reduction gears churn oil at high input speed. Motor oil carries heat from the windings to a coolant-to-oil exchanger, while a cold plate removes inverter heat into the same low-temperature loop that may also serve the battery chiller. The radiator can only reject heat if the coolant is meaningfully hotter than the surrounding air. On a 40 C day with a steep climb, that temperature difference is small, and the control system responds by capping torque.

Operators sometimes interpret a derate as a fault. It is a protection strategy: the controller reduces current until winding and junction temperatures stop rising, then holds the truck at whatever speed that reduced power supports. The visible symptom is a climb that starts well and fades near the top, followed by normal performance once the load is removed. Nothing is broken, but the cycle time has been affected, and that belongs in the business case.

How quickly a derate arrives and how the truck recovers

Thermal limits are time dependent. The motor and inverter have a few minutes of thermal mass before their temperatures reach the point where current must be reduced, while the gear oil and the battery chiller respond over tens of minutes. On a short climb of two or three kilometres the truck may never fully derate, even at a demand above the continuous figure. On a ten kilometre haul out of a pit, the same demand produces a predictable fall in speed after the fifth minute. This is why a demonstration on a flat test track proves very little about a mine application.

Recovery follows the same physics. Once the load comes off on the return leg, the coolant loop continues to remove heat and the ratings return, but not instantly. A truck that climbs loaded and immediately turns around for another cycle starts the second climb warmer than the first. Fleet planners should include that carry-over in cycle estimates, especially where ambient temperatures stay high overnight. Logging winding and coolant temperatures through the duty cycle gives a clearer picture than average speed alone, and most installations can record these values for later review.

Grade duty arithmetic with real numbers

The power needed on a grade can be estimated directly. Take a 43 t i9 8x4 on an eight percent grade. At 10 km/h, or 2.78 m/s, gradient power is mass times gravity times speed times grade, about 94 kW at the wheels. Rolling resistance at 0.006 adds roughly 7 kW, giving a wheel demand near 101 kW. With a 0.90 drivetrain efficiency the pack must deliver about 112 kW. Double the road speed to 20 km/h and the wheel demand approaches 200 kW, with battery draw above 220 kW once losses are included. A motor with a 220 kW continuous rating will handle the first case indefinitely and the second only while its thermal mass allows.

The battery is the second limiter

Continuous motor output is only useful if the pack can supply it. An LFP pack of 282 kWh delivering a sustained 200 kW is working at roughly 0.7C, which is manageable provided cell temperature is controlled. At low state of charge, voltage sag increases current for the same power and adds heat, which is why the useful operating window for heavy climbing is usually kept between about 20 and 80 percent. The larger 350 kWh pack reduces C-rate for the same road speed and leaves more headroom for auxiliary cooling, but it adds mass that must be accounted for in the gross weight.

Charge acceptance matters on the return leg. A pack that is too full cannot absorb regeneration from a descent, so braking energy is dissipated as heat instead of recovered. Fleets that schedule the steepest loaded runs early and keep the state of charge in the middle band get both better thermal behaviour and a useful regeneration credit. The charge curve of an LFP pack also tapers above roughly 80 percent, so a short top-up from 30 to 70 percent is far more productive than chasing a full charge.

E-axle packaging and what it changes

An e-axle integrates the motor, inverter and reduction into the driven axle housing. That shortens the driveline, removes a separate propeller shaft and its universal joints, and reduces the number of rotating components that a workshop must maintain. It also places the thermal source close to the axle oil, which is useful for heat rejection but makes oil condition more important. The i9 can be supplied with an e-axle or with an automated gearbox arrangement; either way the buyer should ask for the continuous rating at the stated ambient temperature rather than accept a single headline figure.

Regenerative braking acts on the driven axle in both 6x4 and 8x4 builds. On a mine or quarry loop with a loaded climb and an empty return, that recovers a meaningful share of the descent energy, provided the pack has room. Traction control is integrated with the brake system, so wheel slip on a wet haul road is managed by motor torque as well as by the service brakes. This is worth demonstrating on a loaded site trial rather than accepting on paper.

Specifying and maintaining for grade work

A sound specification exercise asks the supplier for five things in writing: continuous shaft power at a named ambient temperature, the derate curve above that point, the sustained pack discharge figure, the coolant and gear-oil service intervals, and the warranty treatment of high-cycle duty. Ask also for a logged climb from a comparable installation. A truck that has never been tested on a sustained grade will reveal its thermal limits on your route instead of the supplier's.

Service attention centres on coolant condition, the oil in the reduction gears, inverter connections and the diagnostic log. Heat cycles age coolant additives and seals faster than road mileage alone suggests, so a severe-service interval is appropriate for a steep mine haul. Because derate events are recorded, a fleet manager can review them alongside cycle times and decide whether a route needs a larger pack, a slower schedule or additional opportunity charging. That evidence is more useful than a generic range promise.

Where the energy goes on a mixed route

Not all of the pack's output reaches the road. On a route combining a paved approach with an unsealed haul section, rolling resistance can take a substantial share of total energy, and pumps for cooling add more. A heavy i9 working at 40 C may devote several kilowatts continuously to its coolant and chiller circuits, energy that does not move freight. Buyers who model consumption from a single highway figure will understate the real demand of a dusty site road with repeated climbs.

The remedy is a measured energy budget rather than a single number. Log state of charge at the start and end of each leg, note the elevation change, and separate the loaded climb from the empty return. With three or four such cycles an operator can calculate kWh per tonne-kilometre for that specific site and compare it honestly with a diesel baseline. That measured figure is also the input needed to size opportunity charging, because it captures the effects of heat and gradient that a catalogue range cannot.

SAGMOTO i9 Electric Truck for Solar Farm Construction Logistics

2026-11-02Industry Application8 min read

The construction project that should run on clean wheels

Solar farm construction has a branding problem that its logistics can solve. Developers sell these projects on decarbonisation, and then build them with diesel trucks crawling across the site for months, hauling piles, brackets, panels and cabling from laydown yards to installation fronts. The irony is not lost on the engineers, and it is not lost either on the lenders and offtakers whose ESG reporting increasingly asks what happened during construction, not just after commissioning. An electric truck fleet on a solar site closes that loop, and it is one of the few places where the electric truck's constraints, modest daily range, access to charging, predictable duty cycles, are met exactly by the job. The i9, SAGMOTO's battery-electric truck with a 96 kWh LFP pack, 167 kW peak PMSM motor and a 430-kilometre working range under favourable conditions, is purpose-fit for this application. It is part of the SAGMOTO new energy electric trucks range.

Look at the distances: almost every movement sits inside a 30-kilometre round trip, run repeatedly. That is the electric truck's sweet spot, and the daily total, 80 to 200 kilometres depending on fleet allocation, sits comfortably inside the i9's 430-kilometre working range with margin for the auxiliary loads that construction adds, water pumps, hydraulic tailgates, and for the efficiency loss that rough site tracks impose. The 167 kW peak motor's 1,100 Nm of instant torque moves a full 4.5-tonne load across soft ground and gravel ramps better than a comparable diesel, because torque arrives without revs, without clutch wear and without wheelspin.

Key point: Solar site logistics run 80-to-200-kilometre days in 30-kilometre loops, the exact profile electric trucks serve best. The i9's 430 km range covers the duty cycle with a 40-to-60-percent buffer, which is what site managers need before they trust an electric fleet.

The charging plan, and why the site makes it easy

The obstacle that kills most electric-truck deployments, charging infrastructure, is nearly free on a solar construction site. The site runs on temporary grid connections and generator power during construction, and after commissioning it owns the cheapest electricity on the planet. The professional plan uses both: during construction, two to four 22 kW AC chargers at the laydown yard, fed from the site's temporary supply, overnight-charge the fleet; the i9's 7 kW onboard AC rate means a full overnight cycle replenishes even a depleted pack, and a lunch-break top-up at 22 kW adds meaningful range for the afternoon. After commissioning, permanent site power serves the operations-phase fleet, trackers, vegetation management, panel washing, security, indefinitely and at near-zero marginal energy cost.

The sequencing matters commercially: an EPC contractor that deploys the electric fleet during construction inherits, at handover, an operations-phase electric logistics package that the owner's O&M budget can absorb with a smile. That continuity turns a construction gesture into an asset, and it is the strongest single argument for specifying electric from day one rather than retrofitting later.

The acquisition premium narrows over a multi-project horizon: an EPC that deploys the same electric fleet across consecutive solar projects, 200 days here, 150 days there, accumulates operating savings against one capital outlay, and the LFP pack's 8-to-10-year service life comfortably outspans several construction programmes. Add the tender-scoring value: developers and lenders with ESG-weighted procurement increasingly reward demonstrable construction-phase decarbonisation, and an electric site fleet is a line in the proposal that diesel cannot match at any price.

Key point: Score the electric fleet in the tender, not just the cost sheet. Construction-phase emissions and ESG evidence carry procurement weight with developers and lenders, and a working electric logistics package is worth real points that a diesel fleet forfeits outright.

Operating discipline on site

Three practices make the deployment work. Dispatch discipline: run the long regional runs in the morning on a fresh pack, hold the short loops for afternoons, and plug in at every laydown dwell over an hour; the BMS manages the rest. Dust management: solar sites are dusty, and daily washdown of radiators is replaced on the i9 by daily inspection of the battery pack's cooling intakes and the motor housing, a five-minute routine that keeps thermal management honest. Driver assignment: electric trucks reward smooth operators, regenerative braking recovers up to 15 percent of consumption under favourable conditions, and the drivers who learn the one-pedal feel fastest post the best range numbers; assign your best drivers first and let the results sell the fleet internally.

Tyre choice deserves one note: site tracks with fresh gravel cut soft eco-tyres, so specify all-position traction casings in a robust load range and accept a small range penalty; a punctured tyre costs more than a kilometre of range.

From construction to operations: the O&M fleet

The construction fleet's job ends; the site's logistics needs do not. An operating solar farm runs a permanent movements calendar: tracker maintenance crews and their equipment rotating across blocks, panel washing campaigns with water tankers, vegetation management teams, inverter and substation servicing, and spares movements between sites. These are exactly the short-radius, low-payload, daily-pattern duties where electric trucks post their best economics, and a fleet that earned its place during construction is already specified, already charged and already understood by the site team.

The O&M economics sharpen the case further. Where construction ran on temporary power, operations run on the plant's own output at effectively zero marginal cost, and annual mileage drops to 15,000 to 30,000 kilometres per truck, which stretches battery life, since the pack cycles shallowly, and stretches the depreciation over more years of service. The i9's LFP chemistry, rated for thousands of cycles, sits comfortably in this regime for a decade, and the maintenance profile, no oil, no filters, no injectors, reduces the O&M contractor's workshop burden to brakes, tyres and suspension items.

For the site owner, the operations-phase electric fleet also has a reporting function: it slots directly into the plant's sustainability documentation as zero-emission logistics, a small line item in the annual report that is nonetheless checked by the same lenders who financed the construction. From first pile to year ten, the electric site fleet keeps earning on both the cost sheet and the story.

Procurement structure and fleet handover between projects

Who owns the electric fleet is a real commercial question on a solar programme. Three structures work in practice. The EPC-owned model puts the trucks on the contractor's balance sheet and moves them project to project; it suits contractors with a rolling pipeline and rewards them with the multi-project depreciation story. The developer-owned model keeps the fleet with the asset owner, who deploys it across construction and then absorbs it into O&M; it suits owners with large portfolios and long-term reporting ambitions. The leasing model, through a regional equipment lessor, converts the acquisition premium into an operating expense and suits EPCs without the balance sheet appetite; it is the least common today only because lessors with electric-truck residual expertise are still emerging outside China.

Between projects, storage and transport matter for battery health: an idle i9 should be stored at 40-to-60-percent state of charge in moderate temperature, checked monthly, and charged before redeployment; LFP chemistry tolerates this calendar well, but a truck parked flat for a season is a truck that starts its next project with a degraded pack. The professional handover checklist, charge state, pack health report, charger inventory, damage survey, should be as formal as the crane inventory, because the fleet is earning equipment, not yard furniture.

Finally, structure the order around the project calendar: trucks ordered with the long-lead items at contract award, chargers staged with the temporary power package, and driver training scheduled into mobilisation week rather than after the first month of diesel operation, because the fleet that starts electric stays electric, while the fleet that starts diesel spends the project proving why it never switched.

Conclusion

Solar farm construction is the rare application where the electric truck's constraints vanish and its advantages concentrate: short repetitive loops, on-site power at construction and near-free power after commissioning, instant torque for soft ground, and an ESG narrative that the project is selling anyway. The i9's 96 kWh LFP pack, 430-kilometre working range and 4.5-tonne payload class fit the site's movements exactly. EPC contractors and developers looking to align their logistics with their product should start here.

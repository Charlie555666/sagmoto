X6s Hub-Reduction Axle: Ratios, Ground Clearance and Torque Multiplication

2026-11-27Product Deep Dive9 min read

Hub-reduction axles are common on heavy tippers and mining trucks, yet they are often misunderstood by buyers whose experience is limited to conventional single-reduction axles. The X6s is a 6x4 platform rated around 45 t GVW in tipper duty, powered by a Weichai WP12.480 delivering 480 HP and 2,200 Nm, and its rear axle is frequently specified in hub-reduction form. Understanding what the hub gearing does, and how the axle ratio shifts the balance between gradeability and road speed, is essential before a fleet commits to a specification. This article explains the mechanism, the ratio choices, the service implications and the way to match an axle to a real route rather than to a brochure.

Single-reduction versus hub-reduction axles

In a single-reduction axle, the whole final drive ratio is produced inside the differential carrier by the crown wheel and pinion. Drive passes through the differential, into the half-shafts and directly to the wheel hubs. The ratio is set by one gear pair, so a shorter ratio demands a smaller pinion and concentrates more stress in that pair. In a hub-reduction axle the differential runs a much milder ratio, and the remaining multiplication happens again at each wheel end through a compact planetary gear set. The total reduction is therefore split across two stages instead of one.

The practical consequence is that the heaviest torque work occurs outboard, in the hubs, where there is more physical space and where each side carries only half the total load. The differential and half-shafts see materially lower torque than they would in a single-reduction axle with the same overall ratio. For a loaded tipper that starts often on broken ground, this reduces peak stress in the most expensive driveline components and gives the axle a longer service life under abuse.

How the planetary hub stage works

Each hub carries a sun gear driven by the half-shaft, a set of planet gears running inside a ring gear fixed to the axle housing, and a carrier that drives the wheel flange. Because the ring gear is held stationary, the planets rotate as the sun turns, producing a speed reduction and a matching torque increase at the wheel. The hub stage typically contributes a reduction of roughly 1.5:1 to 2:1, with the differential supplying the rest of the overall ratio. The driver notices only the combined effect: stronger pulling at low speed and a lower road speed for a given engine rpm.

This arrangement also explains why hub-reduction axles are heavier and more expensive to rebuild. There are more gears, more bearings and more seals per wheel end, and each hub holds its own oil supply. The trade is accepted because the components that fail most often in single-reduction axles under high torque are relieved of part of their load.

Ratio choices: 4.77, 5.26 and 5.92

Three final drive ratios are commonly discussed for the X6s platform. A 4.77 ratio favours road speed and fuel economy on flatter haul routes. A 5.26 ratio is a middle setting for mixed terrain. A 5.92 ratio prioritises gradeability and loaded starts, at the cost of a lower cruising speed and higher engine rpm at a given velocity. None is universally correct; the right choice follows the gradient profile, the tyre diameter and the gross weight the truck actually carries. The indicative figures below assume a typical quarry tyre and should be confirmed against the exact tyre and gearbox fitted to your truck.

Key point: A shorter ratio does not create power; it trades road speed for wheel torque. On a 45 t tipper that climbs out of a pit every few minutes, the 5.92 ratio usually earns back its fuel penalty in clutch and driveline life. On a flat haul road the same ratio simply wastes fuel.

Torque multiplication and driveline loads

Multiplication happens in two places. The gearbox amplifies engine torque in the lower ratios, and the axle ratio amplifies it again before it reaches the wheel. With 2,200 Nm at the engine and a planetary hub stage sharing the final reduction, the torque at each wheel flange can be several times the engine figure in first gear. That is what allows a heavily loaded truck to move away on an incline without slipping the clutch or stalling. It is also why driveline components must be matched as a set: a stronger hub stage is of little use if the half-shafts or the differential pinion are marginal for the intended gross weight.

Ground clearance and differential protection

A hub-reduction axle keeps the differential carrier relatively compact because the carrier does not have to house the entire final reduction. That gives a useful gain in ground clearance beneath the centre of the axle, which matters on quarry floors, stockpiles and drainage crossings where a low protruding housing can be struck. The hub units themselves sit inside the wheels and are well protected by the rim and tyre. For operators working on ungraded site roads, this combination of a higher centre and shielded wheel ends is one of the strongest practical arguments for the design.

Clearance is still finite. A loaded truck settles on its springs, and a soft site ruts deeply under repeated passes. Drivers should approach ramps at low speed and avoid straddling sharp edges with the differential. Skid plates are worth considering where the route regularly crosses rock.

Oil volume, change intervals and service

Because the reduction is split, the differential and each hub are separate oil chambers with separate fill and drain points. This is easy to overlook during a quick service: a technician may change the centre oil and leave the hub oil untouched. Hub oil volume is modest per side, but it works at high temperature and under high load, so it should be changed on the interval specified for severe duty rather than stretched to a normal-road schedule. Sampling the oil at each change gives an early warning of gear or bearing wear.

Key point: A hub-reduction axle has three oil chambers, not one. Any service plan that changes only the differential oil has missed two thirds of the lubrication system, and planetary wear will follow.

Planetary set wear signs

Planetary gears wear gradually when lubrication is poor or when shock loading is frequent. Early signs include a faint whine from the wheel ends on a steady cruise, metal sheen in the hub oil, and a warm or hot hub after a normal run. A hub that runs noticeably hotter than its opposite side is a clear warning of bearing distress or oil starvation. Left alone, wear progresses to pitting on the gear teeth, increased backlash and eventually a broken planet or sun gear, which can leave the truck stranded and may damage the housing.

Diagnosis should start with the oil. A magnetic drain plug with a heavy coating of fine particles is normal to a degree, but large flakes or a milky colour indicate a problem. Compare both sides: symmetrical wear points to an oil or interval issue, while a single hot hub points to a local seal or bearing fault. A workshop that records hub temperatures and oil condition at each service will catch most failures before they become roadside events.

Why hub reduction suits high GVW at low speed

The design excels where gross weight is high and road speed is low. Quarry, construction and port work involves repeated acceleration from rest, short distances and frequent direction changes. Under those conditions the advantage of multiplying torque at the wheel end is greater than the penalty of extra weight and friction. The axle tolerates a heavy gross vehicle weight at low speed because the peak torque is distributed across two stages and two wheel ends rather than concentrated in one crown wheel. The extra unsprung mass at the hubs is a real cost in ride and tyre life, but on a site truck it is a small price for durability.

Fleets moving the same payload mainly on motorways should think again. At sustained high speed the hub gears add friction and heat without adding useful pulling power, and a single-reduction axle of suitable rating is usually more economical. The choice is a duty-cycle decision, not a matter of which design is inherently better.

Matching the ratio to tyre size and route profile

Tyre diameter changes the effective final drive. Fitting a taller tyre lengthens the gearing and reduces gradeability; a shorter tyre has the opposite effect. A fleet that switches tyre brand or size between seasons should recalculate the ratio before assuming the truck will still climb the same ramp. The gearbox matters too: a twelve-speed unit gives enough ratios to keep the WP12.480 in its efficient band across a range of speeds, which softens the compromise between a short and a long axle ratio.

The practical method is to define the worst loaded climb, the required start-on-gradient capability and the desired cruise speed, then work backwards to a ratio. Add a margin for tyre wear, since a worn tyre is effectively shorter. Fleets that carry varying loads should choose for the loaded case, because an under-geared empty truck is merely slower, while an over-geared loaded truck can be unable to start safely.

Import planning and total cost

Hub-reduction axles raise the purchase price and the parts bill, so the business case should be written before ordering. Budget for a complete hub rebuild kit per axle at the expected service point, including seals, bearings and a gear set, and confirm that the supplier can ship these items to your port within a reasonable lead time. Add the cost of a second oil grade if the hub specification differs from the differential. Training is another line: technicians used to single-reduction axles must be taught that each hub has its own oil chamber and its own torque sequence. Fleets that plan these items at order stage avoid the long downtime that follows a hub failure in a market with few skilled workshops. Final assessment

For a 45 t X6s tipper working quarry or mining duty, a hub-reduction axle with a 5.26 or 5.92 ratio is usually the right engineering answer. It protects the differential, raises the centre of the axle, and multiplies torque where the truck needs it most. The buyer must accept extra weight, more oil chambers and a more demanding service routine. Specify the ratio from the real route, insist on a complete hub service schedule, and confirm parts availability before shipment.

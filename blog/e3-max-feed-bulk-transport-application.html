SAGMOTO E3 MAX Bulk Feed Transport: Body Volume and Axle Load

2026-11-23Industry Application9 min read

Bulk feed looks simple until you try to haul it. Pellets and mash are light enough that a truck fills by volume long before it reaches its weight limit, yet they are dense enough to shift on every corner and to bridge over a discharge outlet. The SAGMOTO E3 MAX, with 430 to 520 HP, 2,000 to 2,400 N m and a Fast twelve-speed gearbox on an 8x4 chassis rated around 31 t GVW, is often chosen for bulk feed because it combines a long wheelbase with the torque to pull a full auger body. This guide covers body volume, bulk density, discharge hardware and axle loads for a feed specification that works on a real farm round.

How do you size the body volume?

Feed vehicles are sold by cubic metres, not by tonnes. A practical bulk body on an 8x4 chassis ranges from about 30 to 45 m3, divided into two to four compartments so the driver can carry several recipes in one trip. The right volume comes from multiplying the intended payload by the bulk density of the feed. Most poultry and pig pellets fall between 0.55 and 0.70 tonnes per cubic metre, while mash and meal can be lighter at 0.45 to 0.55. A 35 m3 body carrying pellets at 0.65 t/m3 holds about 22.7 t before the body weight is counted.

Because the chassis is rated near 31 t GVW, a body that is too large will be limited by weight rather than space, and the operator pays for volume that can never be used. A body that is too small means extra trips to the mill. Calculate both limits, then choose the volume that lets the truck reach its legal payload with a normal mix of products.

How does the discharge auger work?

Most bulk feed bodies discharge through a horizontal auger running along the floor, feeding a vertical or inclined auger at the rear that delivers the feed into a farm bin. A typical horizontal auger on a body of this size is 150 to 200 mm in diameter and unloads at 1 to 3 tonnes per minute, depending on the product and the auger speed. The rear auger can be extended hydraulically to reach bins several metres from the truck, which matters on farms where the bin is not beside the road.

Drive can come from a gearbox PTO or from a dedicated hydraulic motor. A PTO drive is simple and draws on the E3 MAX engine's ample low-speed torque, but it requires the driver to hold a set rev count while unloading. A hydraulic drive gives finer control over auger speed and reduces the chance of crushing fragile pellets, at the cost of a pump and oil circuit. Specify a variable-speed drive where product quality matters.

Key point: Size the auger for the unloading rate the farm expects, not for the largest number on a catalogue. A body that takes forty-five minutes to empty ties up the truck and annoys the customer, while an oversized auger can damage fragile pellets.

Why do compartments and liners matter?

A bulk feed truck often carries two or more recipes in one round, so the body is divided by vertical bulkheads into sealed compartments. Each compartment needs its own discharge gate and its own aeration or fluidising pad so the feed flows toward the auger instead of sticking. Gates that leak allow one recipe to contaminate the next, which is unacceptable in poultry feed where a medication must be delivered precisely.

Liners deserve attention too. Cracked grain is abrasive and wears a painted steel floor quickly, while some additives are corrosive. A stainless or heavy polymer liner in the high-wear zone adds cost but extends body life. How do axle loads constrain the load?

On an 8x4 E3 MAX, a 30 to 45 m3 body is long and the load sits high. If the body is mounted too far back, the tandem axles take most of the weight and the front axle becomes light, reducing steering response and braking. Weigh the truck empty and again with a full load of the densest feed you carry, and compare both states with the axle ratings. Aim to keep at least 20 to 25 percent of the gross weight on the front axle.

What about dust and product quality?

Bulk feed generates dust during loading and discharge, and a truck without proper sealing will coat the farm and the driver. Fit sealed hatches, a dust collector or filter on the body vent, and an enclosed discharge chute that can be lowered onto the bin. Dust is not just a nuisance: it is a fire and explosion risk in confined spaces, and feed mills increasingly require trucks to meet dust-control standards.

Product quality also suffers from moisture. A body that is not sealed will let rain into the feed, causing caking and mould. Inspect hatch seals and the vent filter before every load, and wash the body on a defined schedule when switching between medicated and unmedicated feed. The documentation of that wash is often part of a feed mill's quality assurance requirements.

Key point: A bulk feed body must protect the product as well as carry it. Sealed compartments, a working vent filter and a documented wash-down routine are what keep a load acceptable at the farm gate.

How should you plan the round?

The economics of bulk feed depend on tonnes per hour, not on the truck's top speed. A round with many small farms and short unloading stops favours a smaller body with a fast auger, because time spent positioning and discharging dominates. A round with a few large farms favours the biggest legal body, since highway travel is a smaller share of the shift. Map the stops and the unloading time before choosing the body volume.

Fuel is part of that calculation. The E3 MAX 430 to 520 HP engine is more than enough for a loaded feed body, but an oversized engine running at low revs during frequent stops wastes fuel. Match the gearbox and rear-axle ratio to the route: a highway-heavy round benefits from a tall ratio, while a hilly district with frequent stops needs more torque multiplication.

Driver training belongs in the same plan. A driver who understands how the fluidising pads and gates work will unload faster and spot a bridging problem before it becomes a blocked auger. Include a practical session at delivery covering gate sequence, rear auger extension and the correct shutdown order, then check unload times during the first month. A few minutes of coaching at the start saves far more time than a call-out to clear a packed compartment on a farm road.

How do you manage medicated feed and traceability?

Many farms receive feed with an included medication, and the delivery record must show which compartment carried it. A body with four compartments lets the driver separate medicated from unmedicated lots, but only if the gates are clearly marked and the loading sheet matches the physical layout. Confusing two lots is a serious error, so require a colour-coded gate system and a signed load sheet that travels with the truck.

Wash-down between a medicated and a plain load is normally required by the feed mill, and the wash must be documented. Include a water tank and a drain point on the body so the driver can flush the auger and compartments on site, and keep a log of each wash with the date and the previous product. Mills that audit their carriers will ask for this record, and a truck that cannot produce it may be refused at the gate.

What maintenance does the body add?

Beyond the chassis service schedule, check auger flight wear, gearbox oil in the drive unit, gate seals, and the condition of the fluidising pads at regular intervals. A worn auger loses unloading speed gradually, which operators often notice only when a customer complains. Keep a record of unload times for each body; a rising trend is an early warning of wear.

Bulk feed transport is a volume business governed by weight. Choose the body from the bulk density of the feed you actually carry, size the auger for the unloading time the farm expects, and check the axle loads at the heaviest product. On the E3 MAX 8x4 those pieces can be balanced into a truck that makes money on a tight round. Ignore any one of them and the truck will either run overweight or spend its life making extra trips.

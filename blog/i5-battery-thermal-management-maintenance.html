Why Battery Thermal Management Matters on the SAGMOTO i5

Heavy-duty electric light trucks place most of their durability risk inside the battery enclosure rather than on the road. The SAGMOTO i5 light electric truck uses a roughly 98 kWh lithium iron phosphate pack sized for urban delivery duty, and that pack lives or dies by temperature control. When cell temperature climbs above the safe window during repeated fast charging or falls too low in winter, the battery management system throttles power and shortens service life. The thermal management loop is the only thing standing between normal fleet operation and an expensive pack replacement, so a disciplined maintenance routine pays for itself quickly.

Fleet operators who run the i5 on SAGMOTO new energy electric trucks platforms usually see the first cooling symptoms at around 40,000 to 60,000 km, when coolant flow restriction or plate fouling raises pack temperature by several degrees under load. Catching that early is the difference between a low-cost flush and a damaged module. This guide covers the inspection routine, the service intervals, the coolant specification and the failure patterns you should train your workshop to recognise before they strand a vehicle.

How the Liquid Cooling Plate Works

The i5 pack is built around aluminium cooling plates seated beneath the modules, with a low-conductivity water-glycol loop pumped through a front-mounted radiator. A dedicated electric coolant pump circulates fluid whenever the battery management system detects a temperature gradient between cells or a pack temperature outside the 15 to 35 degree Celsius comfort band. In cooling mode the loop rejects heat to ambient air; in heating mode a positive temperature coefficient heater warms the fluid before it reaches the plates so the pack can accept charge in cold weather.

Because the plates sit in direct contact with module bases, any loss of thermal interface material, any air pocket in the loop, or any drop in pump pressure shows up first as uneven cell temperatures. The battery management system logs the spread between the hottest and coldest cell group, and a growing spread is the earliest reliable warning sign of a thermal management fault that a visual check would miss entirely.

Reading the Battery Management System Alarms

The i5 dashboard reports thermal status through a battery temperature icon and, when thresholds are crossed, a dedicated cooling fault code. A single steady icon during fast charging is normal; a flashing icon combined with a reduced power message means the loop cannot hold the target band. Technicians should connect the diagnostic tool and read the live cell temperature map rather than guessing from the dashboard alone, because the real problem is usually one outlier module, not the whole pack.

Export workshops should log every thermal alarm in the vehicle file. A pattern of repeated alarms at the same odometer interval points to a wearing pump or a partially blocked radiator, while random single events often trace back to a hard driving session in extreme heat. Trend data turns unpredictable breakdowns into scheduled maintenance you control.

Step by Step Inspection Routine

Begin every preventive visit with a scan of the battery management system live data. Record the maximum cell temperature, the minimum cell temperature and the spread between them. A healthy i5 running in moderate conditions should show a spread under 5 degrees Celsius. If the spread exceeds 8 degrees after the loop has run for ten minutes, suspect a flow restriction or a failed pump stage and escalate before the next duty cycle.

Next, inspect the reservoir sight glass and confirm the level sits near the midpoint. A slowly dropping level with no visible external leak points to an internal weep or a cracked plate joint. Finally, run the cooling fan and pump through a manual activation cycle from the diagnostic tool and confirm the radiator fan ramps to its scheduled speed and the pump draws its expected current without voltage sag.

Treat any cell-to-cell temperature spread above 8 degrees Celsius as a stop-drive condition until the loop is inspected. Running the pack while one module overheats silently ages the entire battery and can void the thermal warranty on the 98 kWh LFP unit.

Coolant Selection and Replacement

Use only the low-conductivity ethylene glycol or propylene glycol mix specified for the i5 loop, typically a 1:1 concentration with deionised water giving a freeze point near minus 35 degrees Celsius. Never top up with tap water, because dissolved minerals raise conductivity and accelerate corrosion at the plate joints. When replacing coolant, drain from the lowest point, flush with deionised water, then fill slowly while the pump runs to purge air.

Colour is not a specification. Two green coolants from different suppliers can have incompatible inhibitor packages, and mixing them can drop the protection rating within a single season. Always label the container and record the batch on the service sheet so a later top-up uses the identical formula and preserves the corrosion warranty.

Bleeding the Loop Correctly

Bleeding trapped air is the single most skipped step in i5 cooling work. An air pocket sits at the top of a plate and creates a local hot spot that the battery management system cannot correct because the sensor reads the cooled side of the module. After filling, run three pump-bleed cycles with the front of the truck raised slightly on ramps so air migrates toward the reservoir.

Recheck the reservoir after each cycle and top up to the midpoint before the next run. Only when the live cell temperature spread stays under 5 degrees during a forced cooling test should the vehicle be released. Skipping this step is the most common reason a freshly serviced i5 returns with the same thermal alarm a week later.

Stock one spare coolant pump and one set of interface pads per ten i5 trucks in your fleet. Pump failures during peak summer are the most common cause of stranded electric light trucks, and a same-day swap keeps the vehicle earning instead of sitting on a hoist.

Seasonal Operation Tips

In hot markets, park the i5 in shade and avoid immediate DC fast charging straight after a heavy load, because the pack enters the charger already warm and the loop cannot shed heat fast enough. Pre-cooling the cabin while plugged in also helps, since the compressor load then draws from grid power rather than from the battery. Both habits reduce thermal stress and extend pack life well beyond the basic warranty period.

In cold markets, pre-condition the battery while still plugged in so the heater draws from grid power rather than from the pack, preserving range. Operators who rotate drivers should brief everyone on the dashboard battery temperature icon, because a driver who reports a rising temperature warning early prevents the cascading throttles that otherwise arrive without obvious symptoms until performance has already dropped.

Workshop Tools and Stock Planning

A well-prepared export workshop should hold the following for the i5 thermal loop: one coolant pump per ten vehicles, two litres of approved coolant concentrate, a spare radiator fan relay, a set of hose clamps, and interface pads for at least two module removals. A low-cost conductivity meter and an infrared thermometer complete the kit and let a technician confirm a repair without a full road test.

Training Your Drivers

Driver behaviour shapes battery temperature more than most fleets expect. Coaching drivers to avoid back-to-back DC fast charges, to pre-condition in extreme weather, and to report the temperature icon at the first sign of trouble removes a large share of avoidable thermal faults. A five-minute weekly briefing costs nothing and protects the most expensive component on the truck.

Pair the briefing with a simple logbook where drivers note charging location, ambient temperature and any warning. Over a few months the workshop gains a clear picture of which routes and which chargers stress the pack most, and can adjust schedules or coolant service frequency accordingly.

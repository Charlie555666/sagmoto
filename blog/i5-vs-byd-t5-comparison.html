SAGMOTO i5 vs BYD T5: Light Electric Truck Comparison 2026

2026-09-16SAGMOTO BlogShaanxi Fenghan Trading Co., Ltd.

The Light Electric Segment Grows Up

The light electric truck segment — 4-8 tonne GVWR battery-electric distribution vehicles — has moved from pilot projects to genuine fleet procurement. Urban low-emission zones, e-commerce last-mile growth and the maturing economics of LFP battery chemistry have combined to make the business case real for daily-route operators. And because the segment's center of gravity is Chinese manufacturing, the shortlist for most international fleet buyers now features Chinese platforms — with BYD's T5 series and SAGMOTO's i5 among the most export-ready options.

The i5, part of the SAGMOTO new energy electric trucks program, is a light-duty electric distribution truck built around an approximately 98 kWh LFP battery pack with a working range in the 200-270 km band depending on payload, temperature and duty. The BYD T5 — available in multiple variants including the T5DD and larger T5 platform variants with battery options spanning roughly 80-100+ kWh in different market versions — brings BYD's formidable vertical integration: BYD manufactures its own blade-format LFP cells, e-axles and electronics. This comparison is written for fleet operators evaluating both on delivery-route duty.

This is a contest between two competent LFP platforms, and the decision is rarely won on the spec sheet. It is won on total delivered cost into the operator's market, charging-integration support, battery warranty terms, and body configuration flexibility — the areas where an export-focused supply model and a vertically-integrated OEM genuinely differ.

The headline observation: on the core numbers — capacity, range, chemistry, warranty duration — the two platforms are functionally equivalent. Both use LFP chemistry for the right reasons (thermal stability in hot climates, cycle life under daily full-charge operation, no cobalt supply-chain exposure). Both offer DC fast charging for mid-shift top-ups and AC overnight depot charging. Both are engineered around the same operating model: depot-based daily routes of 100-250 km.

Where BYD's Vertical Integration Shows

BYD's core strength is cell manufacturing scale. As one of the world's largest LFP cell producers, BYD controls its battery cost base in a way no vehicle OEM using third-party cells can match, and its blade cell architecture brings genuine structural and thermal advantages. In markets where BYD has established commercial-vehicle distribution, the T5 benefits from that infrastructure — trained technicians, parts pipelines, and brand credibility that extends from BYD's passenger-car and bus dominance in many emerging markets. Fleet buyers in Southeast Asia, Latin America and parts of Europe will find BYD's local presence a real advantage.

Where the i5's Export Model Shows

The i5's strengths are the mirror image. As a product of the SAGMOTO commercial vehicle program distributed through Shaanxi Fenghan Trading's export channels, the i5 is specified and supported for exactly the procurement patterns international fleet buyers actually use:

  • Configuration flexibility: factory-coordinated body configurations — refrigerated, box, stake, tipper interfaces — built to the operator's route requirements rather than to a dealer's stock profile.
  • Fleet-scale commercial terms: direct export pricing without the multi-layer distributor margin stack that vertical-integration OEMs often carry in overseas markets.
  • Charging and deployment support: route-energy modeling, depot charging planning, and driver familiarization delivered as part of the procurement rather than as aftermarket consulting.
  • Battery serviceability philosophy: LFP pack architecture designed for module-level service and second-life deployment, with warranty administration through documentation for remote fleets.

Range and Energy Consumption in Real Duty

Both platforms' published ranges are laboratory-adjacent figures; what fleets experience is duty-cycle reality. Light electric trucks on urban distribution consume 55-85 kWh/100km depending on stop density, payload, climate control use and temperature — the spread between a summer parcel route in a flat city and a winter grocery route in a hilly one is wider than the spread between these two trucks. The i5's ~98 kWh pack underwrites a comfortable full-day route in the 150-220 km band with 20-30 percent reserve — the planning margin that prevents route-restriction anxiety — and its active thermal management holds range stability in the 35°C+ ambients of Gulf, South Asian and Southeast Asian deployment zones. BYD's thermal management is equally competent; in the temperature band between 0°C and 40°C, neither platform shows a decisive range advantage.

Battery Warranty and End-of-Life Planning

Both platforms offer 8-year battery warranty terms in export configurations — the industry-standard window that aligns battery risk with fleet ownership cycles. The fine print differs in ways buyers should examine: capacity-retention thresholds (the percentage of original capacity at which warranty remedies trigger), whether remedies are repair, replacement or prorated credit, and the diagnostic documentation required to support claims from remote territories. The i5's documentation-based claims process is designed for fleets far from any service center; BYD's claims process routes through its local channel where one exists. Fleets should request the actual warranty policy documents from both suppliers and compare the retention threshold and remedy structure — these terms matter more than the headline year count.

Charging Integration and Grid Reality

Depot charging is the segment's operating model, and the practical integration work — grid connection applications, charger selection, charge management software, and overnight scheduling — is where deployment timelines succeed or fail. A 20-truck light electric fleet charging overnight at 20-40 kW per vehicle needs a grid connection in the 0.5-1 MW class, plus charge management that staggers sessions across the night window to avoid demand peaks. The i5 procurement package includes depot charging planning support; BYD offers comparable integration through its market organizations where established. The differentiator is availability in your territory — the supplier who will actually sit in the utility meeting matters more than the supplier with the better brochure.

Which Fleet Should Choose Which

Choose the BYD T5 when: your market has established BYD commercial-vehicle distribution and service; brand familiarity matters to your organization or customers; and local channel financing terms are favorable.

Choose the SAGMOTO i5 when: your procurement favors direct export supply with factory-configured bodies; your market lacks dense BYD truck service presence; you want route-energy modeling and charging planning embedded in the purchase; and your fleet pairs light electric units with other SAGMOTO vehicles for consolidated parts and support.

After-Sales Networks and the Second-Owner Question

Light electric trucks will change owners. Fleet cycles in the segment run 5-8 years, and the second and third ownership phases — smaller operators, secondary cities, mixed-use duty — determine residual values and therefore first-owner economics. The after-sales question thus extends beyond the purchase decision into the resale equation, and the two platforms answer it differently.

BYD's channel-led model concentrates capability where its market organizations exist: first-owner fleets near BYD service points enjoy strong support, and the used-truck market prices that proximity into resale values. In territories without BYD commercial-vehicle presence, however, used T5 units depend on independent workshops whose battery-system competence is still developing — a gap that used-market buyers price conservatively.

The i5's model distributes capability differently. Its documentation-based support, generic-tool diagnostics philosophy and module-level battery serviceability are designed for exactly the independent-workshop, second-owner environment that prevails in most emerging markets. A used i5 arriving in a secondary city is supported by: an LFP pack whose module architecture permits local repair, a diagnostic approach that does not require brand tooling, and a parts channel flowing through consolidated export logistics rather than a dealer gate. For fleet buyers who underwrite resale value on serviceability rather than brand presence — and for markets where dealer networks thin out beyond the capital — this distributed model holds value where channel-dependent models do not.

The practical guidance for first owners: whichever platform you choose, preserve the documentation. Battery service history, charging logs, and warranty-transfer paperwork are the used-market's primary risk-reduction instruments for electric trucks, and complete records are worth several percentage points of resale value in every emerging used-EV market surveyed to date. Fleets that treat documentation as an asset class — archived per unit, transferable at sale — close much of the residual-value uncertainty that otherwise colors the electric-versus-diesel and platform-versus-platform decisions alike.

Conclusion

The i5 and T5 are both credible, well-engineered LFP light electric trucks from China's strongest commercial-vehicle manufacturing base — and on the core numbers they are functionally equivalent. The decision is commercial: delivered cost in your market, support model fit for your geography, and body configuration for your routes.

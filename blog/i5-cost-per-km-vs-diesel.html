SAGMOTO i5 Cost per Kilometre Against a Diesel Light Truck

2026-11-18Buying & Import Guide10 min read

Electric light trucks are usually sold on two claims: lower energy cost and lower maintenance. Both are true, and both are routinely overstated by comparing the wrong numbers. A diesel light truck's fuel cost is easy to see and easy to inflate in a sales presentation; the electric truck's cost per kilometre includes charger efficiency losses, demand charges and a purchase price gap that has to be recovered from somewhere. A fair comparison puts every line on the table and lets the annual kilometres decide.

The SAGMOTO i5 is the electric light and medium platform: a 4x2 chassis with roughly 106 to 160 kWh of battery capacity, a 120 kW class motor and a gross weight range suited to urban distribution and last-mile delivery. It competes directly with diesel light trucks on the same routes. This article builds a cost per kilometre comparison from first principles, then shows the annual mileage at which the electric truck pays back its price premium in different electricity markets.

Key point: On urban distribution at 40,000 km a year, an i5 typically runs at USD 0.19 to 0.26 per km against USD 0.31 to 0.40 for an equivalent diesel, including finance and depreciation. Below about 20,000 km a year the price premium rarely pays back inside five years, whatever the fuel saving.

The five lines that make up cost per kilometre

Cost per kilometre has five components: energy, maintenance and repairs, tyres, finance and depreciation, and the residual value adjustment. The first two favour the electric truck strongly, the third is roughly neutral, and the last two depend entirely on the price gap and the resale assumption. Any comparison that omits finance and depreciation is not a comparison; it is a fuel calculation.

The correct basis is total cost of ownership over a defined period, divided by the kilometres driven in that period. Five years and 200,000 km is a reasonable basis for urban distribution, with the residual value of both vehicles estimated at the end. On that basis the electric truck's advantage is real but narrower than the fuel-only figure suggests, and it is highly sensitive to annual mileage and electricity price.

Energy: kWh per kilometre against litres per 100 km

A diesel light truck in urban distribution typically consumes 13 to 17 litres per 100 km loaded, depending on traffic and payload. At USD 1.00 per litre that is USD 0.13 to USD 0.17 per km. An i5 in the same duty consumes 0.55 to 0.75 kWh per km at the wheels, and 0.62 to 0.85 kWh per km at the meter once charger and cable losses are included.

The electricity price is the variable that decides the outcome. At USD 0.09 per kWh the electric truck's energy cost is roughly a third of the diesel's. At USD 0.20 per kWh, with demand charges included, the advantage narrows to 20 to 30 percent. In markets with commercial tariffs above USD 0.25 per kWh the energy saving can nearly disappear, and the case then rests entirely on maintenance and on policy incentives.

Key point: Electricity price decides the energy case. Below USD 0.15 per kWh the i5 is clearly cheaper to run; above USD 0.25 per kWh including demand charges the advantage is marginal and the decision must rest on maintenance, incentives and route access rules.

Maintenance: where the electric advantage is largest

An electric drivetrain removes the engine, the gearbox, the clutch, the exhaust aftertreatment, the EGR system and the fuel system. Those components account for the majority of a diesel light truck's maintenance cost after the warranty period. What remains on the i5 is brakes, tyres, suspension, steering, the cooling system for the battery and motor, and the electrical system itself.

In practice, urban fleets report electric light truck maintenance at 40 to 60 percent of the diesel equivalent per kilometre, with the largest savings in the 100,000 to 200,000 km range where diesel aftertreatment and injection repairs dominate. Brake life is also longer because regenerative braking does much of the deceleration work, which on a stop-start urban route is a significant saving in itself.

Purchase price and payback

The electric truck costs more to buy, and the gap is usually 35 to 60 percent before incentives. On a light truck priced at USD 32,000 diesel against USD 48,000 electric, the gap is USD 16,000. That gap has to be recovered from energy and maintenance savings, and the recovery rate depends almost entirely on annual kilometres.

Those figures assume electricity at USD 0.12 per kWh and diesel at USD 1.00 per litre, and they exclude incentives. Where purchase subsidies or tax relief exist, payback shortens substantially. Where electricity is expensive or the route requires a battery size that adds cost without adding payload, it lengthens.

Utilisation matters more than price

The single most important variable is not the price gap or the electricity tariff; it is how many kilometres the truck covers each year. An electric truck that sits idle cannot repay its premium, and an electric truck on a 60 km urban route doing six days a week will repay it quickly. This is why electric light trucks work best in high-utilisation urban fleets and worst in low-utilisation, long-distance or seasonal operations.

Utilisation also determines whether the battery capacity is right. Buying a 160 kWh pack for a route that needs 90 kWh adds cost and weight for capability that is never used. Sizing the pack to the actual daily route, with a 20 to 25 percent margin, is the single biggest cost lever in the specification.

Battery warranty, degradation and residual value

The battery is the largest single component of the electric truck's residual value, and its condition at year five decides the resale. A pack that has been charged mostly overnight at moderate power and kept in the 20 to 80 percent band will typically retain 80 to 88 percent of its original capacity at 200,000 km. A pack that has been fast-charged daily from near empty to full will retain less, and the difference is worth thousands at resale.

Battery warranty terms on export trucks should be read carefully, because they usually specify a capacity retention threshold and a time and mileage limit, and they often require documented charging behaviour or periodic inspections. Fleets that keep a simple charging log — date, starting and ending state of charge, charger power — satisfy most warranty conditions and also build the evidence that supports a better resale price. That log costs nothing and protects the largest asset on the vehicle.

Where diesel still wins

Diesel remains the better choice in three situations. Long-distance routes beyond the battery range with no reliable charging, operations in markets with expensive or unreliable electricity, and low-utilisation fleets where the premium cannot be recovered. In those cases the honest answer is that the economics do not work yet, and buying electric anyway is a policy decision rather than a commercial one.

There is also a practical constraint that no cost model captures: route access. Some cities restrict diesel vehicles from certain zones, and some customers require zero-emission delivery as a contract condition. Where that applies, the electric truck wins regardless of payback, because the alternative is losing the contract. Conclusion

The i5 competes with diesel on cost per kilometre, and the comparison is favourable in the right application. Energy costs roughly a third to two thirds of diesel, maintenance is 40 to 60 percent lower, and the total advantage on an urban route at 40,000 km a year is USD 0.19 to 0.26 per km against USD 0.31 to 0.40. The premium of USD 14,000 to USD 18,000 pays back in under three years at that utilisation and does not pay back at 15,000 km. Size the battery to the route, charge on the cheapest tariff available, and let the annual kilometres make the decision.

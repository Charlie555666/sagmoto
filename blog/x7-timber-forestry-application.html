SAGMOTO X7 Timber and Forestry Transport: Bunk, Bogle and Load Security

2026-11-24Industry Application9 min read

Timber haulage is one of the hardest jobs a medium truck can be asked to do. Logs are heavy, awkward, uneven and unforgiving of a careless load, and the roads that lead to a forest are often steep, wet and narrow. The SAGMOTO X7 is a flexible medium-special platform offered across four-by-two, six-by-four and eight-by-four layouts with engines ranging from light special specifications up to roughly 340 to 400 horsepower, which makes a six-by-four version a natural candidate for a log truck. Success in this role depends less on raw power than on the bunk and stake design, the way the load is restrained, the axle and overhang limits, and the ability to stop safely on a descent. This guide sets out how to specify an X7 for timber and forestry work.

A loaded log truck behaves differently from a box truck. The cargo sits high, the centre of gravity shifts as logs settle, and a lateral movement of even a few centimetres can change the rollover threshold on a cambered forest track. Long logs overhang the body at the rear and sometimes the front, which moves weight away from the axles and affects braking and traction. The driver may be working under a loading crane where speed of turnaround matters, yet a single poorly secured log can cause a serious incident on the public road. The specification has to serve both the forest track and the highway leg, because most timber trucks spend time on each.

Key point: On a timber truck, the body and restraint system are as important as the chassis. A well-designed bunk and a disciplined strapping routine protect the driver and the load far more effectively than extra engine power.

Bunk and stake design for a six-by-four X7

The bunk is the structure that carries the logs, and the stakes are the uprights that keep them from rolling sideways. A six-by-four X7 used for timber should have a bunk built on a reinforced sub-frame that spreads the load along the chassis rails rather than concentrating it at a few mounting points. The cross members need to be spaced to suit the log lengths in the concession, and the deck should allow water and mud to drain so that the steel is not standing in a wet, abrasive slurry. Stakes are normally forged or fabricated from heavy section steel and should be removable or foldable to make loading with a crane faster. A stake that bends under a sideways push is doing its job; one that snaps is a failure that needs a design change.

Stake height should match the way the timber is stacked. A single layer of large logs needs tall, widely spaced stakes, while smaller pulpwood stacked in several layers needs closer uprights to stop the upper layers shifting. Some operators fit a mid-height cross strap or a chain over the top of the load in addition to the stakes, because a tall stack can bulge between the uprights on a rough track. The bunk should also provide a clear, level surface for the loader operator to place logs quickly, since a crooked first layer makes every later layer unstable. Buyers who describe their average log length, diameter and stack height to the body builder will get a far more useful bunk than one ordered from a generic drawing.

Bogle or tag axle for long logs

Long timber creates an overhang problem. When logs extend well beyond the rear of the truck, a significant part of their weight sits behind the last axle, which unloads the front axle and can make the vehicle wander or lose steering grip. It also increases the bending moment on the frame at the rear. A bogle, sometimes called a tag axle or a trailing axle arrangement, addresses this by placing an additional axle further back under the overhanging load. That extra support carries part of the rear weight, restores a more balanced axle distribution and reduces frame stress. On a timber truck that regularly carries long logs, a bogle is a practical answer to a problem that a shorter chassis cannot solve.

A bogle adds cost, weight and maintenance, so it should be specified on the basis of the actual log length and the legal limits in the operating country. Where logs are short and the load sits fully over the drive axles, a standard six-by-four chassis may be sufficient and simpler to maintain. Where the truck runs long sawlogs with a metre or more of rear overhang, the bogle pays for itself through better stability, reduced tyre wear at the drive axles and lower frame fatigue. The buyer must also check that the extra axle can be lifted or unloaded when the truck runs empty or light, because dragging an unladen tag axle wastes tyre life and fuel. A lift arrangement is worth specifying when the return trip is often empty.

Key point: If the logs overhang the rear bunk significantly, a bogle axle is not an optional extra. It restores axle balance, protects the frame and improves stability, but it should be lift-capable if the truck runs back to the depot empty.

Load restraint standards and daily routine

Load restraint on a timber truck is a discipline, not a one-time fitting. Logs must be chained or strapped so that they cannot move forward under braking, sideways on a camber or backward on a gradient. Chains should be rated for the load, fitted with tensioners that the driver can reach safely, and hooked to anchor points that are welded and rated rather than improvised on site. Each chain needs a clear path over the load and should be tightened after the first few kilometres, because logs settle into the bunk and a chain that was tight at the loading bay can be slack after a short run. A driver who re-checks the restraints at the first stop has done more for safety than any amount of paperwork.

The working pattern at a landing adds its own risk. A loader places logs from above, and the driver should be out of the cab only when the machine operator can see them clearly and the load is stable. Once loaded, a walk-around check should confirm that no log protrudes into the path of the cab or the exhaust, that the stakes are fully seated and that every chain is tensioned. On the road, a defensive driving style matters: timber shifts its weight in a corner, and a truck that feels stable when empty will roll at a much lower speed when carrying a high, dense stack. Fleets that write a short restraint checklist and require a signature at the loading bay see fewer load-loss incidents than those that leave the routine to habit.

Axle loads, overhang rules and route planning

Legal limits on axle load and rear overhang are a hard boundary for a timber operation, and they vary between countries and sometimes between road classes within a country. A buyer should obtain the local rules in writing before fixing the bunk length and the bogle position, because a body that is legal in one province can be an overweight offence in the next. The calculation starts with the gross vehicle weight, then distributes that weight across the steer, drive and tag axles with the load in its typical position. A log truck that passes the scale empty but exceeds the front axle limit when loaded has been specified badly, and no amount of careful driving will correct it.

Overhang rules usually limit how far the load can extend beyond the last axle, both for safety and to protect bridges and other vehicles. A bogle helps by moving the effective support point rearward, but the driver still needs to know the maximum permissible projection and to load accordingly. Route planning is part of compliance: a forest road may allow a longer combination than the public highway on the last leg into town, so the operator should know where the boundary lies and reload or reconfigure if necessary. Buyers comparing medium chassis for this work can review the SAGMOTO cargo truck flatbed box stake configurations to understand how body length and frame strength interact with axle ratings before committing to a timber body.

Traction, descent braking and driver protection

Forest roads test traction and braking at the same time. A loaded truck needs grip to climb a wet, rutted track and then enough braking capacity to come down the same gradient with a high centre of gravity. A six-by-four X7 gives useful drive-axle traction, and the buyer can improve it further with the right tyre pattern, a suitable final-drive ratio and differential locks used only when needed. On the descent, the service brakes must handle repeated application without fading, and an engine brake or exhaust brake is worth specifying so the driver can control speed without riding the foot brake. Cooling capacity matters here as well, because a long downhill with the retarder working transfers a large amount of heat into the cooling system. A pre-trip brake check is not optional on a timber route.

Driver protection behind the headboard is the final piece of the specification. A log that shifts forward under hard braking can enter the cab, so a certified headboard built to a known load rating should stand between the load and the driver. The cab itself should have a sound roof and secure glazing, and the seat belt should be worn even on short forest shuttles. Mirrors and a rear-facing camera help the driver see the overhanging load and the condition of the stakes without leaving the seat. A busy forestry contractor may replace trucks on a predictable cycle, but the safety equipment should never be treated as an optional trim level. Specifying the headboard, the restraint system and the braking package together, on a six-by-four X7 chosen for the real route, produces a timber truck that is productive without asking the driver to accept an unnecessary risk.

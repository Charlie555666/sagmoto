SAGMOTO i9 Cab Electrical Service: 24V Harness and HV Interlock Checks

2026-11-24Maintenance & After-sales9 min read

The SAGMOTO i9 is a battery-electric heavy truck, with an e-motor in the 250 to 350 kW range, an LFP battery of roughly 282 to 350 kWh and a 6x4 or 8x4 chassis rated between 31 and 49 tonnes. It has no AdBlue tank, no SCR catalyst, no DPF, no EGR system and no engine clutch to service, so the maintenance conversation moves to the electrical architecture instead. The cab still runs on a 24V low-voltage supply, but that supply now comes from a DC-DC converter fed by the high-voltage pack, and a chain of safety interlocks and monitoring circuits surrounds it. This guide covers the checks a technician can make on the low-voltage harness, the converter, the interlock loop, the CAN network, insulation monitoring and battery thermal management, always beginning with a safe isolation procedure.

What makes the i9 24V low-voltage harness different?

In a diesel truck the 24V system is charged by an alternator driven from the engine. On the i9 there is no engine accessory drive for an alternator; the 24V bus is supplied by a DC-DC converter that steps voltage down from the traction battery. The harness itself is familiar territory: lighting, instrumentation, cabin HVAC controls, door modules, telematics and the controller power feeds all run on 24V. What changes is the consequence of a fault. A low-voltage brownout can confuse a controller that also supervises high-voltage contactors, so a wiring problem in the cab can present as a traction fault rather than a simple blown fuse.

Inspect the harness where it passes through the cab-to-chassis bulkhead and around the steering column and pedal box, because those are the points where vibration and flexing chafe insulation. Check that connectors are fully seated and that their locking tabs are intact, and look for green corrosion on pins near door and floor connectors. Measure voltage at the distribution box with the vehicle awake and again after it has slept; a slow discharge of the 24V bus often traces to a module that fails to enter sleep mode and drains the auxiliary battery over a few days.

How do you check the DC-DC converter?

The DC-DC converter is the bridge between the high-voltage pack and the 24V bus, typically rated in the range of 5 to 10 kW on a heavy electric truck. A converter that is failing will show up as a low-voltage system that sags under load: the cab lights dim when the HVAC blower starts, or a controller logs an undervoltage code during a steering or braking event. Measure the converter output at its terminals, not only at the battery, and compare it with the reading at the distribution box. A difference between the two points indicates resistance in the cabling or a poor ground rather than a converter fault.

Check the converter's cooling circuit as carefully as its electrical output. Many units are liquid-cooled from the same loop that manages the battery and drive electronics, and a restricted cooler will cause the converter to derate its output on a hot day. Inspect for leaks, confirm that the coolant level is correct and read any recorded converter temperature from the diagnostic tool before condemning the unit.

Key point: Measure DC-DC output at the converter terminals and again at the distribution box. A voltage difference between those points points to cable or ground resistance, while a uniform sag points to the converter or its cooling circuit.

What is the HV interlock loop and why does it matter?

The high-voltage interlock loop is a series circuit that passes through every service disconnect, connector and access cover on the high-voltage system. If any of those points is opened, the loop breaks and the vehicle control unit commands the main contactors open within a fraction of a second, de-energising the high-voltage bus. On the i9 the loop includes the battery service disconnect, the motor and inverter covers, the charging inlet and the DC-DC connector. A broken interlock is a safety feature doing its job, not a nuisance fault to be bypassed.

When the truck reports an interlock fault, walk the vehicle and reseat every high-voltage connector and cover in the loop rather than jumping the circuit. A connector that is not fully latched after a service operation is the usual cause, and the diagnostic tool will normally identify which segment of the loop is open. Never defeat the interlock to keep a vehicle running; it is the mechanism that protects anyone who opens a high-voltage component.

How do you read CAN bus faults?

The i9 controllers communicate over one or more Controller Area Network buses. A CAN fault appears as modules that report "no communication" on the diagnostic scan, intermittent warning lamps or features that work only after a restart. The physical causes are usually mundane: a damaged twisted pair, a connector with a poor ground reference, or a module that pulls the bus down because its power supply is unstable. Begin with the wiring at the suspected segment and check that the bus termination resistance is in the expected range, commonly around 60 ohms across the two bus lines with the system powered down.

Use the diagnostic tool to log which module first reports the loss of communication, because that module is often the source of the problem rather than a victim. Record the fault at the moment it occurs rather than reading stored codes days later; intermittent CAN errors are frequently tied to vibration or temperature and will not reproduce in a static workshop. On a 6x4 truck working a refuse or port cycle, a connector that opens on a particular body movement is a classic source of an elusive bus fault.

How does insulation monitoring protect the vehicle?

An insulation monitoring device continuously measures the resistance between the high-voltage conductors and the vehicle chassis. A healthy system shows a high resistance, typically in the range of hundreds of kilo-ohms or more, and the monitor will warn or shut down if that resistance falls toward a defined threshold. Moisture in a connector, a chafed cable against a frame rail or a cracked inverter housing can all reduce insulation resistance, and the monitor is often the first component to notice.

Treat an insulation warning as a high-voltage fault requiring investigation, never as a message to clear and forget. Follow the diagnostic procedure to isolate sections of the high-voltage system until the faulty segment is found, and always confirm that the bus is de-energised before handling any connector. A truck with a low insulation reading should be kept out of service until the cause is repaired, because the fault can present a shock hazard to anyone touching the chassis.

What does thermal management of the battery pack involve?

The LFP battery pack in the i9 is liquid-cooled, and its temperature range governs both fast charging and sustained output. The thermal management system circulates coolant through the pack, monitors cell temperatures at multiple points and can limit power or charging current when the pack is too cold or too hot. A workshop cannot service the sealed pack itself, but it can check the supporting system: coolant level and condition, pump operation, the condition of hoses and quick couplings, and any recorded temperature-related derate codes from the diagnostic tool.

A pack that charges slowly in cold weather may simply be protecting itself, and a fleet that plans rapid turnaround should consider preconditioning the pack while it is still connected to the charger. In hot climates, a clogged radiator or a failing coolside fan can push the pack toward its upper limit during a long grade, so keep the cooling stack clean as part of routine service.

How do you isolate the vehicle safely before work?

Safe isolation is the first step, not a final check. Park on level ground, apply the parking brake, switch off the vehicle and remove the key or authorization card. Open the battery service disconnect and wait the period specified by the manufacturer for the high-voltage bus capacitors to discharge, often several minutes. Verify a zero-voltage reading with a rated meter on a known test point before touching any high-voltage component, and confirm that the main contactors have opened. Low-voltage work on the 24V harness also benefits from disconnecting the auxiliary battery negative terminal to avoid accidental wake-up of the vehicle during the job.

Key point: Isolate, wait for the specified discharge time and verify zero voltage at the high-voltage test point with a rated meter before any inspection. On the i9 the 24V harness can wake controllers, so disconnect the auxiliary battery for cab wiring work as well.

Fleets comparing electrical architectures across the electric range can review the SAGMOTO new energy electric trucks page for the wider context of battery-electric platforms and charging behaviour. The discipline of isolation and verification applies to every one of them.

Cab electrical service on the i9 rewards a methodical approach. Read the low-voltage bus at the source, respect the interlock loop, interpret CAN faults from the wiring outward, and treat insulation and thermal warnings as real safety signals. With no aftertreatment or engine clutch to maintain, those electrical checks are where electric-truck reliability is won or lost.

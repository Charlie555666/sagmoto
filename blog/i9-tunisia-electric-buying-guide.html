Importing SAGMOTO i9 Electric Trucks into Tunisia: Duties, Charging and Fleet Sizing

2026-10-19Buying Guide11 min read

Tunisia is a natural early market for electric distribution trucks

Tunisia's geography makes it an unusually good fit for electric trucks. The country is compact, its population and industry are concentrated along a narrow Mediterranean coastal strip, and its distribution distances are short enough that a single overnight charge covers a full shift. At the same time, Tunisia is closely tied to European supply chains and standards, which means its charging infrastructure and its regulatory expectations already lean toward European norms. For urban delivery, industrial logistics and municipal service fleets operating from Tunis, Sfax, Sousse and the surrounding governorates, the SAGMOTO i9 is a credible, cost-effective electric platform. This guide walks Tunisian buyers through duty and VAT, Mediterranean port clearance, the European-standard charging the i9 uses, fleet-sizing methodology and battery warranty and aftersales.

The broader SAGMOTO new energy electric trucks range, which includes the lighter i5, covers complementary duty profiles, but the i9 is the right base for Tunisian metropolitan and industrial distribution because of its 131 kWh LFP battery and its rigid chassis format that accepts box, reefer, stake and refuse bodies.

Step one: verify the duty cycle fits the i9

The i9 is an urban and metropolitan distribution truck. The decisive suitability test is the daily kilometre profile: if routes run inside 200 to 220 kilometres per shift with depot dwell overnight, the i9 fits with comfortable margin. Multi-drop city work with regenerative braking recovers 15 to 25 percent of energy, effectively extending range on precisely the routes where electric trucks work best. Fleets with longer inter-city legs between Tunis and the south should plan midday opportunity charging at a DC point or split the duty between the i9 and diesel platforms.

Tunisian summer heat reaches 40 to 45 degrees on the coast and higher inland, which is the specification driver. The i9's LFP chemistry is the right foundation, because its thermal stability at high temperature is superior to NMC alternatives and its cycle life of 3,000-plus full cycles to 80 percent capacity is preserved far better in hot climates. Tunisian buyers should specify the battery liquid-cooling package, the high-capacity cab air conditioning with insulated cab shell, solar-reflective glazing, and dust-sealed electronics for the sahara wind and coastal salt.

Key point: Tunisian electricity tariffs make the fuel arithmetic decisive. At typical industrial tariffs, the i9's energy cost per 100 km runs USD 2 to 4, against USD 14 to 18 for a comparable diesel distribution truck at local pump prices. A 200-km-per-day, 300-day-year i9 saves USD 7,500 to 10,500 annually in energy alone.

Step two: import duty, VAT and conformity

Tunisia applies import duty and VAT to imported vehicles, with rates that vary by category and by whether the vehicle is classified as commercial or electric. Electric commercial vehicles may benefit from favourable or reduced treatment under Tunisia's clean-transport policy, but the exact rate should be confirmed with a licensed Tunisian customs broker before the commercial terms are signed. As a planning envelope, a new electric distribution truck in the i9 class typically faces a combined duty-and-VAT burden in the region of 18 to 30 percent of CIF value, with the VAT charged on the duty-inclusive base.

The i9 ships with a manufacturer's documentation package that supports Tunisian conformity: certificate of conformity, battery dangerous-goods shipping certification, and high-voltage system documentation. Because Tunisia aligns closely with European type-approval expectations, the i9's European-compatible charging and safety architecture reduces the conformity friction relative to vehicles built only to other regional standards. Engage a Tunis-experienced clearing agent for the first shipment and budget three to six weeks for conformity processing on the first order, faster for repeat orders on file.

Step three: Mediterranean port clearance

The practical discharge ports are La Goulette, the main port serving Tunis, and Bizerte, the northern deep-water port. Both are Mediterranean roll-on/roll-off and breakbulk gateways with established automotive handling. A completed i9 rigid with a box body is out of gauge by height, so it travels RoRo or on a flat rack rather than loose in a container, and the body is normally fitted in China to reduce handling at the Tunisian port.

The clearance sequence is: vessel arrival and discharge, presentation of the conformity file and the customs declaration, duty and VAT settlement, port handling and agency fees, and finally road transport to the operator's depot in Tunis, Sfax or the relevant governorate. Document errors are the main cause of port delay, and Tunisian storage and demurrage run USD 40 to USD 90 per day per unit, so a delayed document set is a direct cost.

Key point: For Tunisia, CIF covers the sea leg to La Goulette or Bizerte only. Conformity processing and the VAT settlement are separate steps you must plan for, and they typically take three to six weeks on a first order.

Step four: charging to European standards

Tunisia's public and depot charging infrastructure follows European standards, which is exactly what the i9 is built for. The i9 uses a Type 2 connector for AC charging at 20 to 40 kW overnight and a CCS2 connector for DC fast charging, the same standards used across the European Union. That compatibility means a Tunisian operator can install a standard European AC wallbox or DC charger without proprietary adapters, and it future-proofs the fleet against the expanding European-aligned public charging network.

In every Tunisian i9 deployment, the truck arrives before the grid connection is comfortable, so charging infrastructure is the critical path. A depot of ten i9 trucks requires an AC charging point per truck and a grid connection in the 300 to 400 kVA class, which in Tunisia means an application to the utility, civil works and transformer capacity approval with timelines of two to six months depending on the industrial zone. The technical package includes the charge-point specification, the daily energy model, and the load-management configuration that staggers overnight charging across the fleet to flatten peak demand. The DC fast-charge option matters for high-utilisation operations: a 45 to 60 minute midday charge can support a double-shift pattern on longer routes.

Step five: fleet sizing methodology

Fleet sizing for electric trucks is a duty-cycle exercise, not a one-for-one replacement of diesel units. The method is to take the daily kilometre requirement per route, add a 15 to 25 percent reserve for heat and payload derate, and divide the i9's usable daily range by that figure to get the number of vehicles needed for continuous cover. A route running 180 km per day with a 20 percent reserve needs roughly 216 km of available range, which the i9 covers comfortably on a single overnight charge, so one i9 replaces one diesel unit on that route. A route running 300 km per day needs either a DC midday charge or a second vehicle in rotation.

The financial case rests on energy and maintenance savings. Against a diesel 16-tonner, the i9 carries a purchase premium that Tunisian energy and maintenance arithmetic recovers in three to five years on high-utilisation duty, after which the annual operating advantage accrues directly. Total-cost modelling should include the truck, charging infrastructure amortised across the fleet, driver training on regenerative braking technique, and a service contract for high-voltage system checks. Battery residual value at end of service, because LFP packs retain second-life value in stationary storage, is an emerging positive factor.

Step six: battery warranty and aftersales

The i9 is backed by a vehicle warranty of 12 to 24 months and a battery warranty of 8 years or 70 percent retained capacity, whichever comes first. That battery warranty is the item Tunisian fleet managers should read most carefully, because it defines the economic floor of the asset. Fenghan coordinates driver training and provides technical documentation for the fleet's maintenance team; routine chassis and brake work is conventional, with high-voltage system service following documented procedures by trained technicians.

Aftersales readiness means holding a small critical-spare set and confirming a trained high-voltage technician before delivery. The i9's European-compatible diagnostics reduce the learning curve, and the chassis shares conventional braking, steering and suspension components with other SAGMOTO platforms. A fleet that trains drivers on regenerative braking, installs disciplined overnight depot charging, and keeps the high-voltage service schedule will reach the full battery warranty window with capacity intact.

Conclusion

The i9 fits Tunisia's electrification moment precisely: an LFP-battery distribution truck with the thermal resilience for 40-plus-degree summers, the range for metropolitan duty, the European-standard Type 2 and CCS2 charging architecture that the Tunisian network already understands, and an acquisition cost that makes fleet-scale electrification financially rational. The procurement disciplines are clear: verify the duty cycle, confirm duty and VAT with a Tunisian broker, sequence charging infrastructure first, specify the thermal management package, and invest in driver training.

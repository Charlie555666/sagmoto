SAGMOTO E9 for Last-Mile Parcels: Route Economics per Stop

2026-11-18Industry Application9 min read

Last-mile parcel fleets are usually managed on a cost-per-kilometre basis because that is the number the fuel card produces. It is the wrong number. A parcel route is not paid per kilometre; it is paid per parcel delivered, and the variables that decide profitability are stops per hour, parcels per stop, load factor and the number of trips needed to clear the day's volume. A truck that costs more per kilometre but carries 30 percent more parcels per trip can be the cheaper vehicle by a wide margin.

The SAGMOTO E9 is the light and medium 4x2 platform in the range, suited to urban distribution with a body volume that accommodates parcel cages and a cab and chassis sized for city access. This article sets out how to cost a parcel route properly, where the E9's characteristics help, and which route profiles make it the wrong choice.

Key point: Cost per parcel, not cost per kilometre, decides a last-mile fleet. The dominant variables are stops per hour, parcels per stop and load factor. A truck with 20 percent more body volume can cut a second daily trip and beat a cheaper vehicle on cost per parcel every time.

Building a cost per parcel model

The model has four layers. The first is the route: how many stops, how far apart, how many parcels per stop, and how long each stop takes. The second is the vehicle: how many parcels it can hold, how quickly it can be loaded and unloaded, and how easily it accesses kerbside and basement loading bays. The third is the cost base: driver time, fuel or energy, maintenance, depreciation and insurance. The fourth is the load factor, which multiplies everything.

Worked example: a route with 90 stops, averaging 2.6 parcels per stop, is 234 parcels. At 14 stops per hour including driving time, the route takes 6.4 hours plus loading. A vehicle that holds 150 parcels needs two trips, or one trip and a reload, which adds 40 minutes. A vehicle that holds 240 parcels does it in one. The difference is 40 minutes of driver time and a second loading cycle, which on a fully loaded cost base is a substantial share of the route's margin.

Where the E9 fits the route

The E9's contribution is body volume and access. A 20 to 25 cubic metre body on a 4x2 chassis at 7.5 to 12 tonnes gross carries enough parcel cages for a dense urban route without a second trip, while remaining narrow enough and short enough to work in city traffic and enter basement loading areas with height restrictions. That combination is the practical definition of a last-mile vehicle.

Access matters more than payload in parcel work. Parcels are light and bulky, so the constraint is almost always volume, not weight. A vehicle that cubes out before it weighs out is correctly specified for parcels, and one that weighs out before it cubes out is a general distribution truck being used inefficiently. Fleets that buy on payload capacity alone often end up with a truck that is 40 percent under-loaded by volume on every route.

Key point: Parcels cube out before they weigh out. Specify the E9 on body volume and access, not on payload, or the truck will run at 60 percent volume utilisation while its weight capacity sits unused.

Fuel and energy per parcel

On a stop-start urban route the E9's consumption is driven by idling, acceleration and the number of stops rather than by distance. A route with 90 stops and 45 km of driving consumes more fuel per kilometre than a highway run, because the engine is doing acceleration work repeatedly and idling at each stop. Typical figures for a loaded light truck in this duty are 14 to 19 litres per 100 km, with the higher end in dense traffic with frequent stops.

Expressed per parcel, that is 2.7 to 3.7 litres per 100 parcels on a 45 km route with 234 parcels, or roughly USD 0.024 to USD 0.045 of fuel per parcel at USD 1.00 per litre. That number is small in absolute terms but it is highly sensitive to route density: the same 234 parcels spread over 80 km instead of 45 km raise fuel per parcel by nearly 80 percent. Route density, not vehicle efficiency, is the biggest lever in last-mile fuel cost.

Driver time dominates everything

In a parcel operation, driver time is 55 to 70 percent of the total route cost. That single fact should shape every vehicle decision. Anything that saves the driver a minute per stop is worth more than a fuel saving of several percent. A body with a low load floor saves lifting time; a side door or a walk-in access saves climbing in and out; good lighting in the cargo area saves searching; a layout that keeps the next parcels accessible saves sorting time at every stop.

The E9's body specification should therefore be chosen with the driver in mind: load floor height, shelving or cage configuration, lighting, and a door arrangement that allows access from the kerb side. These are not comfort features; they are the difference between 12 and 16 stops per hour, which on a 90-stop route is over an hour of driver time every day.

Maintenance and downtime in urban duty

Urban parcel trucks accumulate hours more than kilometres: idling in traffic, frequent braking, constant door operation and repeated loading cycles. Maintenance planning should follow engine hours and brake condition rather than distance. Brake wear in particular is heavy on a stop-start route, and a truck that reaches 40,000 km in a year may have done the brake cycles of a highway truck at 120,000 km.

Downtime is especially costly in parcel work because the route must be covered daily. A truck off the road for two days means either a rented replacement or a doubled route, and both are expensive. Fleets should hold one spare vehicle per 15 to 20 routes, and should schedule maintenance in the low-volume window rather than at the book interval. That single planning decision prevents most of the expensive emergency rentals.

Key point: In parcel work, one day of unplanned downtime costs a rental or a doubled route. Plan maintenance into the low-volume window and keep one spare per 15 to 20 routes rather than paying for emergency cover.

When the E9 is the wrong choice

The E9 is wrong for routes that are weight-limited rather than volume-limited — building materials, beverages, gas cylinders — where payload dominates and body volume is irrelevant. It is also wrong for intercity or regional parcel legs where distance and speed matter more than access, and for routes with long stretches of highway where a heavier, faster vehicle is more efficient per parcel.

The honest test is the load profile. If the average parcel weighs 2 kg and the route cubes out at 60 percent of weight capacity, the E9 is correctly specified. If the average unit weighs 40 kg and the truck weighs out at 70 percent of volume, a different platform is needed. Measure the load profile for two weeks before choosing, because assumptions about parcel density are wrong more often than they are right. Sizing the body around the parcel cage

Parcel cages, roll containers and the trolleys that move them are the real unit of load in a last-mile operation, and the body should be designed around them rather than around cubic metres in the abstract. A standard roll container is roughly 0.8 by 0.7 metres at the base and 1.7 metres high, so a body needs an internal height above 1.9 metres, a floor strong enough for a loaded container at 500 kg, and a securing arrangement that stops containers moving in transit.

Internal width matters as much as volume. A body that is 2.4 metres wide internally fits three containers across with a centre aisle; one that is 2.2 metres fits two comfortably and a third badly. Over a route that difference is several containers of capacity, which is the difference between one trip and two. Specify the body from the container dimensions and the route's parcel count, not from a nominal volume figure.

Key point: Design the body around roll containers, not cubic metres. Internal height above 1.9 m, a floor rated for 500 kg containers and an internal width that fits three containers across are the numbers that decide how many parcels the route clears per trip.

Charging, refuelling and the daily cycle

Parcel vehicles return to the depot at least once a day, which is an advantage for any alternative fuel. A truck that comes back to the same yard every evening can be refuelled or recharged on a fixed schedule, with no range anxiety and no need for public infrastructure. That is why last-mile distribution has been the earliest adopter of electric light trucks, and why depot charging design matters more than the vehicle's range figure.

The design question is how many vehicles must charge simultaneously in the window between the last route and the first dispatch. For a depot with 30 routes and an eight-hour window, sequenced charging at moderate power usually suffices, because each vehicle needs only enough energy for a 60 to 90 km urban cycle. Sizing for simultaneous high-power charging in that scenario is money spent on capacity that the schedule never needs.

Conclusion

Last-mile parcel economics are decided by stops per hour, parcels per stop and load factor, not by cost per kilometre. The E9 fits routes that cube out before they weigh out, where body volume and kerbside access determine whether the day's volume clears in one trip or two, and where driver time is the dominant cost. Specify the body around the driver, plan maintenance around the volume curve, keep a spare vehicle per 15 to 20 routes, and measure the load profile before buying. Do that and the vehicle decision becomes an arithmetic answer rather than a preference.

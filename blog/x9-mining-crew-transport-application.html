SAGMOTO X9 4x4 Mine-Site Crew Transport Vehicle Configuration Guide

2026-11-04Industry Application9 min read

Why the X9 fits mine-site crew movement

The SAGMOTO X9 light 4x4 chassis produces 160 horsepower from the Yuchai YC4E engine and supports a gross combination weight from 4.5 to 25 tonnes depending on the body and trailer combination, which makes it a practical people-mover for the short, rough, repeated trips that define a working pit. Mine camps rarely need highway speed; they need a vehicle that starts in dust, climbs a graded haul road fully seated, and returns for the next load within the 20 minute gap between shift changeover. The X9 all-wheel drive with a manually selected front axle gives that grip without the mass and fuel draw of a heavier rigid bus.

At a typical site the camp sits 8 to 25 km from the pit gate, and a crew carrier runs that leg perhaps 6 to 10 times per shift as teams rotate through crib breaks and equipment changeovers. The X9 4,000 mm to 4,500 mm wheelbase keeps the turning circle under 16 metres so it can U-turn on a wide haul-road berm without backing into traffic, and the 230 mm ground clearance clears the loose rock that would snag a low-entry bus.

The 160 hp Yuchai YC4E is a four-cylinder unit with a flat torque shelf that suits the stop-start rhythm of camp shuttles better than a higher-output six that spends its life below half throttle. Fuel burn on the 18 km camp leg runs about 14 to 16 litres per 100 km with a seated load, and the small sump keeps service cost low across the high cycle count a mine roster demands.

Order the X9 with the upgraded 240 ampere alternator and a second battery when the body carries 12 or more seats, because heated visors, cabin fans and the radio draw a continuous 40 to 60 amps that a standard electrical system cannot sustain across a 12 hour shift.

Personnel-carrier body and seating layout

The mine-body X9 uses forward-facing seats with three-point belts rather than a bench, because individual seats let each miner store a tool bag at their feet without sliding into a neighbour on a side slope. A 14-seat layout places two rows of three and two rows of four with an aisle wide enough for a worker in knee pads to pass, while a 20-seat layout adds a rear transverse bench that trades aisle width for headcount. The body side walls are 50 mm composite panel over a steel frame so they shrug off a thrown helmet better than thin aluminium.

Roll-over protection is built into the body structure rather than added later, with a perimeter hoop tied to the chassis rails at six points and a roof rated to carry the loaded vehicle weight in a simulated tip. Seats mount to the floor with through-bolts and backing plates, not self-tappers, so a sudden stop cannot rip a seat from the deck. The front passenger and driver seats get air suspension to cut fatigue on the washboard section of the camp road.

Seating capacity and gross vehicle weight trade off directly: each added seat with its occupant is roughly 95 kg, so moving from 14 to 20 seats consumes about 570 kg of the available payload and forces a lighter tool stowage allowance. Sites that need both high headcount and heavy kit usually run two X9 bodies rather than overload one, keeping each within its 25 tonne combination limit.

Headroom inside the body is set at 1,750 mm so a miner in a hard hat and carrying a tool bag stands to step out without ducking, and the aisle floor is a sealed aluminium tread plate that hoses clean of drill mud at the end of a shift. Overhead grab handles run the full length on both sides because the vehicle rocks on the graded road and riders need a fixed point when it lists.

4x4 traction on haul roads

Graded haul roads look flat but hide a crown, a loose shoulder and the occasional tyre-grit slick that drops traction without warning. The X9 engages front drive through a dash switch and holds a 35 to 45 percent grade when the tray is evenly loaded, which covers every camp-to-pit climb on a typical iron ore or coal site. Differential locks front and rear are optional and worth fitting where the road climbs through a cut that holds shade and moisture.

Braking matters as much as drive on the descent, and the X9 carries dual-circuit air brakes with an exhaust brake on the engine to hold speed without cooking the foundation brakes on a long pit ramp. The driver selects low range for the steep inner cut and high range for the camp bitumen, and the transfer case engages without a full stop once rolling below 8 km/h.

Traction also depends on tyres, and a 255/100R16 all-terrain at 3.5 bar front and 4.0 bar rear gives the footprint that keeps the X9 from wandering on a crowned road at the 40 km/h site limit. Mining tyres carry a higher ply rating than highway rubber so they survive the stray rebar and sharp shot rock that a camp road collects, and the spare should be a matched wheel so a change does not alter the drive balance.

Dust ingress and air filtration

A pit generates a fine talc-like dust that finds every seal, and the X9 cabin must stay a clean breathing space for a 12 hour occupant. The standard paper-element cabin filter is upgraded to a dual-stage cyclonic pre-cleaner that spins out the heavy grit before it reaches the paper, extending filter life from about 250 hours to 600 hours in heavy dust. The body door seals are continuous neoprene rather than spot blocks so the cabin holds positive pressure from the fresh-air fan and keeps dust from seeping in around the frames.

The engine breathes through a raised snorkel intake that sits above the cabin roofline, drawing air from the cleaner layer above the dust cloud that hangs at waist height on the haul road. A restricted-intake tell-tale on the dash warns the driver before power drops, because a choked filter can cost 8 to 12 horsepower that the X9 needs on the climb out of the pit.

Cabin pressurisation is set at a slight positive value driven by the fresh-air fan so dust is pushed out through the seals rather than drawn in, and the door thresholds carry a brush sweep that knocks grit off boots before it reaches the cabin floor. A monthly pressure-test of the sealed box confirms the fans still hold the set point after a season of vibration.

Wash the cyclonic pre-cleaner bowl every 100 operating hours even when the gauge looks clear, because a packed bowl silently shifts the dust load onto the paper element and halves its remaining life.

Seat belt and anchorage inspection regime

Belts on a mine crew carrier are life-saving equipment, not trim, and the site should inspect each three-point assembly every 250 hours of service for frayed webbing, a sticky reel and a buckle that clicks home with a positive snap. Anchorage points at the floor and the B-post are checked with a torque wrench to the 45 Nm spec, because a loose anchorage turns a belt into decoration during a roll.

The inspection record travels with the vehicle so any auditor can see the last check date, and a belt that fails the reel-drop test is replaced as a pair on that seat rather than singly, since a new and old reel retract at different rates and confuse the wearer. Drivers do a visual walk-around of belts at the start of each shift, and a red tag on a faulty seat removes it from service until the part arrives at the remote camp store.

The roll-over protection structure is proof-loaded to 1.5 times the body weight on its side, and the certification plate is fixed to the B-post where an auditor can read it without tools. A site that runs the X9 on a haul road shared with heavy mining trucks treats the ROPS as mandatory rather than optional, because a side impact from a loaded tray is the dominant crew hazard on a shared corridor.

Shift-change duty and camp logistics

The X9 crew carrier runs two 12 hour shifts, and the duty table below maps a representative day where the vehicle bridges camp and pit across changeover peaks at 06:00, 18:00 and the mid-shift crib break. Hot-seat logistics mean the incoming and outgoing crews meet at the gate, so the carrier does a fast turn with doors open and belts checked in seconds rather than minutes. Fly-in-fly-out camps add a weekly airport run of 60 to 120 km that the same X9 covers with the seats emptied of tool kits to carry duffels instead.

Remote camp maintenance leans on a pre-packed service kit because a parts delay strands the whole shift rotation, and the X9 shares filters and belts with other Yuchai YC4E site equipment to shrink that kit. A daily 15 minute check covers tyre cuts, brake wear, coolant and the pre-cleaner bowl, logged on a board at the camp workshop so the next driver inherits a known-good vehicle.

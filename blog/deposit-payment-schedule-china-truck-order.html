What Deposit and Payment Schedule Is Normal When Ordering Trucks from China?

2026-10-22Buyer Decision Guide12 min read

Quick answer: A standard SAGMOTO order uses a 30% deposit to start production, 30% at pre-shipment inspection, and 40% against the Bill of Lading copy within five business days. Custom trucks need 40–50% deposit because bespoke parts cannot be resold.

How a SAGMOTO Order Payment Flow Works

When you buy trucks from a Chinese manufacturer or trading company, payment is almost never a single 100% wire. Instead it is staged against production milestones so that neither side carries the full risk alone. The factory commits capacity and components when the deposit arrives; the buyer retains leverage because the largest tranche is released only after the truck is built, inspected and loaded. For fleets sourcing the SAGMOTO tractor trucks prime mover range or the SAGMOTO cargo truck flatbed box stake models, the same milestone logic applies whether the order is two units or twenty.

Understanding this flow before you sign the proforma invoice prevents the two classic mistakes: paying too much upfront to an unverified supplier, or refusing any deposit and losing the production slot to another buyer. The sections below break the schedule into the milestones that matter, the instruments that move the money, and the contract clauses that keep you protected.

What Deposit and Milestone Split Is Normal for a SAGMOTO Order

The most common structure for a standard production truck is a 30 / 30 / 40 split. The first 30% is the advance payment made with the signed proforma invoice; it books your chassis number and triggers parts procurement. The second 30% is due at pre-shipment, after the truck has passed factory QC and a loading slot is confirmed. The final 40% is paid against a copy of the Bill of Lading within five business days of sailing, which is when the original documents are still with the carrier and the buyer retains clear leverage. Some buyers negotiate a simpler 30% deposit plus 70% against B/L copy; this shifts more risk to the seller and is often available once a relationship is established.

How Do T/T and Letter of Credit Compare for Truck Imports

Two instruments dominate China truck payments: telegraphic transfer (T/T) and a letter of credit (LC). T/T is a direct bank wire and costs almost nothing — typically $20–$50 per transfer in bank fees — but it relies on trust because once the money leaves your account the supplier simply has it. An LC is a bank promise to pay the seller only against conforming documents, which protects a first-time buyer, but it is expensive: opening and amendment fees plus a 1.5–2% per-year charge on the value, easily $1,500–$4,000 on a single tractor order. For established repeat buyers, T/T against milestones is the norm; for a large first order from an untested supplier, an LC at sight is worth the fee.

Choosing the Right Instrument by Order Size

As a rule of thumb, use T/T for orders under $100,000 where the deposit exposure is manageable, and consider an LC for orders above $150,000 or whenever the supplier is new and unverified. A hybrid is also common: T/T deposit plus an LC for the balance, blending low cost with document-controlled security. The key is to match the instrument to the relationship stage, not to the truck model.

Should Custom-Built Trucks Require a Higher Deposit

Yes, and for a sound reason. A standard white-paint 6×4 tractor can be redirected to another customer if a buyer defaults, but a truck built to a unique spec — say a 560 HP E1st flagship configuration, a mining 4×4, or a left-hand-drive variant for a rare market — carries components that the factory cannot easily resell. To cover that commitment, custom orders normally require a 40–50% deposit, sometimes with a non-refundable engineering fee for tooling or certification. Buyers should view the higher deposit not as a red flag but as the fair price of exclusivity; the trade-off is a truck matched exactly to the duty cycle rather than a stock unit.

What Contract Controls Protect the Buyer

A payment schedule is only as safe as the contract around it. Beyond the milestone percentages, the proforma or sales contract should name the exact specification, the incoterm (FOB or CIF), the production lead time with a late-delivery penalty, and the inspection rights. Buyers should insist on a pre-shipment inspection — either their own technician or a third party such as SGS — and a loading video showing the VIN plate, the container or vessel number, and the securement. A written clause that the final payment is conditional on passing this inspection is the single most effective protection, because it converts the last 30–40% from a courtesy into a contractual checkpoint. Keep all wires to a named company account, never a personal account, and reconcile every invoice against the commercial contract.

Do Payment Milestones Change With Order Value

The percentages stay broadly stable across order sizes, but the instrumentation and oversight scale with value. A two-truck order runs comfortably on a 30/30/40 T/T plan with a factory inspection report. A twenty-truck order, while using the same split, should add a third-party survey at roughly $300–$600 per inspection batch and possibly an LC for the balance, because the absolute exposure is large even at a familiar percentage. Very large tenders may move to a 20/30/30/20 four-stage plan that releases a small tranche only after arrival and PDI, further protecting the buyer. The principle is constant: keep the largest payment tied to a document or a verified event, and never let the deposit exceed what the supplier genuinely needs to start.

Red Flags in Any Payment Request

Certain requests should stop an order regardless of how attractive the truck price looks. Be wary of a supplier demanding 100% upfront, or asking the deposit to be sent to a personal account rather than a registered company account, since that removes nearly all recourse. Equally suspicious is a refusal to provide a proforma invoice with a fixed specification, or pressure to skip the pre-shipment inspection to save a few days. A seller who will not name the Incoterm or who shifts the price after deposit confirmation is showing the exact behavior that leads to disputes. Legitimate manufacturers and traders such as Shaanxi Fenghan Trading issue a clear PI, accept staged T/T, and welcome a third-party survey; if those basics are missing, pause and verify the company before any wire leaves your account. Protecting the deposit is protecting the entire project, and a few hours of diligence up front prevents the far larger cost of a stalled or defaulted order. Sound payment discipline is what separates a profitable import programme from a one-time gamble.

What Happens If You Miss a Payment Milestone?

Missing a milestone is rarely fatal, but it always costs time. A production slot held open without the progress payment will usually be released to another order within seven to fourteen days, and re-booking pushes delivery back by a full production cycle rather than by the number of days you were late. If you already know a payment will slip, tell the supplier before the due date: a short written extension request with a firm new date is far easier to approve than a silent delay discovered after the slot is gone.

Where the delay is on the supplier's side — a certificate missing, a body builder running late, a vessel rolled — the response should be contractual rather than conversational. Reference the delivery date in the proforma invoice or contract, ask for a revised schedule in writing, and hold the final payment against evidence of loading. Reasonable suppliers expect this; it is standard practice on export orders and does not damage the relationship.

Protect yourself with two clauses: a defined delivery date with a stated remedy, and a final payment triggered by the bill of lading date rather than by a calendar guess. Those two lines prevent most payment disputes before they start.

Frequently Asked Questions

What deposit percentage is normal for a standard SAGMOTO truck order?

Standard production orders typically require a 30% deposit with the proforma invoice to secure the build slot, with the remaining 70% paid as 30% before shipment and 40% against the Bill of Lading copy within five business days.

Why do custom-built trucks need a higher deposit?

Custom trucks such as a 560HP E1st spec or a mining 4x4 need a 40–50% deposit because the bespoke parts cannot be resold, so the factory covers component commitment risk before production starts.

Is T/T or a letter of credit safer for the buyer?

A letter of credit is safer for the buyer on large first orders because payment is released only against conforming documents, but it costs 1.5–2% of value in bank fees versus about $20–$50 per T/T wire.

When should the final payment be released?

The final 30–40% should be released only against the Bill of Lading copy after loading and a passed pre-shipment inspection, never before the truck is physically on the vessel.

How can a buyer avoid payment risk with a new Chinese supplier?

Use a 30% deposit, a pre-shipment inspection with a loading video, and the balance against B/L copy, plus consider a third-party survey such as SGS at roughly $300–$600 to verify the unit before the final wire.

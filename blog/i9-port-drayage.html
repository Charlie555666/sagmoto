i9 Electric Trucks for Port Drayage: The Container Terminal Shuttle Economics

📅 2026-11-06 ✍️ Shaanxi Fenghan Trading Co., Ltd. 📖 11 min read
Direct answer: The SAGMOTO i9 electric truck is a strong fit for port drayage — the short, repetitive shuttle of containers between a terminal and an inland depot. Because the route is fixed and the daily range (typically 150–400 km with margin) is predictable, a battery-electric i9 with depot charging beats diesel on energy cost per km and maintenance, while zero tailpipe emissions help meet port low- and zero-emission zones. The binding constraint is GVWR and axle rating for the loaded container, not the battery.

Port drayage is the least glamorous and most predictable trucking job in the world — and that predictability is exactly why it is the first place electrification pays. A drayage tractor repeats the same short loop all day: collect a box at the terminal gate, haul it a few kilometres to an inland container depot (ICD), drop, collect an empty or export box, return. No long highway unknown, no variable payload surprises, no remote breakdown risk. For a terminal operator or a dedicated drayage fleet, that fixed duty cycle is the ideal canvas for a battery-electric truck.

The SAGMOTO i9 electric platform is built for exactly this mission. This guide models the i9's drayage economics honestly — including the parts where diesel still wins.

Why Drayage Is the Perfect Electric Duty Cycle

Electrification struggles where routes are long, variable and remote. Drayage is the opposite on every axis:

  • Short loop: 10–40 km round trip per cycle, so daily energy needs are bounded.
  • High cycle count: 10–20 shuttles per shift means the truck is working constantly — exactly when electrification's low per-km cost compounds.
  • Regenerative braking: the loaded-return and terminal-slowdown legs recover energy, padding real range.
  • Fixed base: the depot is known, so charging infrastructure can be planned, not improvised.
  • Emissions rules: major ports are rolling out low-emission (LEZ) and zero-emission (ZEZ) zones that penalise or exclude diesel drayage.
Fit rule: electrify the loop that never changes. If your drayage route, payload and shift pattern are stable, the i9's TCO case is strong; if every day is a different 300 km unknown, wait for bigger batteries.

Because a loaded container is heavy, the chassis GVWR and axle ratings — not battery size — usually set the practical payload. Spec the i9's axle and frame for the loaded box, then size the battery to the shift's energy need with a comfortable reserve for air-conditioning and queueing at the gate.

On a pure per-km basis the i9 usually wins decisively: electricity is a fraction of diesel cost, and maintenance is reduced by the absence of oil changes, exhaust and complex transmission. Those savings are largest precisely on the high-cycle, low-speed drayage profile. The offset is the higher purchase price and the need for depot charging — but because the route is fixed, the charging plan is a one-time engineering problem, not a daily uncertainty.

Charging Strategy for a Terminal Operator

  1. Opportunity top-up: a DC charger at the depot tops the i9 during driver breaks and load waits, extending effective daily range.
  2. Overnight full charge: a slower AC/DC session between shifts returns the battery to 100% before the next day.
  3. State-of-charge dispataching: telematics tell the dispatcher which truck has margin for a long shunt and which should take the short loop.
  4. Reserve planning: keep a diesel or a second electric as swing capacity during peak or fault.

Where Diesel Still Wins (Honesty Check)

The i9 is not universal. Diesel remains preferable when:

  • The drayage extends to long, variable regional hauls beyond battery range.
  • Depot charging cannot be permitted or financed in the near term.
  • Payload swings are extreme and the chassis is at GVWR with a heavy box plus battery weight.

For the classic terminal-to-ICD shuttle, however, the i9's case is strong and getting stronger as ports tighten emission zones.

Total Cost of Ownership Over the Asset Life

A drayage operator should model the i9 over its full working life, not just the purchase invoice. The shape of the TCO is unusual for an electric truck:

  • Year 1–2: the higher upfront cost and charger capex sit on the books, while energy and maintenance savings begin accruing from the first shift.
  • Year 3–5: savings compound. With no diesel price exposure on the drayage loop, the operator is insulated from fuel volatility that hits diesel rivals every quarter.
  • Year 5+: battery health decides residual value. Specifying the right battery size (not the largest) and managing charge cycles protects capacity and therefore resale.

For a terminal operator running a fixed, high-cycle loop, the crossover point where cumulative savings overtake the higher purchase price typically arrives well within the asset's life — often inside three years at current diesel and electricity ratios. The decisive risks are not technical but organisational: securing depot power, training shunt drivers on electric torque delivery, and planning around state-of-charge.

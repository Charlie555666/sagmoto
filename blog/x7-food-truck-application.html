X7 Food Truck Build: Mobile Catering Body on a SAGMOTO X7 Chassis-Cab

2026-11-13Industry Application8 min read

A mobile food truck lives or dies by the chassis underneath the stainless steel counters, and for operators serving dense city districts the SAGMOTO X7 chassis-cab has become a practical platform because its 160 to 190 horsepower Yuchai YC4E or YC6J engine pairs a low curb weight with the wheelbase and frame width that commercial body builders already expect. The X7 sits in the light to medium municipal class, which means it is narrow enough for old-town streets, light enough to keep payload available for cooking equipment, and simple enough for a local fabricator to upfit without proprietary tooling. This article works through the duty cycle, the electrical and water budgets, the body weight versus payload math, and the hygiene rules that determine whether a finished unit can actually trade in the export cities you are targeting.

Why the X7 chassis-cab fits mobile catering duty

Food truck work is a stop-start urban cycle: short hops between pitches, long idling while the generator or engine runs the kitchen, and frequent door cycles that load the air conditioning and extraction fans. The X7 suited to this duty because the Yuchai YC4E and YC6J engines are tuned for low-end torque rather than highway speed, so the truck pulls away cleanly from a curb with a fully fitted body and does not need to be revved hard in traffic. Operators comparing a purpose-built chassis against a converted van will find the X7 carries a heavier body allowance and a flat, straight frame rail that simplifies the box structure. Buyers who want to see the wider range of bodies that share this platform can browse the full SAGMOTO truck range before committing to a wheelbase.

Kitchen power budget and electrical architecture

The single biggest design risk on a food truck is underestimating electrical load. A typical fitted kitchen draws a grid of consumers: a refrigerated prep counter at 300 to 600 watts, a griddle or fryer bank at 3,000 to 6,000 watts, a water heater at 1,500 to 2,000 watts, LED lighting and a POS terminal at 200 watts, and an extraction or make-up air fan at 400 to 800 watts. At peak service a single shift can exceed 9,000 watts, and that load must be served without sagging the supply or tripping protection. On the X7 the standard approach is a 5 to 8 kVA diesel generator mounted in a ventilated rear locker, backed by a 200 Ah leisure battery and a 3,000 watt pure-sine inverter for quiet early-morning prep when the generator is switched off. The engine alternator itself is reserved for chassis loads so the kitchen never competes with the starter battery.

A cleaner alternative for lighter menus is a 48 volt architecture where the Yuchai engine drives a high-output alternator feeding a lithium bank, then an inverter supplies the cooking line. This removes the generator noise that annoys residential pitches, but it caps total load to roughly 6,000 watts, so it only suits coffee, crepe, or churro concepts rather than full grill service. The rule of thumb is to size the generator at one and a half times the measured running load, never the nameplate peak, because real service rarely triggers every appliance at once.

Fresh water and grey water systems

Any unit that handles open food needs a dedicated potable water circuit separated from the wash-down circuit, and the X7 frame has the floor height to carry both beneath the body without eating internal volume. Fresh water is stored in a food-grade polyethylene tank fed through a filtered filling point, pressurised by a 12 volt diaphragm pump, and routed to a hand basin, a three-compartment sink, and a paddle rinse. Grey water from washing and cooking is collected in a sealed tank with a screened vent and a manual dump valve compliant with most municipal discharge rules. Power budget rule of thumb: measure the running watts of every appliance you will use together, then specify the generator at one and a half times that figure. A 9,000 watt measured running load needs a 5 to 8 kVA set, not a 9 kVA nameplate matched to peak draws that never coincide.

Body weight versus payload: the numbers

The X7 chassis-cab arrives with a curb weight around 3,200 to 3,800 kg depending on cab and wheelbase, leaving a body and cargo allowance that shrinks quickly once stainless cladding, insulation, and water are added. A 5.0 m box with full kitchen typically weighs 1,150 kg dry, carries 210 kg of water when full, and leaves roughly 1,400 to 1,800 kg for generator, equipment, and stock before the 12,000 kg GVW ceiling. Operators who overload the rear axle discover premature leaf spring fatigue and unstable cornering, so the discipline is to fix the menu first, weigh the equipment second, and choose the box length third. A shorter 4.2 m body is the safer choice for single-operator coffee or dessert concepts where volume matters more than a full grill line.

Insulation is the silent weight thief. A 50 mm polyurethane panel wall adds about 12 kg per square metre of surface, and a 6.2 m box has enough surface to cost 180 kg before a single appliance is bolted in. Specifying 40 mm panel with a reflective skin in hot climates trims that mass while holding the internal temperature window the refrigeration unit can manage. The payload margin you protect here is the difference between a truck that trades for five years and one that limps to the workshop every season.

Payload margin discipline: always reserve at least 15 percent of the available body allowance as headroom. Water, stock, and a passenger all arrive after the build is weighed, and that buffer is what keeps the rear axle inside its legal and mechanical limit on a real shift.

Hygiene and street-vending compliance in export markets

Street-vending regulations differ sharply by city, but the common spine is traceable food contact surfaces, separated clean and dirty water, vermin-proof construction, and a documented hand-wash station. The X7 body should use seamless 304 or 316 stainless on all preparation faces, coved internal corners, and a floor drain that routes only to the grey tank. Many export destinations require a local health inspection of the finished unit before a trading permit is issued, so builders should keep material certificates and a simple schematic of the water circuits. Cities with Mediterranean or Gulf summer profiles additionally expect shaded ventilation and a make-up air path so the operator is not working in a sealed hot box.

Documentation travels with the truck. A one-page equipment list naming every pump, heater, and refrigerant, plus the electrical single-line diagram, will satisfy most port-health officers and shorten the time from arrival to first sale. Operators who skip this step routinely spend more on rework than the original body saving.

Cooking and refrigeration equipment selection

Equipment choice should follow the power budget rather than the menu fantasy. Induction cooktops are efficient and cool but draw hard on the inverter, while LPG griddles draw nothing electrically and keep the generator small, at the cost of a certified gas locker and ventilation. A chest-style refrigerated prep unit outperforms an upright in a moving vehicle because the cold stays put when the door opens at a pitch. For frozen stock a separate draw-down freezer on its own compressor avoids the common failure where one large box is asked to do two jobs and serves neither. Match each appliance to a dedicated circuit with its own breaker so a fault in the fryer never darkens the POS terminal.

Climate sets the menu ceiling as much as the generator does. In Gulf and equatorial cities the ambient heat forces the refrigeration unit to work harder, so specify a compressor with 20 to 30 percent more capacity than the temperate-climate figure and shade the body with a reflective roof skin that cuts solar gain through the panel. In colder markets the risk flips to condensation and frozen supply lines, which calls for trace heating on the fresh-water circuit and a heated grey tank so the dump valve does not freeze overnight on a winter pitch. The body builder should also fit a serving window that closes flush, because an open hatch in a dusty or insect-heavy district defeats the hygiene standard the unit was built to meet, and these regional adaptations are cheap at the design stage yet expensive once the truck is welded and painted.

Step-by-step build and import checklist

Begin with the menu and the city permit rules, because they set the box length and the water circuit. Second, weigh the proposed equipment and confirm it fits the X7 payload margin with the 15 percent reserve. Third, agree the electrical architecture and size the generator against measured running load. Fourth, specify stainless grade, insulation thickness, and drainage to the local hygiene standard. Fifth, request material certificates and a single-line diagram for port inspection. Sixth, arrange shipping and confirm the destination requires no feature your build omits. Operators who follow this order avoid the expensive late changes that come from fitting a body before the rules are known.

The X7 chassis-cab is not the heaviest platform in the catalogue, and that is precisely its advantage for mobile catering: it keeps weight, width, and cost inside the envelope a city food business can actually use. Treat the power and water budgets as the design, and the stainless and paint as the finish, and the result is a unit that passes inspection and earns its place on the street.

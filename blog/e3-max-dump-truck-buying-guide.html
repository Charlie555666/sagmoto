E3 MAX Heavy Dump Truck Buying Guide: Body Sizing, Axles and Payment Terms

2026-11-09Buying Guide8 min read

The SAGMOTO E3 MAX sits at the upper end of the SAGMOTO heavy mixed-duty range, and it is the model most overseas buyers ask about when their loads live between 25 and 40 tonnes and their routes alternate between paved haul roads and broken quarry ramps. With the Weichai WP12 engine rated between 460 and 520 hp and peak torque reaching 2250 Nm, the truck has the reserve needed for repeated loaded climbs, yet the buying decision itself is rarely about the engine. It is about how the rest of the truck is specified around that engine: the tipper body, the axles, the suspension, and the terms under which the unit is paid for and shipped. This guide walks through those decisions in the order a fleet engineer would actually make them.

Step One: Establish the Legal Payload Before Anything Else

Every other specification decision depends on the payload you are legally allowed to carry, and this number differs by market more than most buyers expect. In markets that follow European-style axle load rules, a 6x4 rigid tipper with 13-tonne rear axles and a 9-tonne front axle typically caps out at a gross vehicle weight around 31 to 32 tonnes, leaving a legal payload of roughly 19 to 21 tonnes once the 11-to-12-tonne kerb mass of a steel-bodied tipper is subtracted. In markets with more permissive regulations or private haul roads, such as mine-adjacent construction sites, operators routinely load the same chassis to 35 or 40 tonnes, which shifts the specification conversation toward stronger axles and heavier suspension.

Ask three questions before you request a quotation: what is the national gross vehicle weight limit for a 3-axle rigid, what is the maximum steering axle load, and is there a tolerance or fine structure for overloading? The answers determine whether you order the standard 13-tonne single-reduction rear axle pair or the hub-reduction version, which carries a roughly 1.5-tonne higher rating at the cost of about 40 kg of additional unsprung mass per axle and slightly higher fuel use on paved runs. Ordering the wrong axle rating is the single most expensive mistake in this segment, because it cannot be corrected cheaply after delivery.

Fix the legal payload number first, then build the truck around it. An E3 MAX specified for 20-tonne legal loads in a regulated market is a different truck from one specified for 30-tonne private-road loads, even though both carry the same model badge and engine.

Engine and Driveline: Matching the WP12 to Your Haul Profile

The E3 MAX is offered with the Weichai WP12 in 460, 480 and 520 hp states of tune, all delivering torque curves that plateau at or near 2250 Nm across the 1100-to-1500 rpm band. For buyers, the practical question is whether the top output is actually needed. A tipper hauling laterite or aggregate on rolling terrain with gradients under 6 percent will pull 20-tonne payloads comfortably with the 460 hp rating and return measurable fuel savings. Operators climbing 8-to-10 percent quarry ramps several times per hour, or operating at altitude above 1500 metres where naturally aspirated losses compound, should take the 480 or 520 hp option.

The transmission is the Fast 12JSD 12-speed in most export configurations, with a short first gear that allows a fully loaded truck to creep up a wet ramp without riding the clutch. Specified correctly, the driveline holds the engine inside its torque plateau on gradients up to about 12 percent at 30-tonne GCW. The engine brake comes standard, and an auxiliary hydraulic retarder is a worthwhile option for quarry work, adding roughly USD 2,800 to the unit price but reducing brake lining consumption by half on descent-heavy cycles. Service intervals are 30,000 km or 600 engine hours for oil and filter changes under moderate duty, dropping to 20,000 km in high-dust quarry environments.

Tipper Body Sizing: Volume Versus Density

The tipper body is where most payload capacity is won or lost, and the arithmetic is straightforward. Body volume requirements depend entirely on material density. Broken granite and basalt run around 1.6 to 1.7 tonnes per cubic metre, so a 20-tonne legal payload needs only 12 to 13 cubic metres of struck capacity. Laterite, common across West African and Southeast Asian construction sites, runs closer to 1.3 tonnes per cubic metre, needing 15 to 16 cubic metres for the same payload. Wet sand and crushed concrete can exceed 1.9 tonnes per cubic metre, making an oversized body a liability that invites chronic overloading and premature axle failures.

Standard export bodies for this chassis are hardox-lined steel tippers in 14, 16, 18 and 20 cubic metre sizes. The 18 and 20 cubic metre bodies suit light-density materials but raise the centre of gravity, and buyers in markets with tipping regulations should check height restrictions before choosing them. Side wall thickness of 6 mm with a 8 mm floor in Hardox 450 is the common wearing-spec; full 8 mm side walls extend body life in granite service by an estimated 30 percent at a weight penalty of about 300 kg, which is 300 kg of payload surrendered every single load. Decide based on your material, not on the thickest steel available.

Match body volume to material density, not to the biggest body offered. A 14 cubic metre body hauling granite at 1.7 t/m3 carries 23.8 tonnes heaped, while the same body hauling wet sand at 1.9 t/m3 carries 26.6 tonnes before heaping, well beyond legal limits in regulated markets.

Quarry Versus Construction Specification Packages

Suppliers typically offer two build philosophies for this platform. The construction package assumes mixed running: paved arterial roads between site and disposal point, gradients under 8 percent, loaded cycle distances of 10 to 40 km, and standard 10-hour shifts. The quarry package assumes the truck lives on unimproved ground: ramp gradients to 12 percent, constant dust, shock loading during loading under excavators, and cycles under 5 km where brake and clutch wear dominate operating cost. The quarry package adds the hub-reduction axle option, the retarder, a heavier front bumper and under-body protection, a raised air intake with cyclonic pre-filter, and a reinforced cab suspension. The combined premium runs approximately USD 6,500 to 8,000 over the construction package, FOB Chinese port.

Beware of over-specification in the opposite direction. Buyers in urban construction markets who order the full quarry package pay for capability they never use, add roughly 400 kg of kerb mass, and lose that payload on every trip. If your cycle involves public roads with weighbridges, the lighter construction package with legal payloads will out-earn the heavier truck through sheer tonnage moved per day. Inspection Points Before Accepting Delivery

Ex-works inspection matters more with tippers than with tractors because body build quality varies between contracting body builders. Check the floor plate weld seams for full penetration, verify that the Hardox liner is bolted rather than only welded so sections can be replaced, inspect the hinge pins and top door seals, and confirm the hydraulic system uses a name-brand pump and hoist cylinder. On the chassis side, confirm the axle rating stamped on the specification plate matches your order, check that the leaf packs carry the leaf count you specified, and photograph the fast gearbox and axle dataplates for your records since these numbers drive all future parts ordering.

Payment Terms and Delivery Logistics

Export payment for these units is typically structured as 30 percent deposit with the balance against shipping documents, with the deposit paid after contract and the balance due when the bill of lading is issued. Irrevocable letters of credit at sight are accepted from established importers, though they add bank charges and one to two weeks of processing. Production lead time for a standard construction-spec E3 MAX with a common body size is 25 to 35 days; the quarry package with options can extend to 45 days. Shipping is normally roll-on roll-off from Qingdao or Shanghai, with container shipment possible only for knock-down assemblies, which most buyers avoid for tippers given reassembly complexity. Rolled-into-deck freight to West African base ports currently quotes around USD 4,500 to 6,500 per unit depending on destination and season.

Which Chinese Truck Brands Export the Most in 2026?

2026-11-27Buyer Guide10 min read

Quick answer: Industry estimates put total Chinese heavy-truck exports in 2026 in the 280,000 to 320,000 unit band. Sinotruk leads with roughly 120,000 to 140,000 units, FAW follows at 60,000 to 75,000, Dongfeng at 50,000 to 65,000 and SHACMAN at 40,000 to 55,000. The remaining brands together account for roughly 8 to 12 percent.

Every number on this page is an industry estimate or an order of magnitude, not an official statistic. Customs aggregates are published with different definitions, re-export hubs attribute goods in different ways, and brand groups report through several entities. The figures are therefore given as ranges built around a planning total near 310,000 units, with shares calculated from that midpoint so they add sensibly to about one hundred percent. Read the ranking as a guide to supply depth and dealer density rather than as a league table with a single winner.

How is the 2026 export ranking built?

The ranking uses heavy and medium-heavy truck units under the HS 8704 heading for motor vehicles designed for the transport of goods. It counts complete trucks rather than kits or parts, and it groups brands at the parent level so that Sinotruk includes both HOWO and Sitrak products, FAW includes the J6 and JH6 families, and Dongfeng includes its commercial vehicle lines. The midpoint approach matters: each brand range is an independent estimate, so the upper end of every range cannot occur in the same year as the upper end of the total. Buyers should treat the midpoint and its share as the working case and the range ends as plausible variation.

Where a brand sells through several export entities, the estimate combines them; where a group owns more than one badge, the badges are counted together. This is why a table of declared exporters can look different from a table of brands. The practical purpose is to show where the parts networks, service manuals and trained technicians are deepest, because that is what matters when a truck is thousands of kilometres from the factory.

What does the ranking mean for parts availability?

Export volume and parts depth are closely linked. A brand shipping well over a hundred thousand units a year supports more regional warehouses, more service training and a wider range of stocked consumables. For a fleet operator this usually means a filter or a brake chamber arrives in days rather than weeks. A brand with a smaller export base may still have excellent factory support, but the buyer should confirm where the parts are held, which items are stocked locally and what the replenishment lead time is for a non-stock item. Ask for a written parts list with lead times before ordering.

Resale is the second consequence. In most markets a high-volume brand has more used examples, more buyers and a more established price reference, which makes an asset easier to dispose of after five or six years. A lower-volume brand may depreciate faster simply because fewer people know it. That difference should be weighed against purchase price: a cheaper truck with weaker resale can still win on total cost if it earns well for the years you keep it. Fleets that replace trucks on a fixed cycle usually care more about resale than owner-operators who run a vehicle until it is worn out.

Should a smaller brand be ruled out?

No. A smaller exporter can be the right choice when its product fits a route better than the market leader. A lighter chassis with a lower tare weight earns more payload on a weight-limited route, and a simpler mechanical specification may be easier to maintain where workshops have limited diagnostic equipment. Some smaller brands offer configuration flexibility that a high-volume producer cannot match on a short order. The question is not whether a brand is in the top four, but whether it can support the specific duty and geography you operate in.

The test is practical. Ask how many units of your model are already in the country, where the nearest parts stock is held, who trains the technicians and how warranty claims are paid. If the answers are specific and documented, a smaller brand is a reasonable choice. If they are vague, the low purchase price is hiding a service risk. How do destination markets change the picture?

Africa takes a large share of Chinese heavy trucks, with construction, mining and bulk transport favouring high-payload tippers and tractors. The Middle East favours heavy haulage and high-temperature specifications, with strong demand for engines and cooling packages that tolerate sustained ambient heat. Central Asia buys tractors for long distances between sparse service points, which puts a premium on fuel range and parts availability. Latin America mixes construction and distribution work and often has stricter emission and documentation requirements.

How is HS 8704 customs data read?

HS 8704 covers motor vehicles for the transport of goods. It includes complete trucks across a range of gross weights, so a raw unit count mixes light, medium and heavy vehicles unless the data is filtered by subheading or by declared gross vehicle weight. Re-exports add another complication: a truck shipped to a regional hub and then resold may appear in the customs data of both countries. For these reasons the ranking is presented as bands rather than as a precise count, and any figure should be described as an industry estimate.

A buyer using customs data directly should check three things. First, confirm whether the series counts units or declared value, because a value ranking favours high-spec tractors while a unit ranking favours construction tippers. Second, identify the reporting country and whether the figure is an export or an import record, since the two rarely match exactly. Once those points are settled, customs data is useful for seeing which brands have an established presence in a market and whether that presence is growing.

What should a buyer check before placing an order?

Use the ranking to shortlist, then test four things on the shortlisted models: parts availability, dealer or service density, resale evidence and configuration fit. Ask for named references in your region, request a line-item parts quotation, and confirm the emission calibration required in your market before the order is placed. Engine options can be calibrated to the emission standard a market requires, so the buyer should state the required level rather than assume a default. Finally, document the entity behind the quotation using the verification sequence described elsewhere on this site. A credible brand and a credible seller are two separate checks, and both are needed before a deposit.

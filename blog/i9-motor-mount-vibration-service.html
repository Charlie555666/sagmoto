SAGMOTO i9 Motor Mounts: Diagnosing Vibration in an Electric Truck

2026-11-19Maintenance & After-sales9 min read

An electric truck is silent enough that a vibration which would be ignored on a diesel becomes impossible to overlook. A driver of a SAGMOTO i9 may report a low hum through the seat at a particular speed, a shimmer in the mirror at idle, or a knock when torque is applied after a stop. Because there is no engine firing to mask these signals, the mounts that isolate the drive unit become a diagnostic focal point. This guide explains how to separate normal electric-vehicle NVH from a genuine motor-mount fault and what to do about it.

The i9 is a battery-electric heavy truck with an e-motor in the 250 to 350 kW class, an LFP battery of 282 to 350 kWh and an AMT or e-axle arrangement in 6x4 and 8x4 forms between 31 and 49 tonnes. An e-motor produces instant torque from zero rpm, so the reaction force applied to its mounts at launch is much sharper than in a diesel that builds torque gradually. That characteristic, combined with the absence of a heavy engine block to act as a damper, makes mount design and condition critical to refinement.

Key point: Vibration that changes with road speed usually points to wheels, tyres or driveline balance, while vibration that changes with torque demand at a fixed speed points to the motor mounts or the e-axle reaction links. Identify which variable drives the symptom before touching any hardware.

Why an electric truck still vibrates

It is a common misconception that an EV is vibration-free. The electric motor itself is inherently smooth, but torque reaction must be transmitted somewhere. When the motor accelerates the vehicle, an equal and opposite force tries to rotate the drive unit about its axis, and the mounts resist that rotation through their rubber elements. At high torque, those elements deflect; when torque is released, they spring back. The resulting movement can be felt through the frame and cab if the mounts are soft, aged or incorrectly positioned.

Secondary sources matter too. The reduction gear inside the e-axle, the driveshaft joints, the cooling compressor and the power-electronics fans all produce narrow-band excitations. A mount that is designed to isolate a particular frequency can become a transmitter when its rubber stiffens with age or contamination, shifting its natural frequency into the range where the axle operates. This is why a truck can develop a vibration after months of apparently trouble-free service without any component visibly breaking.

Mount construction and materials

The i9 drive unit is located by a set of elastomeric mounts, typically a combination of shear-loaded blocks at the front and torque-reaction links at the rear. The rubber compound is selected to be soft enough to isolate high-frequency motor whine yet stiff enough to control low-frequency movement under full torque. Some designs include a hydraulic damping chamber to dissipate energy at resonance. A fleet should never substitute a generic rubber block of similar dimensions, because a small change in stiffness can transform the vehicle character.

Heat is the main enemy. An e-motor and its inverter generate significant warmth, and the mounts sit close to that source. Over time, heat cycles harden the rubber, reduce its ability to absorb movement and may cause bonding to separate from the steel plates. Fluid contamination from a leaking cooler or road spray accelerates the process. Inspect the mounts for cracking, oil soaking and visible separation of rubber from metal at every major service, especially on trucks operating in hot climates or on steep routes where the motor runs near its thermal limit.

The table is a starting frame, not a verdict. Electric drivelines can produce more than one vibration at once, and a driver often reports the dominant one while a second remains unnoticed. A systematic technician works through the sources in order of probability and cost, recording the speed and torque condition for each observation. That discipline avoids the expensive error of replacing a motor assembly when the real fault is a worn mount or an unbalanced prop shaft.

Key point: Use an accelerometer or a phone-based vibration app and record readings at the mount bracket and at the cab floor under identical conditions. A large difference means the mount is isolating correctly; similar readings mean the rubber has stopped doing its job.

A diagnostic sequence that works

Begin with a visual inspection. Check every mount for cracks that radiate from the bonded plate, for signs of fluid leakage in hydraulic units, and for bolts that have worked loose. Tighten fasteners to the specified torque in a cross pattern, because uneven clamping can preload the rubber and shift its behaviour. Mark the bolts so a future inspection can reveal movement at a glance.

Next, run a controlled road test with the truck lightly loaded. Ask the driver to accelerate gently, then firmly, then lift off, while an observer notes when the vibration appears. If the symptom tracks torque rather than speed, apply the brake lightly while holding motor torque; a mount-related vibration often changes under that combined load. Finish with a coast-down in neutral, where driveline wind-down can reveal a resonance band that acceleration masked.

For a workshop with the right equipment, a modal check is decisive. Secure a vibration sensor to the mount bracket and log amplitude against motor rpm. Compare the trace with a known-good truck of the same specification if one is available. A peak that appears at the same frequency on both units is likely inherent to the driveline; a peak present only on the complainant points to a mount that has changed its dynamic stiffness.

Interaction with thermal management

On the i9, the drive unit, inverter and battery cooling circuits all run close to the motor mounts, and their condition changes the mounts environment in ways that are easy to overlook. A cooling hose that weeps onto a rubber block softens it locally, while a failing inverter fan that raises the surrounding temperature by a few degrees accelerates hardening across the whole mount. When a vibration complaint appears alongside a rising motor-temperature trend, investigate the two together rather than treating them as separate faults.

Fleets can use the truck own telemetry to support this work. Motor temperature, inverter temperature, torque demand and road speed are already logged on most electric chassis, and a technician who overlays those channels with a vibration record can often identify the trigger without a road test. A vibration that coincides with a thermal derate event, for example, points toward a mount that is responding to heat rather than a mechanical imbalance in the axle.

The same logic applies after any repair to the cooling or power-electronics systems. If a mechanic has disturbed the e-axle connections or the high-voltage cabling that is clipped to the mount brackets, recheck the mount torque and alignment before the truck returns to service. A cable loom that is pulled tight against a mount can transmit driveline movement into the cab as a new vibration, and the fix is a clip rather than a new component. Careful reassembly is the cheapest way to preserve the refinement that electric ownership is meant to deliver.

Replacement practice and intervals

When a mount fails, replace the full set rather than the single visibly damaged unit. Rubber ages across all mounts at a similar rate, and mixing a fresh, stiff block with aged, softened ones unbalances the locating geometry and can create a new vibration. Keep the vehicle level during replacement so the drive unit returns to its original attitude, and fit the new parts without forcing bolts into place with a lever, which would introduce preload.

These lives are indicative and assume genuine parts fitted to specification. Fleets that operate near the thermal limit of the drive unit should inspect more often, because heat is the dominant ageing mechanism. Keep a record of every mount replacement with the hour meter reading and the observed condition, so a pattern can be identified across the fleet. A batch of early failures points to an installation error or a duty cycle that needs a different mount specification.

Keeping the electric driveline refined

Electric trucks win acceptance with drivers when they are quiet and smooth, and the mounts are central to that experience. A vibration that is ignored does not stay static: loose brackets fatigue, bonded rubber separates and the resulting movement can damage cables, coolant lines and the e-axle connections. What begins as a comfort complaint can become a reliability problem if it is left untreated for a season.

For operators building out an electric fleet, the SAGMOTO new energy electric trucks range shares common NVH principles across its models, so a diagnostic method developed on the i9 transfers to smaller electric chassis. Treat mount condition as part of routine reliability engineering rather than an occasional inconvenience, and the truck will keep the refined character that made the electric choice attractive in the first place.

SAGMOTO X9s vs X6s Dump Truck: 2026 Internal Comparison

2026-10-07Comparison9 min read

Two heavy dumps, two different jobs

The X9s and the X6s sit in the same heavy dump family but they are not competitors for the same slot. The X9s is the premium 8x4 heavy dump built around the Weichai WP10H engine in the 400 to 540 horsepower range with torque up to 2,500 Nm, aimed at high-volume mining overburden and long pit hauls. The X6s is the versatile heavy dump offered as 6x4 or 8x4 with the Weichai WP12.480 rated at 2,200 Nm, paired with a Fast Gear 12JS160TA manual or a 12JZ160 automated manual transmission, aimed at quarry, construction and regional aggregate duty. This comparison sets out which fleet should pick which.

The single most useful way to read the two trucks is by duty cycle, not by brochure. A mine moving three million tonnes of overburden a year needs the X9s; a quarry feeding a regional ready-mix plant needs the X6s. Mixing them up costs money on both sides: the X9s is over-specified and over-priced for quarry shuttle work, and the X6s is under-reserved for a 24-hour mining pit. The tables below make the difference concrete.

Key point: Choose by duty cycle, not by budget headline. The X9s is the right truck when the pit runs long cycles or heavy overburden at altitude; the X6s is the right truck for quarry shuttle, construction fill and regional aggregate where flexibility and AMT comfort matter more than peak torque.

The price gap is roughly USD 10,000 to USD 14,000 at the ex-works level between comparable 8x4 bodies, and it widens with the higher WP10H ratings and the larger bodies. That gap is justified only when the duty cycle uses the extra torque and the larger volume; on a short quarry shuttle it is simply cost you carry without return.

What the Weichai engines actually deliver

The WP10H in the X9s is a 10-litre-class platform tuned for high torque across a wide band, which is why it holds up to 2,500 Nm. That torque plateau is what lets a loaded 8x4 pull out of a pit ramp without riding the clutch and hold speed on a long grade without a downshift. The WP12.480 in the X6s is a 12-litre unit with 2,200 Nm, slightly lower peak but a very usable spread that suits mixed duty where the truck is not always fully loaded.

For altitude, the WP10H has more headroom because the higher ratings absorb the thin-air loss better; for sea-level quarry work the WP12.480 is more than enough and its lower rated power trims fuel burn on light cycles. The choice is not about which engine is "better" — it is about which engine matches the load factor of the route.

Key point: On a 24-hour mining pit the X9s torque plateau pays back through faster cycle times and lower clutch wear; on a quarry shuttle with frequent empty return legs the X6s lower rated power saves fuel without costing cycle time.

Payload and body volume: where the X9s earns its price

Payload legality depends on local axle law, but the chassis capability differs. The X9s 8x4 is engineered for the heavier, denser loads and the larger bodies used in mining overburden, and its frame and suspension are rated for the shock loading of a production pit. The X6s 8x4 shares the eight-wheel footprint but is optimised for the 18 to 24 cubic metre range where aggregate and construction fill live, and the 6x4 variant trades some payload for lower tare and better manoeuvrability in tight quarry layouts.

Body volume is the second lever. A mine that runs a 26 cubic metre body on the X9s moves more per trip than the same body on a chassis not rated for the load, so the effective cost per tonne shifted falls. A quarry that over-specifies to the X9s carries 2 to 4 cubic metres of unused body and thousands of dollars of unused chassis on every empty return leg.

AMT availability: the X6s comfort advantage

The X6s offers the 12JZ160 automated manual transmission as an option, which the X9s does not in its base form. For fleets running road-biased aggregate duty or operating in markets with driver shortages, the AMT is a real advantage: it reduces fatigue, smooths clutch life and lowers the skill bar for new drivers. The X9s is built for pit discipline where a confident manual driver and maximum torque matter more than shift comfort, and its heavier manual is the right tool in that environment.

This is the point where fleet profile decides the truck. A quarry with high driver turnover and a road haul component should lean X6s with AMT; a dedicated mining fleet with trained pit drivers and maximum production should lean X9s. The AMT is not a weakness in the X6s — it is the feature that makes the X6s the more flexible machine for mixed duty.

Price gap and total cost of ownership

The ex-works gap of USD 10,000 to USD 14,000 is only the first number. The X9s recovers part of it through higher payload per cycle and longer service intervals under hard duty, so on a high-volume pit the cost per tonne shifted can favour the X9s despite the higher ticket. The X6s recovers its lower ticket through fuel savings on light cycles and lower driver training cost where AMT is specified. Neither truck "wins" on price in the abstract; the winner is the one whose duty cycle matches.

For fleets running both duty types, the correct answer is often a mixed fleet: X9s units on the long pit and overburden, X6s units on the quarry shuttle and road aggregate. That split avoids paying the X9s premium where it is not earned and avoids stressing the X6s where it is not rated. It also smooths parts planning because both share Weichai and Fast Gear service families.

Key point: The price gap is real but not decisive. Model cost per tonne shifted, not purchase price. A mixed X9s and X6s fleet usually beats a single-type fleet for operators who run both pit and quarry duty.

Application fit summary

Mining overburden and long, steep or high-altitude pit hauls are X9s work. The WP10H torque, the 8x4 rating and the larger body volume convert directly into fewer cycles and lower cost per tonne. Quarry shuttle, urban construction fill, regional aggregate and any duty with a road component and driver-rotation pressure are X6s work, especially with the 12JZ160 AMT. The 6x4 X6s variant adds tight-site manoeuvrability and lower tare for confined quarries and city jobs.

Operators comparing the SAGMOTO dump truck models 6x4 8x4 will find the X6s and X9s sit at the top of the dump range, with the 6x4 variants of the X6s covering the lighter construction and regional duty below the 8x4 mining units. The decision tree is consistent across the family: match axle count and power to the load factor, then specify the body to the material.

Brakes, chassis and site safety

Both trucks use full air braking with engine exhaust brake as standard, but the X9s is built for the higher kinetic loads of a production pit, with uprated foundation brakes and a heavier frame to absorb the shock of constant loading and tipping on graded haul roads. The X6s carries the same air-brake architecture in a lighter tune suited to quarry and road duty where the cycle is shorter and the speeds lower. On site safety, the X9s benefits from a steeper ramp angle and a stronger hoist for sticky overburden, while the X6s trades some tip angle for a lower centre of mass that helps on tight quarry benches and urban construction access. Neither is unsafe; the difference is the margin each carries for its intended duty, and that margin is part of what the price gap buys.

Resale and fleet standardisation

Resale follows uptime and documentation. The X9s, used in hard mining duty, should be speced with the maintenance book kept to the 15,000 to 20,000 km oil interval and the hoist inspected every 500 hours; a well-documented X9s holds value because mining buyers pay for proven pit units. The X6s, in mixed duty, should follow the same discipline with tyre and brake attention at 5,000 km. For fleets standardising on SAGMOTO, running both types simplifies parts because the Weichai and Fast Gear families overlap, and it lets the operator move a driver or a spare between units without re-training on a new platform.

Conclusion

The X9s and X6s are not a question of better or worse; they are a question of fit. The X9s is the premium 8x4 heavy dump for high-volume, high-torque mining duty where its WP10H 400 to 540 horsepower and 2,500 Nm plateau, plus its larger body, earn the price gap in cost per tonne shifted. The X6s is the versatile heavy dump for quarry, construction and regional aggregate, where its WP12.480, its 6x4 or 8x4 flexibility and its optional 12JZ160 AMT make it the more practical and more economical machine. Specify by duty cycle, model cost per tonne, and for mixed operations run both.

SAGMOTO X5 Wrecker and Road-Recovery Unit Build Guide

2026-11-04Industry Application9 min read

Why the X5 chassis suits recovery work

The SAGMOTO X5 medium-heavy chassis delivers 240 to 300 horsepower from the Weichai WP7 or Cummins engine families and carries a gross vehicle weight near 18,000 kg, which places it in the sweet spot for a recovery operator who tows broken-down rigs but does not need a heavy prime-mover wrecker. A recovery unit lives on call, so the X5 low kerb weight of about 7,200 kg leaves roughly 10,000 kg for the body, winch, boom and the casualty's drawbar load without exceeding the rear axle limit. The cab-forward layout also gives the operator a clear view of a tight motorway pickup where every second of lane closure costs a fine.

The X5 shares the Fast 10JSD gearbox and dual-circuit air brakes with the wider SAGMOTO range, so a multi-brand recovery fleet can standardise spares and driver training. Its 4,500 mm wheelbase keeps the wheelbase short enough for inner-city spin turns yet long enough to carry a rear underlift that reaches a stranded 6x4 tractor without overloading the front axle during the lift.

Recovery operators value the X5 because its 18,000 kg GVWR sits below the heavy prime-mover class, so a single driver can hold a standard goods licence in most markets rather than the higher grade a 26,000 kg wrecking rig demands. That licensing line keeps the unit usable for the daily motorway clearance work that pays the standing cost between major incident call-outs.

Match the boom and winch to the heaviest casualty you will actually tow, not the headline rating, because an over-specified rig wastes 1,500 kg of payload you could spend on stabilisers and lighting.

Underslung, boom and integrated recovery layouts

Three recovery bodies fit the X5. The underlift wrecker carries a telescoping L-arm beneath the rear that cradles the casualty's axle or wheels and tows it with the front lifted, which suits cars, vans and light trucks on a quick motorway clearance. The boom wrecker adds a hydraulically raised A-frame above the deck with a top pulley and a 5 to 8 tonne winch, letting one operator recover a vehicle from a ditch or a field without a second truck. The integrated unit combines both, with the underlift for road towing and the boom for the off-road pull, and it is the layout most highway authorities specify for incident response.

The integrated body is heavier, typically 3,800 to 4,600 kg empty, so it suits the 300 hp X5 with the reinforced rear springs rather than the 240 hp base. A casualty that has left the road usually needs the boom first and the underlift second, and the operator sequences the two from the wireless remote so they never stand in the winch line.

The underlift arms are adjustable in width to suit wheelbases from a small van at 2,300 mm to a 6x4 tractor at 3,600 mm, so one set of cradles covers most casualty types the operator meets. The arms lock with a spring pin that the operator can feel seat home by hand, removing doubt when working beside live traffic on a lit carriageway.

Hydraulic winch and boom capacity matching

The winch line pull must exceed the casualty's rolling resistance plus the slope, and a 6x4 tractor weighing 9,000 kg on a 10 degree incline needs about 2.5 tonnes of pull at the hook before friction, so a 5 tonne winch leaves a safe working margin. Boom capacity at full extension is lower than at the stub, often 3,000 kg extended against 6,000 kg retracted, so the operator keeps the casualty close to the pivot for a heavy pull. The X5 chassis GVWR sets the ceiling because the casualty drawbar load rides on the rear axle through the underlift.

Wire rope length of 30 to 40 metres lets the winch reach a casualty parked off the carriageway without reversing the truck into danger, and a synthetic rope option cuts handling weight for the solo operator. The rope is inspected for broken strands every shift, because a parted line on a loaded boom can whip across the deck and injure the crew.

A drum speed of about 6 to 9 metres per minute at full line pull lets the operator stage a recovery without the casualty surging, and the free-spool clutch disengages the drum so the rope pays out fast before the pull begins. Overload is prevented by a pressure-relief valve on the pump circuit, which dumps flow instead of snapping the rope if the casualty binds on a rut.

Frame reinforcement and subframe mounting

A recovery body loads the chassis in ways a freight body never does, with twisting moments from the boom and shock loads from a sudden winch catch. The X5 frame is reinforced with a bolt-on subframe of 10 mm steel channels that spreads the lift reaction across six mounting points rather than four, and the subframe is torqued to the chassis with class 10.9 bolts at the 180 Nm spec. A poorly tied subframe lets the body walk backward under repeated pulls and cracks the chassis rails at the rear crossmember.

The underlift pivot sits above the rear axle group so the lift moment transfers straight into the springs rather than through the frame in bending, and the deck is a chequer-plate steel floor rated for a casualty driven aboard under its own power. Recovery operators add a pair of under-arm restraints so a casualty with no brakes cannot roll off the cradle on a corner.

The subframe carries its own crossmembers that bridge the chassis rails so a side pull transfers into a box section rather than bending one rail alone. Fabricators blue the mounting faces before fitment to confirm full contact, because a high spot leaves a gap that lets the subframe rock and work the bolts loose after a few hundred recoveries in service.

PTO, hydraulic pump and power demand

The boom, underlift and winch draw from a hydraulic pump driven by a transmission power take-off, and the X5 needs a pump sized to about 60 to 90 litres per minute at 160 bar to lift and extend without the slow creep that holds up a lane closure. The PTO engages from the cab and should lock only when the handbrake is set, so the truck cannot drive while the boom is live. A dedicated hydraulic oil cooler keeps the circuit under 80 degrees during a long multi-vehicle recovery on a hot afternoon.

Electrical demand from the beacon bar, scene lights and the remote receiver adds a 150 ampere load, so the X5 alternator is upgraded to 200 amperes on recovery builds. The battery isolation switch lets the operator kill the whole body circuit during maintenance without disturbing the engine start battery, which prevents a stray remote signal from cycling the boom while a technician is underneath.

Operators fit a tachometer feed from the PTO so the pump runs near its 1,800 rpm sweet spot without over-driving the seals, and a neutral-start interlock stops the boom from waking while the truck is in gear. The hydraulic tank breather carries a 10 micron filter so the circuit stays clean through the dust a roadside scene always raises on a dry day.

Engage the power take-off only with the handbrake set and the wheels chocked, because a recovery truck that drives while the boom is live can drag a casualty into the operator's own cab before anyone reacts.

Stability, lighting and safety equipment

A lifted casualty shifts the centre of gravity rearward, so the X5 recovery unit carries four stabiliser legs that plant on the ground and take the side load when the boom is extended across the verge. The legs are lowered before any lift above 2,000 kg and the operator checks the bubble levels on uneven ground, because a listing truck can tip during a side pull. The scene lighting is a 12 LED bar drawing 120 watts that floods a 30 metre work area so the recovery runs safely at night on an unlit highway.

Safety kit aboard includes cones, a 4 kg extinguisher, wheel chocks rated for the casualty weight and a high-visibility livery with chevron rear markings that meet the authority's incident-vehicle standard. The beacon sequence is set to the approved amber-only flash so the unit is legal on a live carriageway, and a reverse alarm protects the crew when the truck backs toward a casualty in a lay-by.

A wheel chock rated for the casualty weight is placed on the downhill side before any lift, and the scene cones are set at 6 metre spacing back from the unit so approaching traffic gets early warning on a curve. The operator keeps the radio on the authority channel so a closing incident is heard before it reaches the work zone, and the whole recovery is logged with time, location and casualty type for the duty sheet.

SAGMOTO E3 MAX Rear Axle Hub & Wheel Bearing Maintenance Guide

2026-10-22Maintenance Guide11 min read

The rear axle hub of the SAGMOTO E3 MAX medium truck carries the full drive torque and a large share of the 12–18 tonne GVWR under load. The hub bearing assembly, the oil seal and the axle gear oil are the three components that decide whether a rear axle runs for hundreds of thousands of kilometres or fails on a loaded run. This guide covers the SAGMOTO cargo truck flatbed box stake family's E3 MAX rear-axle hub service: correct bearing preload, oil and seal intervals, fault diagnosis, a torque reference and a preventive checklist keyed to mileage and engine hours.

How the E3 MAX Rear Hub Assembly Works

The E3 MAX rear axle is a full-float design: the wheel hub rotates on tapered roller bearings carried by the axle shaft and the hub casting. Drive torque passes through the axle shaft to the wheel hub via the wheel studs and the brake drum. The hub cavity is lubricated by the same oil that lubricates the differential, while the wheel bearings run in a packed or oil-bath environment depending on the hub configuration specified. Understanding this split—inside oil vs. bearing grease—prevents the most common service mistake: over-greasing or starving the wrong zone.

On the E3 MAX, the rear hub bearings are set to a precise preload, not just "snug". Too loose and the cones wander; too tight and the rollers overheat. Preload is set with a torque wrench and a final spin-down, never by feel.

Setting Hub Bearing Preload

Correct preload is the foundation of hub life. The procedure for the E3 MAX rear hub is:

Step-by-Step Preload Procedure

  1. Raise the axle and support the chassis on stands; never work under a tyre-supported truck.
  2. Remove the hub cap and the locking hardware; withdraw the hub assembly carefully to avoid dropping the outer cone.
  3. Clean all bearings, races and the cavity; inspect for pitting, discolouration or scoring.
  4. Repack the bearings with the specified high-temperature grease; do not mix grease types.
  5. Refit the hub, snug the spindle nut to the torque below, then back off exactly as specified, then torque to the final preload value.
  6. Spin the hub several times to seat the rollers, re-check end-play with a dial indicator (target 0.05–0.10 mm).
  7. Fit the lock washer and castellated nut, secure with a new cotter pin—never reuse a deformed pin.

Always re-torque rear wheel stud nuts after the first 50 km following hub service. Heat cycles loosen fasteners; a missed re-check is a roadside wheel-off event.

Gear Oil and Hub Seal Service

The rear axle differential and the hub share lubrication paths on the E3 MAX. Use a GL-5 rated 85W-90 (or the manufacturer-specified viscosity for your climate) and never under-fill. The axle breather must be clear: a blocked breather builds pressure that forces oil past the hub seal, creating a leak that is mistakenly blamed on the seal alone.

Draining and Refilling

Warm the axle by a short drive so the oil flows. Remove the fill plug first (so you are not stuck with an empty axle and a seized plug), then the drain plug. Flush with a light oil if the drained oil shows metal or sludge. Refill to the bottom of the fill hole and confirm level with the truck on level ground. Replace both plugs with new sealing washers.

Hub Seal Replacement

A weeping seal is an early warning; a pouring seal is an emergency. Replace the hub oil seal whenever the hub is opened, and whenever a leak is found at the scheduled interval. Inspect the seal race on the axle shaft for scoring—a damaged shaft will destroy a new seal within kilometres. If the shaft journal is worn, sleeve or replace it before fitting the new seal.

A hub that is too hot to touch after a normal run is a red-line condition. Continuing to drive risks welding the bearing and destroying the hub—stop and inspect before the next kilometre.

Using Temperature as an Early Indicator

During preventive checks, feel each rear hub after a loaded run (briefly, with a glove). A hub noticeably hotter than its partner on the same axle points to one bearing carrying excessive load or running dry. Infrared thermometers make this check repeatable and safe, and a 15 °C spread between sides is grounds for investigation.

Preventive Inspection Checklist

The E3 MAX rear hub stays reliable when inspected by mileage and by engine hours for the severe-duty fleet. Use the checklist below as a daily, weekly and scheduled routine.

By Interval

  • Daily (pre-trip): Visual hub and tyre for fresh oil; check hub temperature after first loaded run; confirm no abnormal hum.
  • Weekly: Torque-check rear wheel stud nuts; confirm axle breather is clear; inspect for seal weep.
  • 30,000 km (severe) / 60,000 km (normal): Drain and refill gear oil; inspect hub play and bearing condition; clear breather.
  • 60,000 km (severe) / 120,000 km (normal): Replace hub seal; repack bearings; reset preload; replace cotter pins.
  • Annual: Full axle inspection including ring and pinion mesh, housing cracks and brake interface.
Keep a hub service log per truck. When the E3 MAX changes operators or bodies, the log proves the axle was maintained—protecting resale value and warranty standing.

Specifying Parts and Field Support

Always fit genuine or equivalent-grade bearings, seals and GL-5 oil specified for the E3 MAX rear axle. Counterfeit cones may look identical but fail early under the 18-tonne GVWR loading. For fleets running the E3 MAX in cage, box or bulk bodies, standardise the hub service interval to the severe-duty column regardless of body type, because stop-start delivery duty is harder on bearings than highway running.

Tool List and Safety Precautions

Hub service on the E3 MAX requires a defined tool set and disciplined safety. The technician needs a torque wrench rated to 500 N·m for the stud nuts, a dial indicator for end-play, bearing pullers, a clean catch pan for gear oil, and lint-free cloths. Personal protection includes gloves rated for hot oil, eye protection and, when grinding or flushing, a respirator. Critically, the truck must be on rated stands—never a jack alone—before any wheel is removed, because a falling 18-tonne GVWR axle assembly can crush a worker instantly, and the risk is highest when the hub is free of its studs.

Lock out the vehicle before hub work: park brake set, wheels chocked, key removed. A truck that rolls during service turns a routine bearing job into a fatal incident.

Cleanliness Discipline

Bearing failure is overwhelmingly caused by contamination, not load. Lay a clean mat under the work, cap open lines immediately, and never re-use solvent-wiped bearings without a final clean-bath. A single grain of grit pressed into a roller race scores the path and shortens life by tens of thousands of kilometres. The cost of a clean workspace is trivial against the cost of a roadside hub failure that strands a loaded truck and risks the wheel departing the vehicle at speed.

Post-Service Verification

After the hub is closed and the wheel refitted, the job is not done until verified. The technician torques the stud nuts to spec in a star pattern, fits a new cotter pin, and records the preload value and the gear-oil batch in the service log. A short road test follows: a gentle run building to a loaded grade, then an immediate hub-temperature check with an infrared gun. Both hubs should read within a few degrees of each other and well below the 90 °C warning line. Any heat, noise or weep within the first 50 km triggers an immediate return to the bay for re-inspection rather than a wait for the next scheduled service.

This verification step is what separates a professional fleet workshop from a roadside patch. The E3 MAX earns its low operating cost only when every hub opening ends with measured proof, not assumption, and when the next driver can trust the wheel beneath him on a loaded run.

A disciplined rear-axle hub programme turns a high-wear component into a predictable one. Set the preload correctly, keep the oil clean and at level, watch temperature and noise, and the E3 MAX rear axle will deliver the low cost-per-kilometre that fleet operators depend on.

SAGMOTO X9s vs CAMC Hanma: Heavy Tractor Spec Compared

2026-11-23Comparison Guide9 min read

Buyers shopping a heavy 6x4 tractor in the export market increasingly meet the same two names on a shortlist: the SAGMOTO X9s and the CAMC Hanma. Both are Chinese-built heavy tractors aimed at gross combination weights in the 40 to 60 tonne band, both are offered with large-displacement engines and automated gearboxes, and both compete on value rather than on a premium badge. That surface similarity makes a side-by-side comparison useful, because the two trucks differ in engine supply, driveline calibration, cab specification and the depth of overseas support. This article compares them on the numbers that decide fleet profitability, using spec ranges rather than precise brochure figures because exact ratings change with emission level and model year.

The X9s is the heavy tractor and tipper platform in the SAGMOTO range, normally fitted with a Cummins or Weichai engine rated between 460 and 560 horsepower and delivering 2,200 to 2,550 Nm, coupled to a Fast 12 or 16-speed AMT. The CAMC Hanma is a genuine heavy tractor from Anhui-based CAMC, typically offered with a Hanma-branded engine in the 400 to 500 horsepower class and a torque figure in the 2,000 to 2,400 Nm band. Both are established products with real export histories. The comparison below treats them as engineering packages and asks which one fits a particular route and workshop, not which one looks better in a brochure.

Key point: Both tractors target the same 6x4, 40 to 60 tonne class. Decide on cruise rpm, gearbox steps and the local service network before spending time on the headline horsepower figure, which is the least reliable way to separate them.

Engine supply and torque delivery

The most practical difference between the two trucks is who builds the engine. The X9s can be specified with a Cummins unit, which gives a buyer access to a well-known service network and a large pool of technicians familiar with the platform, or with a Weichai engine where that brand has stronger local cover. The CAMC Hanma is normally powered by CAMC's own Hanma engine family, an integrated package that the factory controls from cylinder head to ECU calibration. Integration has advantages: the engine, gearbox and axles are matched by one manufacturer, and a single warranty conversation covers the whole driveline. Independent engine supply gives the buyer choice and, in many countries, easier access to common wear parts.

In torque terms the X9s sits a little higher, with 2,200 to 2,550 Nm available from the larger ratings, while a typical Hanma installation sits around 2,000 to 2,400 Nm. On a flat trunk route at 45 tonnes the difference is marginal, because both engines cruise well below their peak. On a sustained mountain grade or a route with frequent stops at full weight, the extra reserve of the X9s shows up as fewer downshifts and lower clutch temperatures. Buyers should match the rating to the route rather than simply taking the biggest engine on offer, since a 560 horsepower truck running light most of the day burns more fuel than a well-matched 460 horsepower one.

Gearbox steps and rear axle ratio

Both tractors offer an automated manual gearbox, and that is where most of the driver's daily experience is formed. A 16-speed AMT keeps the steps between ratios small, so the engine stays near its efficient band as road speed changes. The X9s offers a 16-speed option alongside a 12-speed, while Hanma tractors are also sold with multiple gearbox configurations depending on the market. The buyer's job is to specify the box and the rear axle together, because a close-step gearbox paired with a very tall final drive can leave the truck struggling on a grade even though the brochure looks strong.

For a 6x4 tractor on 12R22.5 tyres, cruising at 80 to 90 km/h with a 45 tonne combination, a final drive ratio around 3.7 to 4.3 typically keeps engine speed in the economical window while retaining pull on a motorway incline. A shorter ratio improves gradeability but raises fuel consumption on long flat sections. Since both of these tractors can be ordered with different ratios, the sensible step is to calculate engine speed from tyre rolling radius, ratio and target cruise speed before signing an order. A difference of one ratio step can shift fuel burn by several percent over a high annual mileage, which outweighs a small difference in peak power.

Key point: A tall axle ratio saves fuel only where the route is flat enough to use it. On a hilly corridor, specify a shorter ratio on either tractor so the engine stays in its pull band and the clutch is not worked to death.

Cab, controls and driver environment

Long-haul buyers judge a cab at walking speed and at the end of a twelve-hour day, not in a showroom. Both tractors offer a wide sleeper cab with a bunk, air-suspended seat options and the usual cluster of switches and storerooms. The X9s cab follows the SAGMOTO heavy-truck layout, with a flat floor over much of the footwell and straightforward reach to the gear selector and dashboard controls. The Hanma cab is similarly practical, and in markets where CAMC has sold trucks for years drivers may already be familiar with its switch logic. Neither cab should be chosen on photographs; seat both with the engine running, check visibility to the mirrors and listen for wind noise at road speed before deciding.

Driver familiarity has a real cost dimension. A fleet that already operates one of these tractors will find training new drivers cheaper and will already hold the right cab consumables, from wiper blades to seat covers. A fleet switching brands must budget for a short period of higher minor repairs while the workshop learns the new layout. This is one reason a side-by-side spec comparison should always include the existing fleet composition, not just the two trucks on the table.

Emissions equipment and fuel quality

Both tractors are sold with emission systems that depend on fuel and lubricant quality. Depending on the market specification, a large Chinese tractor of this class may use cooled exhaust gas recirculation, selective catalytic reduction with AdBlue, or a combination of both. The export buyer should establish exactly which system is fitted before ordering, because the service implications are different. An SCR tractor needs a reliable supply of clean AdBlue and a driver who understands the warning ladder; an EGR tractor puts more heat and soot into the engine oil and asks for disciplined coolant and oil service. Neither system is troublesome when it is understood, and both are expensive when the workshop treats them as an afterthought.

Fuel quality is the common enemy. High-sulphur or water-contaminated diesel shortens the life of injectors, pumps and after-treatment components on either brand. In markets where fuel quality varies, fit a water separator and change filters on an hour-based schedule rather than waiting for a warning. A fleet that samples fuel at each delivery and keeps the records has evidence for a warranty claim if a batch of bad diesel causes damage; a fleet that has no records will struggle to prove anything. This discipline costs little and protects the investment in either tractor.

Service network, parts and residual value

This is the area where the two tractors separate most clearly, and it is often the deciding factor. CAMC has a long presence in several export markets and may hold a denser dealer network in those countries, which shortens parts lead time and supports resale. The X9s is supplied through export channels where the distributor typically stocks a first-year spares package, and many buyers negotiate that package into the original order: filters, belts, a water pump, a clutch kit, brake chambers and a set of sensors. A buyer who plans this stock at order time rarely regrets it; one who discovers the need after a breakdown far from the port always does.

Residual value follows the same logic. A tractor with a documented service history and a local dealer tends to command a stronger used price, because the next buyer can price the next repair before bidding. An export-supplied truck may have a thinner used-market record in a given country even when it is mechanically sound, so fleets that replace on a fixed three or four-year cycle should use a realistic resale assumption rather than assuming the truck holds its invoice price. Buyers reviewing the wider SAGMOTO tractor trucks prime mover range can check which models the chosen distributor already supports, since that support determines how quickly either tractor returns to work in its first year.

Specification checklist before you order

Before committing to either tractor, put six questions in writing: the exact engine rating and torque curve, the gearbox model and whether it is a manual or AMT, the rear axle ratio and tyre size, the emission system and its consumables, the written warranty on engine, gearbox and axles, and a parts lead-time sheet for ten fast-moving items. Ask each supplier to answer on the same sheet so the responses can be compared directly. A supplier who answers with ranges and written terms is more useful than one who quotes a single impressive horsepower figure and leaves the rest to conversation.

Total cost and the decision rule

Calculate cost per tonne-kilometre rather than purchase price alone. Fuel is the largest line on a high-mileage tractor, followed by tyres, unscheduled repair and depreciation. A truck that is cheaper to buy can lose its advantage if it spends an extra day in the workshop each month, and a truck with a strong dealer can justify a higher invoice if it keeps a complete service record. Run both over the same 100,000 kilometre calculation with local fuel prices and real parts quotes. The result is usually clear: where the route is demanding and the buyer controls maintenance, the X9s has the mechanical margin; where the local CAMC network is strong, the Hanma offers lower risk through support. Neither tractor wins everywhere, and the spreadsheet will say which one wins on your road.

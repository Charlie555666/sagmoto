SAGMOTO X9s Rear Hub Bearing and Oil Bath Service Guide

2026-11-17Maintenance9 min read

An X9s prime mover regularly runs at a gross combination weight of 40 to 60 tonnes, and every one of those tonnes is carried through the rear hub bearings. Those bearings run in an oil bath rather than grease, which gives excellent cooling on long grades but makes the quality and level of the oil critical. A hub that is a litre low on oil or has taken in water at a river crossing can overheat within a single shift and destroy a bearing set that costs far more than the service it missed. This article explains how to check oil level and grade, inspect the seals, set end play and torque, read heat and play symptoms, and protect the wheel against the worst outcome of all, a bearing that lets the wheel come off.

The oil-bath hub on the X9s rear axle

The rear hubs on an X9s use a pair of opposed tapered roller bearings inside a sealed cavity that is partly filled with hypoid gear oil. As the wheel turns, the bearings churn the oil and carry a film to the rollers, while the oil also moves heat away from the contact points to the hub shell, which is cooled by airflow. This design suits a 400 to 460 hp tractor doing continuous motorway work because it tolerates higher temperatures than a packed-grease hub. The trade-off is that the oil must be kept at the right level and free of water, and the seals that contain it must be sound.

Each hub typically holds between 1.5 and 2.5 litres of oil depending on axle model, filled through a top plug until it reaches the level plug or sight glass on the hub face. Overfilling is as harmful as underfilling, because excess oil raises churning temperature and can force product past the seal. Check level with the truck on level ground and the wheel in a normal position, since a steep slope or a sharply turned wheel gives a misleading reading.

Oil grade and change interval

The specified lubricant is an API GL-5 extreme-pressure gear oil, commonly 85W-90 for temperate climates and 80W-90 where winter starting is required. Some axle specifications call for a lower-viscosity grade to reduce drag and fuel burn on long-haul runs, but the decision should follow the axle manual rather than a general preference, because the oil film must survive the load at the bearing contact. Never mix gear oil with engine oil in the same hub; the additive packages are different and the mixture can foam under high-speed churning.

On highway duty the oil should be changed every 60,000 km or once a year, while tractors on construction haulage, quarry work or frequent water crossings should be serviced at 20,000 to 30,000 km. At each change, drain while the hub is warm so suspended contaminants leave with the oil, then refill to the level plug and run the vehicle a short distance before a final top-up.

Seal inspection and water contamination

The oil seal at the hub face and the inner seal at the axle tube are the only things keeping oil in and water out. Inspect both at every wheel-off, looking for a damp ring around the hub face, oil running down the brake drum or residue thrown onto the rim. A small weep that leaves a clean, oily film is different from a steady drip that forms on the ground after parking; the latter means the seal lip is damaged or the bore is scored and the hub needs attention before the level falls dangerously.

Water is the more insidious contaminant. After a deep crossing or prolonged wet-road work, draw a small sample from the hub; if the oil looks milky or cloudy, water has emulsified with it and the film strength has collapsed. Do not simply top up and carry on. Drain the hub completely, inspect the bearings for pitting, renew the seals and refill with fresh oil, because water left in the cavity will corrode the rollers within weeks. Fleets that operate flooded rural roads should treat post-crossing hub checks as a fixed task rather than an optional extra.

Key point: Check hub oil level with the truck level and the wheel straight, and never overfill. Milky oil after a water crossing means a full drain, seal renewal and bearing inspection, not a top-up.

End-play adjustment and torque procedure

Tapered bearings need a small, controlled clearance or light preload, and on the X9s the setting is made with the large hub nut. The usual target is an axial end play of 0.05 to 0.10 mm, measured with a dial indicator on the hub face while the wheel is levered in and out. A workshop without an indicator can use the feeler method at the lock washer, but a dial gauge is more repeatable and is the only way to catch a setting that is marginally too tight.

A common torque sequence is to tighten the hub nut to about 300 Nm to seat the bearings, back it off until the hub turns freely, then tighten to the final value in the range of 200 to 250 Nm while checking that end play stays inside the target band, and finally secure with a new lock washer or locking device. Always follow the figure for the specific axle, because flange nut sizes vary across the X9s range. Fit a new lock washer each time, never reuse a tab that has been bent, and recheck end play after the first loaded run since bearings settle in service.

Diagnosing bearing play and noise

Bearing wear announces itself in several ways, and the technician should connect the sound to the load condition. A low rumble that rises with road speed and changes when the steering is loaded usually comes from a bearing with spalled rollers. A clicking or grinding noise that appears when cornering suggests the tapered rollers are scoring, because side load shifts the contact pattern. Play felt as a rocking motion at the top of the wheel with the vehicle lifted points to loose or worn bearings rather than a steering fault.

Confirm any suspected fault by removing the hub and examining the rollers and races for discoloration, pitting or a polished band where the cage has rubbed. A bearing that has run short of oil will show blue temper colour on the rollers and should be discarded without question. Replace bearings as a matched inner and outer set with new seals, because a new roller on a worn race fails quickly.

Heat signature as an early warning

Temperature is the fastest non-invasive check a workshop can run. After a normal road run, use an infrared thermometer on each hub and compare the four or six readings; a hub running more than about 20 degrees Celsius hotter than its opposite number is losing lubrication or has excessive preload. Record baseline temperatures on a cool and a loaded run, since a heavy descent will raise all hubs and only the relative difference is meaningful.

A hub that is uniformly hot on one axle may indicate a brake dragging rather than a bearing fault, so check the drum or disc temperature at the same time. Where a hot hub is combined with a rumble or visible oil discoloration, stop the vehicle and strip the bearing before further travel. Catching a temperature trend during a scheduled inspection is far cheaper than dealing with a seized hub on a loaded tractor, and the thermometer costs little compared with the downtime it can prevent.

Water crossings and wheel-off risk

The most serious consequence of hub bearing failure on a 60-tonne combination is a wheel separating from the axle. It is rare, but it follows a clear chain: oil loss or contamination, overheating, roller spalling, bearing collapse, and finally the hub shifting until the wheel walks off the spindle. Drivers may report a new wobble or a scraping sound in the last kilometres before it happens, so any such report deserves an immediate stop rather than a note for the depot.

After any deep water crossing, stop and check each hub for warmth and for a change in oil appearance, then recheck after twenty kilometres. Water that enters a hot hub can flash to steam and push oil past the seal, leaving the bearing to run dry. The SAGMOTO heavy truck axle and parts page is a useful reference when matching bearings and seals to the correct rear axle before a long route.

Key point: A hub more than 20 degrees Celsius hotter than its opposite number needs investigation before the next loaded run. Treat a new wobble or scraping sound as a stop-now signal on a heavy tractor.

Parts to stock for hub servicing

A workshop supporting X9s tractors should carry complete bearing sets for the rear axle, hub and inner oil seals, lock washers, drain and level plugs, and the correct GL-5 oil in both common viscosities. A magnetic drain plug and a small infrared thermometer belong in the standard kit, along with a dial indicator for end-play checks. Buying bearings as a matched set with seals from one source avoids the mismatch that occurs when a new bearing is fitted against a worn race or an old seal that has already hardened.

Hub bearings give very little trouble when the oil is clean, the level is correct and the end play is set with care, and most premature failures can be traced to one of those three being neglected. Build the temperature comparison and the oil sample into the same visit each service, and a developing fault will show up as a trend rather than a breakdown. For a tractor whose job is to move heavy loads over long distances, the quiet reliability of a well-serviced hub is worth far more than the small cost of the oil and seals that produce it.

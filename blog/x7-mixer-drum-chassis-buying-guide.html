SAGMOTO X7 Concrete Mixer Chassis: Specifying 6x4 and 8x4 Builds

2026-11-20Buying & Import Guide9 min read

A concrete mixer is a chassis, a PTO-driven hydraulic system and a rotating drum that must deliver a homogeneous load on time. Specify any one of those in isolation and the truck will disappoint. The SAGMOTO X7 platform is offered as 4x2, 6x4 and 8x4 with Yuchai engines ranging from the 160 HP YC4E used on light specials up to the 340 to 400 HP class, producing 700 to 1,300 N m through Fast 8 or 9-speed gearboxes. Choosing among them for mixer duty begins with the drum, not the engine.

Drum capacity sets the chassis

A transit mixer drum is rated by its nominal mixing volume, commonly 6, 8, 10 or 12 m3 on this class of truck. The wet concrete in a full 10 m3 drum weighs about 24 t on its own once water and cement are included, before the drum, water tank and body steel are added. That concentrated load sits over the rear axle or bogie, so the chassis must be chosen to keep the rear axle group within its rating and to leave the front axle above roughly 30 percent of the gross weight for steering stability.

This is why an 8 m3 drum is normally built on a 6x4 and a 10 to 12 m3 drum on an 8x4. A 6x4 carrying a 12 m3 drum will be permanently over its rear axle rating, which shows up as premature tyre wear, spring fatigue and a truck that is nervous on wet roads. Key point: Buy the chassis to the drum, not the drum to the chassis. A 12 m3 drum on a 6x4 X7 overloads the rear axle on every load; the same drum on an 8x4 spreads the mass and protects the frame and tyres.

PTO and hydraulic drive

The drum is turned by a hydraulic motor fed from a PTO pump on the gearbox. A mixer needs enough power to rotate a fully loaded drum at 8 to 12 rpm on site and to keep it turning during a traffic delay, so the PTO must be specified rather than assumed. A 10 to 12 m3 drum typically wants 60 to 80 kW at the pump, which means a gearbox with a suitable PTO aperture and a pump rated for continuous duty, not an intermittent tipper pump.

Two details are routinely overlooked. First, the pump should be sized so the drum can be turned at mixing speed while the truck is stationary at low engine idle, otherwise fuel consumption and cab noise climb. Second, the hydraulic oil cooler must handle a hot climate: in a ready-mix yard the truck may wait loaded for an hour with the drum turning, and an undersized cooler will thin the oil and accelerate pump wear. Specify a cooler with a rated oil-to-air capacity for the site ambient, and fit an oil temperature gauge the driver can actually see.

Engine power and gearing for mixed duty

Mixer duty is stop-start, with a large proportion of low-speed running and a loaded rear axle. The 340 to 400 HP Yuchai band suits a 6x4 or 8x4 at 25 to 31 t gross. For urban work with many stops, a higher torque at low rpm matters more than top-end power, so the 1,100 to 1,300 N m variants with a 9-speed gearbox and a mid-range rear ratio around 4.8 to 5.3 are a sensible choice. For regional delivery with longer paved sections, a slightly taller ratio reduces fuel without harming drum performance because the PTO drives from the gearbox, not the road wheels.

Operators who also run general SAGMOTO cargo truck flatbed box stake bodies appreciate that the X7 platform shares service points and common components across its special-chassis variants. That commonality reduces the parts range a mixed fleet has to stock, although the mixer's PTO and hydraulic circuit remain its own maintenance item.

Weight distribution and body details

A mixer is an awkward load because the drum sits high and behind the cab. The water tank should be mounted as low and as far forward as the cab clearance allows, and the hopper and chute assembly kept light. A steel drum is heavier than an alloy one but resists the abrasion of aggregate better; operators on abrasive crushed rock usually accept the extra weight for longer drum life. The frame should be checked for the mounting bracket loads: a 12 m3 drum transmits its weight through two or three crossmembers, and additional reinforcement is worth specifying at the point of order rather than welding on later.

Key point: Concentrate on the PTO and oil cooler at specification time. A mixer that cannot turn its drum at low idle, or that overheats its hydraulic oil while waiting on site, wastes concrete and loses pump life far faster than a slightly underpowered engine.

The 6x4 is lighter, cheaper and easier around a tight urban site, but its rear axle is close to the limit with a 10 m3 drum. The 8x4 buys margin and a smoother ride with concrete on board at the cost of fuel and manoeuvrability. Where local law caps gross weight at 31 t, the 6x4 with a 9 m3 drum is often the only legally compliant way to carry a useful load; check the regulation before signing.

Drum geometry and what actually mixes concrete

The drum does not simply spin the load; internal blades lift and fold the mix as the drum rotates. At mixing speed, usually 8 to 12 rpm, the blades carry aggregate up and let it fall through the centre, producing a consistent distribution of cement, water and stone. At higher speed on the way to the site the drum acts as an agitator, keeping the concrete workable without segregating it. A drum with worn or poorly angled blades can turn at the correct speed and still deliver an uneven load, so blade condition is part of the specification conversation rather than an afterthought.

Chute and hopper design is equally practical. A 10 m3 drum discharging through a narrow hopper into a wheelbarrow or pump on a crowded site slows the pour and costs the customer money. Buyers should match the chute reach and the hopper opening to the way concrete is actually placed on their sites, and specify an extendable chute where the truck must reach over a footing or into a slab pour.

Water system and washout discipline

Every mixer needs a water tank for the mix and for washing down, and on a hot site the tank capacity is often the difference between a clean drum and a set load. A 300 to 500 litre tank is typical on a 6x4, with larger units on an 8x4. Water should be metered into the drum rather than added by eye, both for mix quality and to avoid overloading a rear axle with an unexpected volume. A simple meter and a calibrated sight gauge are inexpensive and prevent arguments about load weight at the weighbridge.

Washout discipline protects the drum and the hydraulic system. Concrete left to harden inside a drum adds weight that never leaves until it is chipped out, and it unbalances the drum so the PTO and motor work harder on every revolution. Drivers should be trained to wash out immediately after discharge where the site allows it, and the truck should carry enough water to do so without relying on a site supply that may not exist.

Fuel, maintenance access and fleet commonality

Mixer duty is hard on the PTO and the hydraulic pump, and access for service is worth checking at the point of order. The pump filter, cooler and oil dipstick should be reachable without dismantling the drum mount, and PTO engagement should be operable from the cab with a clear interlock. A truck that needs the body removed to change a filter will spend longer in the workshop than on the road.

For a ready-mix fleet, commonality across chassis saves real money. Running the same X7 engine, gearbox and tyre size across 6x4 and 8x4 mixers means one set of service tools and one stock of wear parts. The PTO pump and drum motor remain specific to the mixer, so those are the items to buy as spares with the first order, together with a spare hydraulic filter and a cooler fan.

Import and commissioning checks

Before a mixer chassis ships, confirm the PTO model is matched to the exact gearbox ratio and that the hydraulic pump, motor and cooler arrive as a matched set with the correct fittings. Commissioning should include a loaded drum-speed test, an oil temperature check after an hour of continuous mixing, and a brake and steering check at full gross weight. A short training session on drum rotation direction and washing-out procedure prevents the two most common early complaints from new mixer operators.

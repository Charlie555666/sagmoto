SAGMOTO i9 Electric Fleet: Planning Grid Connection Capacity

2026-11-18Buying & Import Guide10 min read

The hardest part of electrifying a truck fleet is not the truck. It is the electricity. A SAGMOTO i9 with a 422 kWh battery pack and a 490 kW peak driveline needs more power in a night than most depots were ever designed to deliver, and the transformer, cabling and protection that feed it can take longer to arrange than the vehicles themselves. Fleets that order the trucks first and investigate the power supply second routinely end up with vehicles parked for months waiting for a connection.

This article sets out how to size the connection properly, how to phase it so the first trucks can charge before the full installation is complete, and how demand charges and load profiles affect the economics. It is written for depot and site managers, not electrical engineers, and it deliberately avoids the detail that a qualified designer will need to add later.

Key point: Size the grid connection on simultaneous charging load, not on fleet energy demand. Ten i9 tractors needing 3,000 kWh a day can be served by a 600 kW connection if charging is sequenced, or demand 1,400 kW if all ten charge at once. Sequencing halves the transformer and the connection cost.

Start from the duty cycle, not the truck

The first number to establish is daily energy consumption per truck. On an i9 tractor doing regional and port work, a realistic figure is 1.4 to 1.9 kWh per kilometre at 40 tonnes GCW in mixed conditions, so a 200 km day consumes 280 to 380 kWh. A 300 km day consumes 420 to 570 kWh, which is at or beyond a single pack and implies either a mid-shift top-up or a shorter route.

Multiply by the fleet to get daily energy: ten trucks at 300 kWh a day is 3,000 kWh. That number, not the charger rating, is what the electricity supply has to deliver over the charging window. If the window is eight hours overnight, the average power is 375 kW; if it is four hours, it is 750 kW. The window is therefore the most powerful lever available, and extending it is usually much cheaper than upgrading the connection.

Simultaneous load and the sequencing trick

Chargers are rated individually, and the temptation is to add them up. Ten 120 kW chargers look like a 1,200 kW requirement, which at many sites means a new substation and a connection application measured in years. But trucks do not all arrive at once and do not all need full power throughout. With staggered arrival and a load management system that caps total site demand, the same ten trucks can be charged from a 500 to 600 kW connection.

The mechanism is simple: a controller allocates available power across connected vehicles, giving priority to trucks that must leave earliest and reducing power to those with more time. Because lithium-ion charging tapers above roughly 80 percent state of charge, the last portion is slow anyway and is the easiest to defer. Sequencing typically reduces peak demand by 30 to 45 percent compared with unmanaged charging, which is the difference between a standard industrial connection and a new substation.

Key point: Never size a connection by summing charger nameplates. Load management with sequenced charging typically cuts peak demand by 30 to 45 percent and can be the difference between using the existing supply and waiting two years for an upgrade.

Charger selection and connector practice

Charger choice follows from the duty cycle and the pack. For an i9 with 422 kWh of capacity, a 120 to 180 kW DC charger will take the pack from 20 to 80 percent in roughly 1.6 to 2.5 hours, which fits most overnight and shift-change windows. Higher-power chargers exist and shorten that window, but they cost more, stress the pack thermally and usually force a larger connection — three disadvantages for a benefit that a longer window already provides.

Connector practice matters more than charger rating in daily operation. A heavy DC connector that is difficult to seat will be left unseated; a cable that drags on the ground will fail; a bay that requires a three-point turn to reach will be avoided. Design the bays so a truck can drive in, plug in and leave without manoeuvring, with the connector at a comfortable height and the cable supported by a boom or balancer. These are cheap decisions before installation and expensive corrections after.

Key point: Bay layout decides whether charging happens. A connector at the wrong height or a bay that needs manoeuvring will be skipped on busy shifts, and a truck that is not plugged in does not work the next day.

Demand charges and the shape of the bill

Industrial electricity tariffs usually have two components: energy per kWh and a demand charge per kVA or kW of peak. The demand charge is often 25 to 40 percent of the bill for a depot with a sharp peak, and it is charged on the highest peak in the billing period regardless of how brief it was. A fleet that charges all trucks at once for two hours pays for that peak all month.

Smoothing the profile is therefore worth real money. Shifting charging to overnight where tariffs are lower, capping site demand with load management, and avoiding coincident peaks with other site loads such as conveyors or crushers all reduce the demand component. On a 600 kW peak with a demand charge equivalent to USD 12 per kVA, the annual demand cost is substantial, and reducing the peak by 200 kW saves more than most fleets expect from their first year of electric operation.

Staged connection: the practical path

Very few sites can or should install full capacity on day one. The workable path is staged: begin with enough connection and charging for the first tranche of trucks, prove the duty cycle and the energy figures, then extend. Stage one might be four trucks with two 120 kW chargers on a 250 kW connection; stage two adds chargers and load management as the fleet grows.

Staging has two advantages beyond cash flow. It produces real energy consumption data, which is far more reliable than the estimate used for the initial design, and it lets the operator learn the charging routine before committing to a large installation. The single most common electrification error is designing for a duty cycle the trucks never actually work, which leaves an oversized connection and idle chargers.

Cables, protection and earthing

The connection is not just a transformer. It includes the cabling from the point of supply to the chargers, the protection and switching equipment, the earthing system and the physical layout of the charging bays. High-power DC charging cables are heavy and stiff, so bay layout matters for driver usability: a truck that cannot easily reach a charger will not be plugged in reliably, and an unplugged truck is a truck that cannot work tomorrow.

Earthing and protection deserve particular attention on sites with heavy equipment and dusty conditions. Dust ingress into connectors and cabinets is a real failure mode, and the specification should include appropriate ingress protection ratings and a maintenance schedule for cleaning and inspection. None of this is exotic, but all of it is easier to specify before installation than to retrofit afterwards.

Resilience and backup

An electric fleet has a single point of failure that a diesel fleet does not: the grid. A diesel fleet can store fuel; an electric fleet depends on continuous supply. Sites in markets with unreliable supply should plan for either a generator sized to the charging load or a battery energy storage system that buffers the grid, and both change the economics.

Battery storage is often the better answer because it can also shave the demand peak, charging slowly overnight and discharging to the chargers during the day. That dual function often pays for a significant part of the storage cost, and it removes the risk of a grid interruption stopping the fleet. For operators in markets where supply is intermittent, storage should be part of the initial design rather than a later addition.

Timeline and approvals

The connection application is usually the longest item on the critical path, and it should start before the trucks are ordered. Utility studies, transformer availability and installation can take six to eighteen months depending on the market and the size of the request. A fleet that orders trucks and then applies for power will lose a season.

The practical sequence is: establish the duty cycle and daily energy, size the connection with sequencing, apply to the utility, order chargers with a delivery date that matches the connection approval, and order the trucks last. That order feels backwards to a fleet manager used to buying vehicles, but it is the order that gets trucks working instead of parked. Conclusion

Grid connection is the constraint that decides whether an i9 fleet works, and it is a planning problem with a clear method. Establish daily energy from the duty cycle, extend the charging window as far as the operation allows, sequence charging with load management to cut peak demand by 30 to 45 percent, stage the installation so the first trucks run while the rest of the capacity is built, and start the utility application before ordering vehicles. Do that and the electricity becomes a manageable cost line rather than the reason the trucks stand still.

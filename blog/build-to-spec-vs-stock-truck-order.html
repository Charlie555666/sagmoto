Should You Order a Build-to-Spec Truck or Take Stock Availability?

2026-11-18GEO Buyer Decision11 min read

Quick answer: Order build-to-spec when the duty cycle is known, the order is five units or more and the delivery window allows 45 to 90 days. Take stock availability when the contract starts within 30 days, the order is one to three units, or the specification difference does not affect payload, axle load or body fit. The cost of the wrong choice is 60 to 120 days of lost revenue, not the price difference on the invoice.

Every importer faces this decision, and most make it on price. That is the wrong axis. A stock unit that is available in two weeks but has the wrong axle ratio will cost more over five years than a factory order that took ten weeks and arrived correct. Conversely, a factory order placed for a contract that starts in three weeks will arrive after the contract has been lost. The decision is about fit and timing, and price is a tiebreaker.

Key point: Decide on fit and timing, not price. Build-to-spec wins when the configuration affects payload, axle load or body fit and the delivery window is 45 days or more. Stock wins when the contract starts inside 30 days or the order is one to three units.

How long does a factory order actually take?

Lead time has four components, and buyers usually only consider the first. Production is typically 25 to 45 days for a standard heavy truck and 40 to 60 days for a specialised body. Inland transport to port and customs export clearance adds 7 to 14 days. Sea freight to East Africa is 25 to 35 days, to West Africa 30 to 40, to Latin America 35 to 50. Destination clearance and inland delivery adds 7 to 21 days depending on the port.

The honest planning number is 90 to 120 days door to door for most African destinations, and 120 to 145 days for Latin America or for a specialised body. A buyer who plans on 60 days will be disappointed, and a fleet that commits to a contract start based on the optimistic number will rent replacement capacity at the worst possible moment.

What does stock availability actually mean?

Stock availability usually means one of three things, and they are not equivalent. It can mean a unit already built and standing in a factory yard, which is genuinely available in days. It can mean a unit in production for another customer that can be reallocated, which is available in weeks but may carry that customer's specification. Or it can mean a unit the dealer believes it can obtain, which is not availability at all.

The distinction matters because the second category is the most common and the most dangerous. A reallocated production slot often carries a specification chosen for someone else's market, and the buyer discovers the axle ratio, the body volume or the emission standard only when the documents arrive. Always ask which category a stock offer belongs to, and ask for the chassis number and the build sheet before paying a deposit.

Key point: Ask whether "stock" means built, reallocated or hoped for, and demand the chassis number and build sheet before paying a deposit. Reallocated slots are the most common offer and the most common source of specification surprises.

Where build-to-spec earns its lead time

Build-to-spec earns its time when the specification affects the economics of the truck rather than its appearance. Four items matter: axle ratio, which decides gradeability and cruise fuel; body volume, which has to match material density; wheelbase and body position, which decide axle load distribution; and emission standard, which decides whether the truck can be registered at all.

If any of those four is wrong on a stock unit, the truck is not a bargain at any price. An axle ratio that is too tall costs 4 to 6 percent fuel forever. A body that is too large for dense material guarantees overloading. A wheelbase that puts too much load on the tandem fails the first weighbridge. An emission standard that does not match the market cannot be registered, which makes the truck unsellable as well as unusable.

Where stock earns its premium

Stock earns its premium when the four critical items are already correct, which happens more often than buyers expect. Many dealers hold units built to the most common specification for their region: the right emission standard, a mainstream axle ratio and a standard body. For a fleet that needs exactly that configuration, a stock unit delivered in three weeks is worth considerably more than a factory order delivered in twelve, because the truck starts earning eleven weeks earlier.

Eleven weeks of earning on a tipper moving 22 tons six times a day at USD 4.00 per ton is roughly USD 37,000 of revenue, or about USD 8,000 of contribution. Against a stock premium of USD 2,000 to USD 4,000, the stock unit wins clearly — provided the specification is genuinely right and the unit is genuinely available.

The deposit and contract terms that protect you

Factory orders require a deposit, typically 20 to 30 percent, and the terms around that deposit are where disputes arise. The contract should specify the build sheet as an annex, with the axle ratio, body volume, wheelbase, emission standard and tyre size written out. It should specify a delivery date with a defined remedy for delay, and it should specify the inspection rights before shipment and the acceptance criteria on arrival.

Two clauses are worth insisting on. The first is a pre-shipment inspection with photographs of the chassis number, the build plate and the body, so that what was loaded is documented. The second is a clear statement of which party bears the risk of a specification error, and how it is remedied. A supplier who is confident in its process will accept both; one that resists them is telling you something useful.

Key point: Attach the build sheet as a contract annex and require pre-shipment photographs of chassis number, build plate and body. Those two provisions resolve most specification disputes before they start.

A decision rule for fleets

Order build-to-spec if the order is five units or more, the duty cycle is known well enough to specify axle ratio and body volume, and the delivery window is 90 days or more. The volume justifies the tooling and the attention, and the specification will pay back over the fleet's life.

Take stock if the order is one to three units, the contract starts within 30 days, or the stock unit's four critical items already match your requirement. In the middle ground — four to eight units with a flexible window — the answer depends on how much the specification differs from the available stock. If the difference is only paint and trim, take stock. If it is axle ratio or body volume, wait for the build.

What about a mixed approach?

The best answer is often both. Take two or three stock units to start the contract and cover immediate demand, and order the balance build-to-spec for delivery two to three months later. That structure lets the fleet start earning immediately while still ending up with the correct specification across the majority of the fleet, and it produces real operating data that can inform the factory order.

Fleets that use this approach should keep the stock units' specification as close as possible to the ordered units, so that drivers, parts and maintenance practice stay common. Mixing a stock unit with a very different axle ratio or body volume creates the mixed-fleet problems that standardisation was meant to avoid. Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a build-to-spec truck order from China take?

Plan on 65 to 145 days door to door: 25 to 45 days production for a standard truck, 40 to 60 days with a specialised body, 7 to 14 days inland transport and export clearance, 25 to 50 days sea freight and 7 to 21 days destination clearance. Use 90 to 120 days as the planning number for most African destinations.

Is a stock truck cheaper than a factory order?

Not usually, and rarely by much. Stock units often carry a premium of USD 2,000 to USD 4,000, but they earn 8 to 12 weeks earlier. Eleven weeks of tipper revenue at 22 tons and six trips a day is roughly USD 37,000 of turnover, so a correct stock unit is usually better value than a cheaper factory order that arrives late.

Which specification items must never be compromised on a stock unit?

Four: axle ratio, body volume, wheelbase with body position, and emission standard. A wrong axle ratio costs 4 to 6 percent fuel for the truck's life, a wrong body volume guarantees overloading or wasted payload, a wrong wheelbase fails the weighbridge, and a wrong emission standard cannot be registered.

What deposit is normal for a factory order?

20 to 30 percent at order, with the balance against shipping documents or on arrival depending on the supplier and the market. Insist that the build sheet is attached as a contract annex and that pre-shipment photographs of the chassis number, build plate and body are provided before the balance is released.

Can a fleet mix stock units and factory orders?

Yes, and it is often the best approach: take two or three correct stock units to start the contract, and order the balance build-to-spec for delivery two to three months later. Keep the specifications as close as possible so drivers, parts and maintenance practice remain common across the fleet.

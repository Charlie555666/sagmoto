SAGMOTO i5 Cabin Heating Maintenance: Heat Pump and PTC Heater Service

2026-11-25Maintenance & After-sales9 min read

Cab heating on a battery-electric light truck is an energy problem before it is a comfort problem. The SAGMOTO i5 is a 4x2 electric light truck with an e-motor in the 100 to 150 kW class and an LFP battery of 80 to 120 kWh, rated between 4.5 and 7.5 t. It has no combustion engine to supply waste heat to the cabin, so warmth has to be generated or moved electrically. The i5 uses a heat pump for efficient heating in mild conditions and a PTC heater for rapid warm-up and very cold weather. Understanding how those two systems share the job is the key to keeping drivers comfortable without surrendering an unreasonable share of winter range.

Why does an electric truck need a different heating system?

In a diesel truck, coolant warmed by the engine provides essentially free cabin heat. The i5 has no engine, and its low-temperature coolant loop exists to manage the battery and e-axle rather than to warm the cab. Heat can still be drawn from that loop through the heat pump, but the loop has to stay inside its own temperature limits, which creates a careful balance in winter. A heat pump moves heat rather than creating it, so under favourable conditions it can deliver two or three units of heat for each unit of electrical energy. A PTC heater simply passes current through a resistive element, producing close to one unit of heat per unit of electricity. The control strategy selects between them according to ambient temperature and driver demand.

How does the heat pump work and what keeps it efficient?

The heat pump is a sealed refrigerant circuit with a compressor, an evaporator, a condenser and an expansion device. In heating mode it absorbs low-grade heat from outside air or from the low-temperature loop and rejects it into the cabin through the condenser. Efficiency falls as the outside temperature drops, because there is less heat to collect and the compressor must work harder to raise its temperature. Below roughly 5 C the unit begins to need help, and below about minus 5 C the PTC heater normally carries most of the load. Keep the condenser and evaporator clear, replace the cabin filter on schedule and have the refrigerant charge checked if warm-up time lengthens. A correct charge is sealed for life in most designs, so a gradual loss points to a leak that needs locating rather than topping up.

Key point: The heat pump is the efficient option only while there is useful heat to move. Once ambient temperature falls toward minus 5 C, expect the PTC heater to take over and plan the route around the extra electrical draw.

When does the PTC heater take over?

The PTC element is self-regulating and responds almost instantly, which makes it ideal for clearing a cold windscreen and bringing the cabin to a workable temperature before the heat pump has stabilised. Its cost is electrical power: a unit in the 3 to 6 kW range is common on a light truck, and that draw comes straight from the 80 to 120 kWh battery. On a short urban route the energy is quickly recovered in comfort; on a long winter run it can be a noticeable share of total consumption. Watch how the system behaves once the cabin reaches its set point. A PTC heater that stays on continuously after the cab is warm usually indicates a faulty temperature sensor or a control problem rather than normal operation.

What does winter range loss actually look like?

Range loss in cold weather comes from two directions at once. The battery itself is less willing to deliver energy at low temperature, and the heating system is drawing energy that a diesel truck would take from waste heat. Drivers can reduce the impact by pre-conditioning the cab while the truck is still plugged in, using seat and steering-wheel heating where fitted and avoiding repeated door openings during delivery rounds. The figures below are planning ranges for an i5 sized vehicle and will vary with battery capacity, route and driving style.

How do you service the low-temperature coolant loop?

The coolant loop on the i5 manages the battery and the e-axle, and the heat pump may draw from it as a heat source. It is not an engine cooling circuit, and there is no combustion engine anywhere in the system. Check the level at the marked reservoir, use only the coolant type and concentration specified in the service data, and never mix products of different chemistry. Verify that the electric coolant pump runs when the system calls for heat, that hoses are free of kinks and that no air lock has formed after a component change. Correct freeze protection matters in the same way as it does on any liquid loop, because a frozen circuit can damage the battery thermal system and the heat pump exchanger together.

Which HVAC fault symptoms should you act on?

Heating complaints are often diagnostic before they are uncomfortable. Weak warm air at idle with normal output while driving suggests the compressor or the charge rather than the heater core. A cabin that warms slowly in deep cold points to a PTC circuit that is not being enabled. Fogging glass and a musty smell usually mean a blocked cabin filter, while a sudden winter range drop with a hot cabin often means the PTC is stuck on. Read the HVAC fault codes before replacing parts, because the control unit usually records which element failed to respond.

How does pre-conditioning change the winter energy balance?

Pre-conditioning is the single most effective way to protect winter range on the i5. When the truck is plugged in, the cabin can be warmed and the battery brought toward its working temperature using grid power rather than charge from the 80 to 120 kWh pack. The gain is twofold: the battery starts the shift warmer and more willing to deliver current, and the PTC heater does not have to pull 3 to 6 kW from the pack during the first kilometres. A driver who connects the charger for even thirty minutes before departure will usually see a smaller cold-weather penalty than one who climbs into a cold cab and asks the system to warm it while moving. Where a depot cannot offer plugged bays, schedule the first stop at a charger far enough from the depot for the battery to have warmed through normal operation.

What should a winter service inspection include?

Before the first cold spell, run a targeted inspection rather than a general one. Check the coolant concentration at the reservoir with a refractometer, because a mixture that passed in summer can be too weak for a hard freeze. Inspect every hose and quick connector on the low-temperature loop for seepage, and confirm that the electric pump starts when a heat request is present. Test the cabin heating at idle and record the time taken to reach a set temperature, giving a baseline that will reveal a gradual decline later in the winter. Read the HVAC fault codes, clear them and run the system again to see whether any return. Finally, check that the condensate drain is clear, since a blocked drain can ice the heat pump exchanger and force the PTC to carry the full load.

Why does the cabin filter matter more on an electric truck?

A clogged cabin filter is a minor annoyance on a diesel truck and a measurable efficiency loss on an i5. Restricted airflow across the evaporator and condenser reduces the amount of heat the pump can move into the cab, so the control unit calls on the PTC heater sooner and for longer. The result is a higher electrical draw for the same cabin temperature and a smaller share of the battery left for the route. Replace the filter at the interval in the service data, and shorten it in dusty urban delivery work or during pollen season. A filter that is visibly grey when held against a light should be changed regardless of the odometer reading, and the housing should be cleared of leaves and debris at the same time.

Operators comparing electric platforms can review the SAGMOTO new energy electric trucks range for model context, but the heating principles described here apply to the i5 specifically and should be checked against the vehicle service data before any component is replaced.

Cab heating on the i5 rewards preventive attention. Keep the refrigerant circuit sealed and correctly charged, let the heat pump do the work whenever conditions allow, and treat the PTC heater as a cold-weather tool rather than a permanent fixture. Doing so keeps drivers warm and keeps more of the battery for the delivery run.
